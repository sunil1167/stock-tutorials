    01Building blocks · ~1:42–15:08

    How daily candles hint at higher-timeframe zones

    Before the EMA chapter, the instructor demonstrates a useful observational skill: from the sequence of daily candles, anticipate how a larger weekly or monthly candle and a demand-zone pattern might look.

    Reconstruct the three-part pattern

    Leg-in: price falls→Base: price consolidates→Leg-out: price rallies

    The illustrated teaching case is a possible higher-timeframe drop–base–rally (DBR) demand zone. A sizable fall on the daily chart suggests a higher-timeframe leg-in; several days moving inside one limited range suggest a base; then a substantial rise suggests a leg-out. The point is to form a hypothesis about a higher-timeframe zone, not to assume its exact OHLC values from a loose visual guess.

    • A trading month has roughly 20–22 trading days in the tutor’s illustration, so daily-candle sequences may combine into a monthly candle.
    • For a weekly candle, he frequently reasons in approximate groups of five daily trading candles; holiday weeks or other irregularities can differ.
    • At minimum, the zone concept needs a leg-in, a base, and a leg-out; there may be more than one leg candle.
    • He checks the proposed structure on the actual higher timeframe rather than treating inference as verification.
    Worked lesson reasoning: why a daily pattern can be a monthly demand zone
    1. Spot a sustained daily decline. That suggests selling pressure which may appear as a monthly red leg-in candle.
    2. Locate a period of daily compression or a narrow trading range. It may contribute to a monthly base candle.
    3. Identify a meaningful daily rally out of that range. It can form a higher-timeframe leg-out.
    4. Switch to the monthly chart, inspect the true candles, and mark the valid zone only if it meets the previously learned demand/supply rules.
    5. Check higher-timeframe location, intermediate-timeframe trend and lower-timeframe execution before considering a trade.

    Important: the tutor stresses that visually anticipating a weekly/monthly zone is an intellectual exercise; actually opening the weekly or monthly chart is simpler and more reliable.

    Quality of the location is not the whole story

    The opening case references a 7-out-of-7 evaluation, a strong closing location, and a zone that coincides with the desired execution area. The instructor then acknowledges a problem: the weekly trend may be sideways or down, despite a strong-looking daily execution. He does not tell students to ignore this conflict. Rather, the lesson evaluates whether other permitted strengths offset it while continuing to respect the underlying rules.

    Class score extensions: The tutor says useful factors taught after the base seven-point score—such as a golden/death crossover, EMA 20 support, or sector support—can contribute one additional point per factor in his trade assessment. This is his teaching framework, not a validated universal scoring system.
    02EMA mechanics · ~15:23–43:49

    EMA 20: dynamic support and resistance

    The tutor distinguishes the 20-period exponential moving average (EMA 20) from the 50-period moving average used for a separate purpose. EMA gives more influence to recent prices, so it tracks price relatively closely.

    Why he pays attention to period 20

    The lecture reports that the tutor tested other EMA periods, including 6, 12, 21 and 26, and found 20 especially useful in his chart study. He also attributes part of the behavior to other market participants and automated traders watching this level. Treat those as the tutor’s explanation and observations, not as proof that the EMA must work in every market.

    He displays EMA 20 prominently, while keeping another moving-average line light in the background to retain moving-average crossover context.

    Interactive chart 01 / 05

    EMA 20: resistance can become support

    Schematic
    Up candleDown candleEMA 20

    Tap the steps. Hypothetical candles demonstrate the logic; no actual stock or tutor chart is reproduced.

    EMA above the price

    In a declining market, an overhead EMA 20 may hinder a rally and act like dynamic resistance. Price can react downward when it approaches the line, even without a visible supply zone.

    EMA below the price

    In a rising market, an EMA 20 below price may help halt a pullback and act like dynamic support. Earlier resistance may later become support, but never as a certainty.

    Important limits

    • Not every touch works. Price can cross, break, retest, or ignore EMA 20 altogether.
    • Trending markets matter. He explicitly warns against using the EMA in sideways conditions as though it provides the same edge.
    • Repeated tests can weaken the reaction. He notes that after repeated interactions, the level may lose importance.
    • No independent entry. Even where EMA 20 visibly reacts, a clear entry, stop-loss and location may be absent.
    Why an EMA squeeze is an observation—not permission to buy an options breakout

    During compression, the EMA can creep closer to candles; the tutor discusses narrow bases and possible breakouts. Yet he explicitly warns against turning this into a standalone breakout-options tactic. Consolidation can continue longer than expected, and option positions may lose value even when an eventual move appears. His instruction is to keep the original top-down, zone-driven process intact.

    03The actual application · ~17:48–22:26; 39:41–46:15

    Put demand and supply first; EMA 20 second

    This is one of the lecture’s strongest repeated messages: find a valid higher-timeframe demand or supply zone and suitable execution first. EMA 20 confluence may improve the setup, but it does not create a trade by itself.

    At a demand zone

    1. Identify a demand zone from the higher-timeframe analysis already taught in the course.
    2. Check the corresponding intermediate trend and allowed execution timeframe.
    3. If a trending-timeframe EMA 20 lies near that demand area, treat it as an additional potential support.
    4. Keep the stop-loss based on the zone / execution plan; the tutor discusses placing it below the distal line for a long setup in an example.
    5. Do not assume that touching EMA 20 means a bounce will happen.

    At a supply zone

    The mirrored idea applies: when price rallies toward a legitimate supply zone, an overhead EMA 20 can be another possible source of resistance. This remains a supporting observation rather than a trigger to short without a complete setup.

    A surprisingly important scoring rule
    Two supporting EMAs do not mean two EMA points.In the lecture’s confluence example, a weekly and a monthly EMA 20 align around a zone. The tutor says that is extra comfort, but it is still one EMA-based score contribution, not two.
    Do not reverse the process: “I see EMA 20 support, therefore I should find a trade” is precisely the approach the tutor rejects. His order is research → location → execution → additional EMA alignment.
    Confluence does not override adverse context

    The instructor repeatedly contrasts attractive-looking execution against weak intermediate trend or a nearby opposing zone. A trade may become more convincing if a valid higher-timeframe EMA aligns, but an extra factor does not magically eliminate trend risk or make an aggressive entry acceptable. In the examples, he sometimes prefers a lower level, a more conservative position inside the execution zone, or simply observation.

    04Practical chart workflow · ~51:47–54:55; 1:25:16–1:27:38

    Mark EMA 20 levels from higher to lower timeframes

    When seeking helpful EMA 20 confluence, the tutor scans yearly → half-yearly → quarterly → monthly → weekly → daily, placing horizontal reference lines only where EMA 20 is part of a clearly trending chart.

    06Core of the lesson · ~1:30:34–1:44:53

    The advanced trend analysis rules

    The tutor describes trend transitions with breaches of valid supply/demand zones. In his framework, a complete reversal from one established directional trend to the other normally passes through a sideways classification first.

    Down → SidewaysOne relevant supply zone is breached with a valid close and the required ability to hold above the level.
    Sideways → UpA further relevant supply zone is breached. The full down-to-up reversal therefore uses two supply breaches.
    Up → DownMirror the logic with demand: first breach → sideways; second → down.
    Starting classificationWhat the price breaksInstructor’s resulting classification
    Established downtrendOne relevant supply zoneSideways
    Sideways after that supply breakOne more relevant supply zoneUptrend
    Established uptrendOne relevant demand zoneSideways
    Sideways after that demand breakOne more relevant demand zoneDowntrend
    Already sidewaysOne qualifying supply zoneUptrend
    Already sidewaysOne qualifying demand zoneDowntrend

    “Break” means more than touching a zone

    For a supply zone, the lecture specifically asks for price to get above the distal boundary and close there. A momentary wick or visit followed by a return below does not count as the confirmed breach he is describing. The corresponding interpretation is used for breaking demand downward.

    07The subtle cases · ~1:42–2:16

    New zones can form before the old second zone is reached

    Do not memorize two fixed horizontal lines and ignore what price builds next. The tutor spends significant time on cases where rallies and corrections create new valid supply or demand zones between the older reference levels.

    Why this changes the practical question

    Imagine a downtrend in which the first supply level is breached: by the tutor’s framework, the market becomes sideways. Before price reaches the old second supply zone, a fresh valid supply zone forms. When price closes above that new supply zone, it may satisfy the next breach required for an uptrend. Waiting for an obsolete level can cause a late classification.

    Old downtrend→First supply broken→Fresh supply develops→Fresh supply broken: uptrend

    Adjacent bases are not always independent zones

    In a chart walkthrough the tutor explains that several neighboring base candles may belong to one combined zone, even when an alternative interpretation would separate them into small patterns. In other places he counts new continuation formations as valid zones. The meaning is structure-dependent: do not manufacture the second breach by counting every candle cluster as a new level.

    How to distinguish continuation zones and reversal zones in this discussion

    The tutor uses drop–base–drop (DBD) to illustrate supply formed during a falling market, and refers to both continuation and reversal formations during a rally. The key is not the label alone: identify valid leg-in, base and leg-out structure, mark the meaningful zone boundaries, and ask whether price truly invalidated distinct relevant supply/demand zones.

    He also shows why a conventional drawn trendline or simple higher-high/higher-low sketch may appear visually unchanged while additional continuation supply zones emerge. Those additional zones can change when the advanced rule judges a reversal complete.

    Why the tutor wants practice

    Some real charts are messy: corrections are very small, bases sit near each other, zones overlap, and one person’s two markings may be another person’s single broader zone. The instructor explicitly acknowledges that this part can be difficult, and advises continuing to compare it with SMA 50 when uncertain.

    Source boundary: The recovered passages do not establish one universal numerical definition for the minimum zone height, number of bars, or every ambiguous chart case. This notebook therefore does not add arbitrary thresholds.
    08The point of “advanced” · ~2:11–2:33

    Why this can identify a change before SMA 50

    The tutor’s argument is fundamentally about timing. A moving average summarizes previous prices, while zone breaches can be recognized as the structure develops.

    50-period SMA trend check

    Retains many prior bars in the calculation, so the line can still look bearish even after the beginning of an upward reversal. It is simple and remains a useful fallback.

    Supply/demand-based advanced analysis

    Uses relevant breaches and closes, including fresh zones created by corrections. The tutor presents it as an earlier classification technique, which can uncover setups before a conventional moving-average label changes.

    Why ordinary swing-high analysis may lag

    The lecture compares its two-zone method with conventional higher-high/higher-low price structure. A tiny correction can create a fresh continuation zone without producing the kind of prominent swing that the conventional drawing tracks. In such a case the zone framework can change classification earlier. The tutor also demonstrates cases where the two methods tell broadly the same story; he is not saying every chart gives a different answer.

    Keep the same timeframe hierarchy

    Near the conclusion, the tutor makes an explicit correction to a possible misunderstanding: you still check the trend on the intermediate timeframe according to the course’s multiple-timeframe rules. Advanced trend analysis changes the method of reading trend, not the overall location–trend–execution structure.

    Before versus after
    Old method: “Has SMA 50 visibly turned?”New complementary method: “How many distinct relevant zones have been closed beyond, and what trend state follows?”

    Entry, stop-loss, and target

    The class does not teach a single new universal entry trigger for EMA 20. In a long-demand example, the tutor locates the stop-loss below the distal boundary, consistent with his pre-existing execution method. He also discusses choosing between upper/lower levels and distinguishing aggressive from conservative placement when trend is marginal.

    Some examples discuss 2:1 and 3:1 reward-to-risk targets. These are illustrative ratios within existing plans, not a prediction that every EMA-aligned trade will earn them. As ever, no ratio overcomes invalid analysis, slippage or costs.

    Practical financial caution: The tutor’s anecdotal results and predictions about future accuracy or reward ratios are not independently verified here. Use paper-trading, journaling and controlled position sizing instead of relying on those claims.
    10One-page workflow

    Your repeatable chart-review checklist

    1. Choose a stock for research, not because EMA happens to be touched.
    2. Find major higher-timeframe demand and supply zones.
    3. Check whether the zone is properly formed, located and already tested.
    4. On the intermediate timeframe, classify trend using validated supply/demand breaches.
    5. Determine whether price is still in a directional trend or has shifted sideways.
    6. If assessing a reversal, identify two distinct relevant zones and any newly formed ones.
    7. Require the qualifying close beyond the appropriate distal boundary, not merely a wick.
    8. Find execution on the timeframe allowed by the existing course method.
    9. Mark entry, stop-loss and planned target using the established execution rules.
    10. Scan EMA 20 on relevant trending timeframes; ignore sideways signals.
    11. Use EMA confluence as an additional score component—not the reason the trade exists.
    12. Review the complete trade score, competing levels, and risks before any decision.

    • EMA 20: a supporting reference, primarily in trending markets.
    • Demand/supply: the primary trade structure.

    1 of 10

    Lesson in 60 seconds

    Start from valid demand/supply. Use EMA 20 on trending timeframes as one extra confluence factor. Record why the trade is good before considering EMA; skip EMA-only trades in choppy ranges. For trend classification, a downtrend breaks one supply to become sideways, then another to become up. Reverse the logic using demand zones for up-to-down moves. A sideways market needs one relevant new breach to turn directional. Look for fresh zones, require convincing closes beyond distal boundaries, and apply the classification on the intermediate timeframe.
