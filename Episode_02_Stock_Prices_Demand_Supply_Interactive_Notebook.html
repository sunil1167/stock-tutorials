Getting oriented

The question underneath every chart: why do prices move at all?

The tutor frames this second lesson as the foundation for later entries, stops, targets and zone-marking. He asks students to make their own notes and to avoid treating the market as a stream of tips. The earlier homework included removing distracting trading apps and squaring off existing positions in the course context.

The teaching journey is deliberate: open and close → buyers and sellers → imbalance → candle shape → base and departure → demand/supply area → possible return to that area → probability, not certainty. Each link matters.

Your objective

  • Class 2 is about the reason behind movements, not a complete mechanical trade setup.
  • The tutor emphasizes notes, patience, and avoiding unplanned trades.
  • Some stock examples are old historical classroom illustrations, not current buy recommendations.

CHAPTER 02SOURCE · 05:50–06:55; 08:50–09:20

Open, close, high and low

A candle is a record of the price path within one chosen interval.

The open is the interval’s starting transaction price; the close is its last. A green candle closes above its open; a red candle closes below. The upper and lower wicks show how far price visited outside the open–close body.

The tutor begins with a straightforward question: if a green body is simply a positive close-minus-open difference, what caused the difference? That is the lesson’s doorway into the interaction of buying interest and selling interest.

Two mathematical definitions
Body = |close − open|. Full range = high − low. These values refer to the same candle and the same timeframe.
Teaching-language caution
The tutor often says “more buyers than sellers” to describe stronger buying pressure. Every executed trade has both a buyer and a seller; the important distinction is the urgency and quantity willing to trade at each price, not a literal unmatched count of completed buyers versus sellers. This clarification is editorial, not a direct quotation.

CHAPTER 03SOURCE · 09:20–14:10

Why every buyer needs a seller

Demand alone does not complete a trade; a matching opposite order is required.

A person wanting to buy 1,000 or 2,000 shares cannot force an execution at a chosen price if insufficient shares are offered there. To buy immediately, the person may have to accept the next available selling prices. The mirror image applies when a seller cannot find enough buyers at the desired price.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

The tutor’s 100,000-share illustration

Imagine a participant wants to buy 100,000 shares. If 100,000 shares are offered at the acceptable price, the order can be satisfied there; the tutor says there is no remaining imbalance from that particular request. If only 50,000 shares are available there, 50,000 of that requested quantity remain unfilled at that price unless the order changes or finds liquidity elsewhere.

To fill the balance immediately, the buyer may have to pay higher prices. The same reasoning in reverse explains selling into lower bids. The numbers are explicitly invented by the tutor for explanation, not measured exchange data.

  • “Every buyer needs a seller; every seller needs a buyer.”
  • Large requested quantities do not guarantee execution at the quoted price.
  • An executed trade produces volume. Volume alone does not identify outstanding orders.
  • Price discovery happens when participants accept or reject the quantities available at successive prices.

CHAPTER 04SOURCE · 06:35–20:50

What actually pushes price up or down

The tutor uses demand–supply imbalance as the central explanation.

In the tutor’s framework, buying pressure stronger than available selling is associated with upward movement, and selling pressure stronger than available buying with downward movement. His market analogy is ordinary shopping: when a scarce item is eagerly demanded, prospective buyers offer more; when sellers compete to exit, they may accept less.

The distinction is between an intention to transact and a completed transaction. The tutor’s examples of 50,000, 100,000 or 150,000 are illustrative quantities, chosen to make unmet interest easy to picture; they are not a formula for estimating an actual chart candle’s orders.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

Two demand/supply cases

Case A: intended buying = 100,000, available selling = 50,000 → 50,000 units of buying interest remain unfilled at that price. Case B: intended buying = 150,000, available selling = 50,000 → 100,000 remain. The second case shows a larger hypothetical mismatch.

Reverse the relative sizes for stronger selling interest. A green/red candle is the result of price movement over an interval; one cannot directly read the exact buyer/seller quantities from its body.

The tutor’s main line
A large directional candle is treated as visible evidence that one side won the recent buying–selling contest. It is a clue, not a direct order-book report.

CHAPTER 05SOURCE · 20:50–27:35

Exciting candles: strong directional departure

The tutor names and formally defines the first major candle category.

An exciting candle has a relatively large body compared with its full high–low range. This is his visual proxy for strong imbalance and directional conviction. It can be green or red.

Exact rule stated in class
Exciting candle: |close − open| is greater than 50% of (high − low). A strict “greater than” means precisely 50% does not meet the stated definition.
WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

Practice the calculation

Consider an entirely illustrative candle with high 110, low 90, open 93 and close 106. The range is 20, and the body is 13; 13 ÷ 20 = 65%, so the candle qualifies as exciting under the tutor’s threshold. These OHLC numbers are added only to let you practice the explicitly taught ratio.

  • Large bullish exciting candle: buyers moved the close substantially above open.
  • Large bearish exciting candle: sellers moved the close substantially below open.
  • Compare body to the whole range, not just to one wick.
  • The size of a body is a visual clue; do not turn it into a guarantee about the next candle.

CHAPTER 06SOURCE · 27:35–39:00

Base candles: the fight is still unresolved

A small body with a larger combined wick is the opposite picture.

The tutor defines a base candle as having an open–close body smaller than half its total high–low range. He describes it as a contest where buyers push price up, sellers push it back, buyers return, and neither side wins decisively during that interval.

Exact rule stated in class
Base candle: |close − open| is less than 50% of (high − low). It may be green or red. An exactly 50% ratio is neither category under the lesson’s strict inequalities; the lecture discusses visual ambiguity and context.

A base candle can show tall upper and lower wicks even with a tiny final body: price travelled during the interval, but much of the move was reversed. The tutor also mentions doji-like cases with a very small body. Green does not automatically mean a strong base; red does not automatically mean strong sellers.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

How the wick “fight” unfolds

An interval might start around 100, travel above that level as buying appears, drop as sellers gain control, then recover again near its starting price. The final body can be tiny while the full range is wide. The tutor is making the point about an indecisive outcome, not about a pre-set path all candles must follow.

  • Price can make both a high and a low without a large open–close displacement.
  • The base is the area to watch for a later resolved imbalance.
  • A small body alone does not establish that a future bounce will happen.

CHAPTER 07SOURCE · 39:00–49:00

Borderline candles need context

The tutor does not want students trapped by a difficult-to-classify body.

When the body seems close to half the full range, or when differently shaped wicks make the candle visually confusing, the tutor asks students to inspect the preceding and following price action. The important diagnostic is whether a significant directional move actually follows the apparent area of hesitation.

He explicitly warns that a borderline candle should not be isolated from the structure around it. A long succession of small candles may still be an unresolved struggle; a clear departure afterward supplies the more meaningful context.

Decision discipline
Measure first. If the proportion is near the boundary, do not force a confident classification. Look at the candles before and after; the tutor treats the subsequent move as a critical clue.

CHAPTER 08SOURCE · 49:00–57:50

Base → departure: the turning point

The battle inside the base is followed by a visible result.

Several green and red base candles may appear while buying and selling remain roughly balanced. Eventually, a large green exciting candle may leave the region. In the tutor’s interpretation, that is the buyers winning the struggle. A large red departure means sellers gained control.

This is the point where his pending-order hypothesis begins: if the strong move left the base area, he reasons that some of the stronger side’s interest might not have been completely filled there. A future return to that price area can therefore matter.

Terminology
Base = temporary indecision. Departure = strong directional candle(s) leaving the area. Potential demand = a base area departed upward. Potential supply = a base area departed downward.
  • First identify a plausible base; then determine whether the subsequent move resolves upward or downward.
  • Do not buy solely because a base candle exists—at that instant there is no clear result yet.
  • This lesson explains the logic; exact zone boundaries are explicitly deferred to the next lesson.

CHAPTER 09SOURCE · 52:00–59:00

Demand areas and a return from above

A strong upward departure turns attention to the earlier base.

The tutor calls the area associated with a base and an upward departure a demand area in this framework. The chart moves away, possibly far above it. If price later declines into that earlier area, previously unfilled buying interest may meet the incoming selling pressure.

If that buying interest is still meaningful compared with current sellers, the area can produce a bullish reaction. The tutor compares this to a buyer who wanted more at a particular price but received only part of the desired quantity.

Crucial qualifier
The tutor explicitly says a demand area will not always hold. A bounce depends on how the alleged remaining interest compares with new selling when price revisits it. Do not read the drawn zone as a guaranteed support level.
  • The green departure is the evidence the teacher wants to see after the base.
  • A return can take days, months or years; there is no promised deadline.
  • The precise entry and invalidation boundary are not specified in this lesson.

CHAPTER 10SOURCE · 57:40–1:00:00

Supply areas and a return from below

Reverse the reasoning for sellers.

After a base and a strong downward departure, the tutor interprets the previous area as possible supply: sellers may not have fully transacted at the price levels they wanted. If price later rallies back into that area, those sellers may reappear, creating a downward reaction.

But a supply zone is not a wall. More urgent buyers can absorb the offers and push price through. The zone is therefore an observation of earlier movement plus a hypothesis about future interaction, never an ironclad ceiling.

Symmetry
A base plus upward departure suggests potential demand. A base plus downward departure suggests potential supply. The same logic and same uncertainty apply on both sides.

CHAPTER 11SOURCE · 59:00–1:10:00

Why zones work sometimes—and fail sometimes

This is a probability lesson, not a promise.

The tutor repeatedly addresses the most natural question: “Will price always bounce when it returns?” His answer is no. It depends on the quantity and urgency of fresh opposing orders relative to whatever interest is still present around the old area.

He uses examples with progressively larger hypothetical pending-buy quantities—such as tens of lakhs and crores—to show why a strong area could react earlier or resist a larger selling wave. He explicitly warns that these are made-up figures and that no calculation in the lecture reveals the actual pending quantity.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

A hypothetical retest

Suppose an old base still has strong interest to buy. A relatively small wave of selling may be absorbed and price may turn up. A more aggressive wave may drive price deeper into the same area. An even larger wave may overcome it entirely. The teacher’s point is to compare pressure against pressure—not to forecast an exact bounce price from arbitrary numerical figures.

  • An apparent zone is a conditional idea.
  • A “better looking” zone can still fail; market orders and intentions change.
  • The farther/deeper a visit into a zone, the more relevant the strength of new opposing pressure becomes.
  • No chart candle reveals a live count of hidden pending orders.

Judging relative zone strength

The size and energy of the departure matter in the tutor’s ranking.

The lesson compares a base followed by a mild, a strong, or a very strong directional move. In the tutor’s reasoning, the stronger the departure from the base, the stronger the initial evidence of imbalance, so the more promising the area may be on a later revisit.

He repeats the same idea at the end of the class for selling: an ordinary red departure, a stronger red departure, and very strong or consecutive red departures are progressively stronger evidence of selling pressure. This is a relative probability judgment, not a numerical success rate.

What is—and is not—measured
The lecture supplies a qualitative ranking (normal / strong / very strong). It does not supply calibrated percentages, a validated scoring formula, an exact remaining-order count, or a guaranteed return.
  • A large departure matters more than a weak nudge from a base.
  • A cluster of powerful directional candles may indicate an especially forceful departure.
  • A smaller-timeframe chart can reveal bases not visible in a large single candle; detailed timeframe rules come later.
  • More than one base candle can occur; how to draw the full zone is taught in the next lesson.

CHAPTER 13SOURCE · 1:09:40–1:15:30

No one can stop new buying or selling

Old information never locks the market into one future.

The tutor pauses to emphasize a simple statement: you cannot stop somebody from buying or selling. A person determined to acquire shares may accept progressively higher offers; a person determined to exit may accept lower bids. In either case, new intent can overwhelm an older area.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

Tata Motors and the price ladder

The tutor describes a hypothetical buyer wanting shares near ₹450, then deciding to pay ₹460 or ₹470 if offers at ₹450 are insufficient. Another participant may instead urgently want to sell, even accepting ₹440 or ₹430. These figures illustrate an order’s willingness to move across prices, not historical Tata Motors quotes.

A second example uses a possible buying range around ₹55–₹60 for J&K Bank. The participant may buy what is available and wait for another visit to acquire more; other independent traders may keep moving the market elsewhere. This is why he calls trading a game of probabilities.

The key implication
A prior base cannot compel later investors to behave the same way. Orders may change, fresh participants arrive, and a test can end in a reaction, penetration, or breach.

CHAPTER 14SOURCE · 1:15:30–1:21:30

Institutions, liquidity and misleading tips

Who can create a visibly forceful price imbalance?

The tutor contrasts individual traders with large participants, naming institutions such as LIC and mutual-fund asset managers. His point is that substantial orders are more likely to leave a visible effect in liquid, widely traded shares than a retail purchase of a few shares.

He also warns about heavily promoted small or penny stocks: a relatively small freely traded share supply can make manipulation easier. Unsolicited messages promising rapid doubles or repeated upper circuits should not be treated as reliable analysis.

He teaches that traders should try to identify the effects of large demand/supply activity from chart behavior, rather than imagine they can directly see an institution’s full private order book.

Important limitation
The tutor sometimes attributes historical zones to specific institutional unfilled orders. The actual identity, persistence, and quantity of such orders cannot be verified from the candles alone. Treat those accounts as his explanatory interpretation, not established order-book evidence.

CHAPTER 16SOURCE · 1:29:50–1:48:00

Expensive buying, panic selling and news

A behavioral detour explains why chasing can feel irresistible.

The tutor criticizes buying simply because a stock has risen sharply or a confident headline suggests it will rise further. He compares this with normal shopping: people seek discounts on phones, laptops and other purchases, yet may chase a higher price in stocks after hearing optimistic predictions.

An analogy about land near a rumored road, shopping development or airport explains how expectations can move a quoted price before the promised project has actually appeared. The lesson uses such examples to stress the difference between an expectation, a price movement, and a sound trade plan.

Behavioral checklist
Am I evaluating an identifiable area of earlier imbalance, or reacting to fear of missing out? Am I buying after a major run without a planned exit? Am I mistaking a rumor, promotional message or enthusiastic crowd for a trade setup?

Nifty and the 2020 selloff anecdote

A demonstration that even previously strong demand can be breached.

Discussing the 2020 lockdown decline, the tutor says two strong weekly Nifty demand zones were breached, which he regarded as evidence of unusually intense selling pressure. He recalls earlier levels near 8,200 and 7,800 as attractive areas in that historical market context.

He describes having reduced part of his positions on a technical observation rather than predicting a pandemic. He also says that an eventual deep drop toward demand should not automatically trigger indiscriminate panic selling. These are his historical claims, not audited returns or advice to trade those past levels now.

Learn the principle, not the old price
When strong demand areas fail, that is meaningful information: new selling can exceed old buying pressure. It does not prove what the next move must be, and 2020 price numbers are not current levels.

What can you responsibly do after this lesson?

Separate finding an area from placing a trade.

The tutor poses three indispensable questions before entering: Where is the entry? Where is the stop-loss if the view is wrong? Where is the profit-taking target if the view is right? If these are not defined, he rejects buying merely because candles are green or because a base has just appeared.

This episode is intentionally not a completed strategy. Exact zone drawing, entry location, stop-loss location, targets, and further strength filters are announced for the next class. Filling those gaps with guessed rupee offsets, risk–reward ratios or candle-by-candle signal rules would misrepresent this source.

Do not invent an entry rule
Use these notes to observe and annotate candle structure, not to place an unvalidated trade. A candidate area becomes actionable only after you have independently established execution, invalidation, target and risk limits.
  • Mark the possible base and its direction of departure as a study exercise.
  • Write down which evidence supports or weakens the hypothesis.
  • If an exact entry, stop, target, or risk size is unknown, write “not specified in Episode 2.”
  • Do not use a single older chart example as a promised forward test result.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

Can we know the exact pending order quantity?

No. The tutor says real-time remaining orders cannot be precisely counted from the candle/volume chart. He uses relative departure strength to infer which area might be stronger. Volume contains matched buying and selling, not a direct readout of unfilled orders.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

Can there be multiple base candles?

Yes. He says they can appear in a cluster. How many count and how to draw the correct full range will be covered in the next lesson.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

Does this apply beyond individual stocks?

He describes demand and supply as widely applicable, mentioning indexes, commodities, foreign exchange, international markets and crypto. This is a description of the broad concept, not validation of a trading edge in every instrument.

WORKED EXAMPLE · FROM THE LECTURE

What if price is at an all-time high or low?

The tutor says he will address those special circumstances later. This lesson does not supply a complete rule to invent here.

Central idea
