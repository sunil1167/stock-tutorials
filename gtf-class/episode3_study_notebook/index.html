01

What a demand or supply zone represents

Think in terms of the struggle between buyers and sellers

Tutor’s framework: ~10:00–20:00 and ~27:28–30:43

The class describes price action as a contest between buyers and sellers. An upward rally is evidence of buyers pushing price higher; a downward drop is evidence of sellers pushing price lower. Between these moves there can be a base, where the two sides compete before one side pushes price out decisively.

Demand-side outcome

Buyers take control

After a base, price leaves with an exciting green candle or upward move. The base is treated as the origin of a demand zone. You later look for these zones below the current market price.

Supply-side outcome

Sellers take control

After a base, price leaves with an exciting red candle or downward move. The base is treated as the origin of a supply zone. You later look for these zones above the current market price.

The tutor interprets strong moves away from a base as evidence of strong order-flow imbalance and discusses pending orders as the reason a zone may react in the future. Treat this as the tutor’s explanatory model: the candlestick pattern alone does not show actual exchange order-book quantities.

First mental modelBase = competition. Leg-out = the observed winner. The direction of the leg-out identifies whether you are reading a demand formation or a supply formation.
02

All four patterns — DBR, RBR, RBD, DBD

Learn to pronounce and classify the formations

~08:57–14:17, ~23:15–26:51

The labels describe the order of three components: the incoming direction, a base, and the outgoing direction. Drop is a bearish/red move; Rally is a bullish/green move; Base is a temporary pause/contest.

PatternFull nameZone typeFormation
DBRDrop–Base–RallyDemandReversal: down → up
RBRRally–Base–RallyDemandContinuation: up → up
RBDRally–Base–DropSupplyReversal: up → down
DBDDrop–Base–DropSupplyContinuation: down → down

Visual 1 · Compare the four formations

SCHEMATIC, NOT MARKET DATA

DBR: Sellers push price lower, a base develops, then buyers cause a sharp upward departure — a demand reversal pattern.

Demand: two possible beginnings

In both DBR and RBR, the leg-out must be upward/green. The incoming candle may be red (DBR) or green (RBR).

Do not label a zone “demand” just because there was once a green candle — find the complete leg-in → base → exciting green leg-out sequence.

Supply: two possible beginnings

In both RBD and DBD, the leg-out must be downward/red. The incoming candle may be green (RBD) or red (DBD).

The outgoing move, not the incoming move, determines the type of zone in this lesson.

03

Three indispensable components

Leg-in → base → leg-out; what makes a zone worth marking?

~14:24–20:38, ~2:06:59–2:07:35

The tutor introduces names for the three parts so that you can identify a pattern on any chart without being distracted by candle count or colour.

01 · Left of baseLeg-inMove approaching the base. Can be a rally or a drop.
02 · The pauseBaseThe candle(s) marking the contested area where the zone is drawn.
03 · Right of baseLeg-outMove departing the base. Exciting green for demand; exciting red for supply.

Visual 2 · Anatomy of one zone

DEMAND EXAMPLE
Leg-inBaseLeg-out

The base can be multiple candles; each side can also contain multiple leg candles. The labels refer to the phase, not a mandatory single candle.

Rules the tutor emphasizes

  • A complete zone requires at least one leg-in candle, one base candle and one leg-out candle. Missing any component means the formation is incomplete.
  • The leg-in and leg-out should be exciting (directional); the stronger or more explosive the leg-out, the stronger the tutor considers the zone.
  • Multiple leg-in candles, base candles and leg-out candles are possible. Do not force a one-candle-only interpretation.
  • In the class’s earlier candle terminology, an exciting candle has a body greater than about 50% of its total high-to-low range. But a candle can meet that basic label and still represent a relatively weak departure (19:59–20:38).
  • A very small supposed leg-in or leg-out that fits inside the base’s range may be better treated as part of the base — classification needs visual judgment (31:56–32:30).
Do not invent a candle-count filterThe tutor says the ideal maximum or optimum number of base candles and several refinement questions are for later lessons. This notebook does not add a “maximum 3 candles” or another unverified cutoff.
Why does a larger leg-out matter in the tutor’s framework?

A strong departure shows that one side won decisively after the base. The tutor associates a larger movement — and sometimes several strong consecutive departure candles — with a more powerful zone and more remaining order interest. He also explains that a weak leg-out can technically fit the shape but might not be attractive for trading.

The key practical point for this lesson: learn to mark even weaker examples for practice, while noticing which departures look substantially stronger.

04

Reversal vs continuation: strength has context

The tutor’s preference is conditional, not absolute

~26:09–31:49; ~2:10:24–2:10:38
Reversal formations

DBR and RBD

The incoming side initially controls price. In the base, the other side fights back and eventually changes the direction of price. The tutor sees this as an especially forceful change of control.

Continuation formations

RBR and DBD

The original dominant side wins again after the base. Price continues in its earlier direction. This is not automatically inferior: a strong trend can make continuation formations powerful.

When the chart does not show a particularly strong trend, the tutor gives a general preference to the reversal examples DBR (demand) and RBD (supply). However, he repeatedly cautions that continuation zones should not be ignored: in a strong uptrend or downtrend they can behave more aggressively, and broader chart context is needed.

Correct interpretation“Reversal is stronger” is not a universal rule. The tutor’s comparison depends on trend strength and context. In this episode, the goal remains recognizing and marking both families correctly.
05

Mark a demand zone — the exact formula

Look left and down, then draw the base’s body-to-wick boundaries

~33:59–56:29; ~2:11:03–2:13:19

Use the same basic identification procedure on stocks and indices and across the time frames covered in class. The tutor first demonstrates it on chart examples and later recaps the sequence.

  1. Draw CMP. Identify the current market price and mark it with a horizontal reference line.
  2. Look left and down. Search the already-formed historical candles below CMP for an explosive upward/green departure.
  3. Reconstruct the pattern. Find the leg-in → base → green leg-out. It may be DBR or RBR.
  4. Mark the base, not the entire rally. Draw two horizontal lines that enclose the selected base candles using the formula below.
  5. Check freshness. Only retain zones that meet this lesson’s fresh-zone rule (explained in Chapter 07).
Demand zone · body to wick

Proximal line

Highest body price among all selected base candles.

Take the highest open/close body edge, ignoring upper wicks.

Distal line

Lowest wick price among all selected base candles.

Take the absolute lowest low, including lower wicks.

Single-base versus multiple-base marking

If there is one base candle, its upper body boundary is the proximal line and its lower wick is the distal line. If there are several base candles, compare all their bodies for the highest body edge and all their lower wicks for the lowest wick. The two boundaries might belong to different candles.

In the teaching example around 49:20–50:25, the tutor temporarily treats three candles as a base, demonstrates how different candles can supply the two lines, and then shows how the marking changes if the base is instead interpreted as a single candle (50:45–51:17). First identify the base correctly, then apply the formula.

Why mark “body to wick” instead of the complete high-to-low range?

The tutor specifically introduces body-to-wick marking in this class (54:52–56:02). For demand the near/upper boundary is based on the highest body, and the far/lower boundary uses the lowest wick. He explains this by referring to where the strong rebound and pending-order interest may originate. He notes that wick-to-wick marking can be considered in some later cases, but does not replace today’s primary method with it.

06

Mark a supply zone — the mirrored formula

Look left and up, then take the base’s lower body and upper wick

~1:11:00–1:19:05; ~2:14:32–2:15:27

The chart-reading process mirrors demand, but you search above CMP for an exciting red departure. Do not simply reuse the demand formula: proximal and distal are reversed vertically around the base.

  1. Draw CMP at current market price.
  2. Look left and up through historical price action above CMP.
  3. Identify an explosive red/drop leg-out with a leg-in and base immediately before it: RBD or DBD.
  4. Mark the selected base candles with the formula below.
  5. Confirm the zone is fresh before keeping it as a current candidate.
Supply zone · body to wick

Proximal line

Lowest body price among all selected base candles.

Nearest/lower boundary from the selected body edges.

Distal line

Highest wick price among all selected base candles.

Farthest/upper boundary from the highest high.

Remember: for a green candle the lower body edge is open; for a red candle it is close. The exact tool shortcuts are incidental — the price comparison determines the line.

07

Fresh versus tested — the essential validation

A zone is not fresh forever; check what happened after its departure

~52:47–57:17; ~1:00:57–1:01:26; ~2:12:48–2:13:13

After marking the proximal and distal lines, scan price action after the first leg-out. The tutor checks whether price has subsequently returned to the proximal line and completed a reaction away from the zone.

Fresh

Not yet revisited

Price has not touched the proximal line after departing. The zone remains fresh under the rule used in this class.

Tested

Revisited and reacted

Price has returned to the proximal line/zone and then moved away. The tutor treats its opportunity as already played out and stops considering it for this lesson’s fresh-zone exercise.

Visual 5 · Compare fresh and tested price paths

DEMAND EXAMPLE

Fresh: after the first green leg-out, later candles do not reach the proximal line.

Critical nuance in the class (56:48–57:17)Touching a zone as price is currently arriving is not the same as a completed previous test and reaction. The tutor clarifies that students are waiting for price to reach an opportunity; his “tested” exclusion is about a revisit that has already happened and played out. He defers the detailed entry question to the next lesson.

Therefore, for historical chart marking, check each candidate’s post-formation candles. A zone can have been fresh on an earlier date and be tested now. Judge it from the date you are analyzing, not with hindsight hidden in the chart.

What if price stops just short of the proximal line?

A student asks about a near miss around 2:17:24–2:17:35. The tutor postpones the operational answer to the later lesson about entry and stop loss. This notebook does not invent a tolerance in rupees, paise, ticks or percent.

Should I keep using a zone after two or three tests?

Not according to this episode’s practice rule. The tutor specifically says to stop considering the zone once it is tested, rather than waiting for second and third reactions (54:37–54:43). Later methods might differ; they are not part of this source-backed lesson.

08

What the tutor does on actual charts

Translate the spoken demonstrations into repeatable chart-reading actions

~34:27–1:10:52; ~1:11:00–1:59:26; ~2:11:03–2:16:08

The transcript includes chart demonstrations rather than clean downloadable OHLC data. The notes below preserve the teacher’s method and conclusions supported by the searchable passages; the numerical drawings in this notebook are deliberately schematic, not reconstructions of the original chart.

Walkthrough A — Demand zone(s) on Marico (daily / weekly)
  1. Open the stock chart, begin on daily and later examine weekly. Place the CMP horizontal reference line.
  2. Look to the historical left and below for an exciting up move, then identify the leg-in, selected base candle(s), and green leg-out.
  3. Draw highest base-body edge as proximal and lowest base wick as distal, explicitly demonstrating both single-base and three-base interpretations.
  4. Check whether price touched the proximal line after the first leg-out. When no past retest occurred, label the zone fresh (Demand Zone 1, then Demand Zone 2 in successive examples).
  5. When examining weekly supply examples, he finds already-tested candidates and says there is no current fresh supply zone on that weekly chart at the moment shown.

Evidence clusters: 34:47–35:07; 49:20–51:17; 1:41:39–1:46:33; 1:49:50–1:50:44.

Walkthrough B — Supply zone examples
  1. Keep the CMP reference; this time look left and above it for a red exciting drop.
  2. Identify RBD or DBD, and distinguish the arrival, base and departure.
  3. With multiple base candles, choose the lowest body for proximal and highest wick for distal; compare exact open/close numbers if necessary.
  4. Trace subsequent price. Some examples are rejected because the zone has already been visited and price has moved away.
  5. In another candidate, a clear drop is present but the base is missing, so do not force a three-part formation.

Evidence clusters: 1:14:17–1:19:05; 1:47:15–1:50:44; 2:14:43–2:15:21.

Walkthrough C — Bank Nifty weekly chart

The tutor switches to Bank Nifty and scans weekly candles for demand and supply. For one prospective demand area, he mentions a future large decline toward a lower zone and tells students not to panic or simply buy immediately because he named the area. The useful lesson is wait for a genuine setup instead of acting on a forecast alone; today’s class does not give the executable trade rules.

Evidence cluster: 1:53:23–1:58:03. Historic levels and quoted figures from the spoken class are not intended as present-day prices.

Practical identification when the chart is messy

When several nearby candles might be part of the base, classify them according to the incoming directional move, the true pause, and the first meaningful outgoing move. Marking a different base changes both line positions. The tutor acknowledges some such decisions need further chart practice and later advanced material; he does not supply a definitive mechanical algorithm in this episode.

09

Common mistakes and unresolved questions

Separate the demonstrated rules from premature trading assumptions

Selected Q&A across ~31:56–32:38, ~54:37–57:17, ~1:25:50–1:26:58, ~2:17:04–2:17:35
1. Calling any green rally a demand zone

A green candle alone is not enough: find a meaningful leg-in, base and exciting green leg-out. A candidate with no base is incomplete.

2. Confusing base candles with leg-out candles

Very small candles embedded in the basing range may not provide a convincing directional leg. Decide which candles actually comprise the pause. The tutor shows that changing this interpretation changes the resulting zone boundaries.

3. Using the wrong side of a candle body

For demand, proximal is the highest body edge among the selected base candles; distal is the lowest wick. For supply, proximal is the lowest body edge; distal is the highest wick.

4. Using the leg-out candle to draw the zone boundary

In the lesson’s method, you identify the zone from the exit move but draw the two lines on the base candle(s), not around the entire leg-out or arbitrary levels.

5. Ignoring that a zone has already been tested

Ask what happened after the first departure. Once price returned to the proximal line and reacted away, the tutor does not keep counting that candidate as fresh for the marking exercise.

6. Assuming reversal is always preferable

Reversal patterns can reflect a stronger change of control, but continuation patterns can be important in strong trends. Classify the chart context before making any comparison.

7. Forcing both a demand and a supply zone on every chart

Sometimes one category has no qualifying fresh candidate. The tutor explicitly says do not invent one. Learn marking first; deciding when to act on demand versus supply is discussed later.

8. Treating today's zone lessons as a complete entry system

The tutor defers entry, stop-loss, target, near misses and finer selection criteria. No fixed risk/reward, stop-loss distance, profit target or execution signal can be reliably extracted from this lesson alone.

Key takeaways

  1. The leg-out determines whether the formation is demand or supply.
  2. All four patterns have a leg-in, base and leg-out, but candle counts can vary.
  3. Prefer more decisive departure candles as a quality clue, not a guaranteed outcome.
  4. Apply the correct body-to-wick formula to all selected base candles.
  5. A previously tested zone is not a fresh candidate in this tutor’s marking exercise.
  6. Reversal and continuation patterns both matter; trends affect the comparison.
  7. Marking zones and executing trades are separate skills.
