02

LoTL — Level on Top of Level

SOURCE ~05:15–12:35

LoTL (also called Level Over the Level) describes two nearby demand zones or two nearby supply zones, often one on top of the other.

How it forms

  1. A drop pauses in a base candle or base area: the first potential demand zone appears.
  2. Price produces an upward move, but the move may not travel very far.
  3. A second nearby base forms above the first, followed by another upward move.
  4. The two nearby levels may represent linked buying interest. The equivalent idea can occur with supply zones.

The presence of two levels is not, by itself, proof of strength. The tutor repeatedly asks whether there is a meaningful origin, whether a zone achieved something important, and whether the overall context supports treating them together.

Why not always trade only the upper zone?
If price travels through the first zone and reacts from the lower origin, an upper-zone-only entry with a tight stop could get stopped out before the larger buying reaction. LoTL analysis encourages you to examine the whole nearby structure, not just the visually convenient base.
Can a lower demand zone matter even when the upper one appears stronger?
Yes. A lower zone may be the origin that initiated the move or helped remove prior supply. The tutor says that the achievement of the move, not merely the candle’s appearance, should inform the interpretation. ~19:51–20:41
Working principle

If two like-direction zones are close together and belong to the same buying/selling idea, evaluate their combined footprint. Do not mechanically merge unrelated zones just because they are nearby.

03

LoTL entries, stop-loss and the trade-off

SOURCE ~12:35–19:43

For stacked demand zones, the tutor emphasizes that the lower buying origin must be accounted for. In his illustrations, the stop-loss is kept at the lower extreme of the combined structure rather than just beneath the upper base. Entry placement is a separate decision.

Entry choiceAdvantageDrawback
Near the upper zoneGreater chance that price reaches the order.Wider distance to the lower stop; may worsen achievable reward:risk.
At the lower zoneCloser to the actual deeper origin; potentially tighter stop distance.Price may turn at the upper zone and never fill the order.
Between the twoThe tutor’s preferred practical compromise in the schematic case.Still depends on zone quality, timeframe, location, and trade parameters.
The tutor’s personal approach · ~15:00

He usually favors placing a single entry somewhere between the two levels rather than automatically splitting a position into half at each level. This is presented as experience-based judgment, not a mathematical guarantee.

Why splitting the order complicates risk accounting

The lecture uses a ₹1,000 intended-risk illustration: if only the first half of two planned entries fills, your actual exposure may be equivalent to roughly ₹500 of the planned risk, and the result must be evaluated against the quantity actually filled. This is the tutor’s reason for preferring a simpler single-entry plan in that example. It does not mean a staggered entry is always wrong.

The reward:risk reality

If you move the entry upward but retain the lower stop, the risk distance increases. Keeping the same target can reduce the reward:risk ratio. The tutor warns that a trade that approaches 1.91R but never reaches a planned 2R target and later stops out cannot be logged as a 2R winner. ~43:10–43:34

Common mistake

Do not move an entry solely to avoid missing a trade. The tutor stresses that a missed trade is not a realized loss. An unattractive reward:risk trade should not be forced.

Does every LoTL require exactly a midpoint entry?
No. The tutor says outcomes vary case by case. Sometimes price turns at the upper area; sometimes it reaches the lower area. The midpoint is a practical preference for an illustrative structure, not a mandatory, statistically established rule.
What if the levels are on different timeframes?
Read the higher-timeframe location, trend, and lower-timeframe execution together. The instructor also discusses circumstances in which a small execution area and larger location range can be treated as a combined trading area rather than insisting on one chart’s labels. ~23:53–24:47

05

Bull traps and bear traps

SOURCE ~47:11–59:59; ~01:40:53–01:41:50

The class uses “trap” for situations in which traders acting on an obvious chart signal enter in the wrong direction shortly before price turns.

Bull trap

Buying a breakout that reverses

Price moves through a visible resistance area, prompting buyers to act. If the move runs into stronger selling pressure and reverses, breakout buyers can be trapped.

Bear trap

Selling a breakdown that reverses

Price falls below visible support, prompting selling. If price meets meaningful underlying buying and rebounds, sellers can be trapped.

How the course asks you to think

  1. Spot the obvious entry signal that conventional traders might use: a support or resistance break.
  2. Ask which larger demand/supply zone lies immediately beyond that trigger.
  3. Check whether the zone has been tested, broken, or supported by earlier price action.
  4. Check whether the breakout is actually compatible with the location and advanced trend analysis.
  5. Avoid treating a breakout line as a guaranteed entry or predicting that every breakout must fail.
Tutor’s framing versus established fact: the tutor describes many traps as institution-driven. The charts alone do not establish who placed the orders or prove deliberate manipulation; what a learner can actually verify is the price behavior, levels, and subsequent outcome.
Why does the tutor distinguish consolidation before versus after a monthly demand-zone test?
A sell decision immediately before a strong monthly demand test is a very different situation from a consolidation that forms after price has already interacted with that demand. The tutor says a strong downtrend can meet the monthly buying area, pause, consolidate, and only then reveal its next movement. ~54:39–55:42
06

The deeper-zone trap: a hidden origin

SOURCE ~01:00:34–01:16:21

A visible monthly demand zone can fail while a less obvious daily origin just behind it holds. That is an important extension of the LoTL idea.

The tutor’s two variations

Variation A

Same timeframe, separated levels

Two demand zones may be far enough apart that the deeper one is visually overlooked. The tutor refers to this as LoTL with a trap.

Variation B

Different timeframes

A monthly/quarterly/half-yearly visible area may coexist with an origin visible on a weekly or daily chart.

The class emphasizes a subtle point: time aggregation can hide the actual initiating move. A larger green monthly candle can contain an earlier smaller-timeframe reaction that does not present as an obvious separate monthly base. In an example, the lecturer distinguishes the area that actually overcame supply from the one that merely completed the closing move. ~01:10:12–01:11:20

Critical warning

A smaller-timeframe zone beyond a failed larger zone does not automatically mean “keep buying lower.” The idea is to identify the origin in advance and evaluate it using normal trade-quality and risk rules.

07

Multi-timeframe context and actual origins

SOURCE ~01:15:39–01:39:49

Repeatedly, the tutor traces old price action from the monthly chart through weekly and daily candles to see which zone actually started the move, which zone only provided a temporary pause, and what happened to each zone when revisited.

Why the old trend label can mislead

In the L&T Finance Holdings example, a previously discussed position had been evaluated using moving averages before the class covered advanced trend analysis. The tutor revisits that trade and says the advanced interpretation of demand-zone breaks produced a different, more cautious trend reading. His point: updated context may change the trade assessment; a simple “uptrend” label is not enough. ~01:33:20–01:34:27

Three distinctions that prevent false confidence

  • Touch versus close: price may cross a marked line intrabar without giving the kind of close the tutor considers meaningful.
  • Zone appearance versus achievement: a neat-looking base is not always the origin that removed prior opposition.
  • Location versus execution: a powerful higher-timeframe zone can still require a separate, well-scored lower-timeframe setup.
Journal and watchlist habit

The instructor suggests tracking a consistent watchlist for months. Knowing the stock’s unfolding history makes it easier to understand what caused a stop-loss or unexpected reaction, rather than reconstructing the entire chart only after a trade fails. ~01:38:39–01:39:49

08

Conventional support and resistance: the critique

SOURCE ~01:40:16–01:47:48

The tutor explains the usual breakout convention before challenging its lack of precise zone and risk criteria.

Conventional descriptionThe tutor’s counter-question
Buy if price breaks above resistance.What supply lies above the apparent breakout, and is the breakout occurring at a sensible location?
Sell if price breaks below support.What demand lies below the break, and could selling pressure run into it?
Broken support becomes resistance.Which actual supply origin would make that former line effective? An old line alone is insufficient evidence.
Small wiggles around a line are acceptable.Where exactly is entry, invalidation and risk measured? The tutor prefers clearly bounded proximal/distal zone lines.

His strongest objection is not that price never reacts at support or resistance; it is that the common line-only treatment may leave the entry, stop and target too loosely defined. He contrasts this with his demand/supply-based trade-score method. ~01:45:23–01:47:32

Interpretation note: these are the tutor’s claims about technical methods, not a controlled comparison of strategy performance. This notebook does not assert that all support/resistance methods fail or that a zone-based method is always superior.
09

Head-and-shoulders, inverse patterns and repeated tops

SOURCE ~01:48:01–02:06:30

The class names head-and-shoulders, inverse head-and-shoulders, double top/bottom, triple top/bottom, flags, pennants and consolidation structures. The recurring message is that these shapes form because demand and supply repeatedly interrupt price.

Head-and-shoulders in the conventional method

A left shoulder, higher head and right shoulder appear, and a neckline is drawn across the intervening lows. The textbook method discussed in the class looks for a break or close below the neckline and projects a decline using the size of the formation. The tutor asks: what causes price to travel that full distance if a meaningful demand zone interrupts it? ~01:49:12–01:50:14

Inverse head-and-shoulders

He also examines an inverse formation after a strong quarterly demand test. In that example, what conventional traders might read as an upside pattern is interpreted as price recovering from the higher-timeframe demand area, with a weekly demand execution available before waiting for the pattern to fully confirm. ~02:00:34–02:01:52

Double and triple tops / bottoms

Repeated turning points may be signs of price reacting to the same previously tested area. The tutor draws attention to how repeated tests can weaken a zone and why buying solely because “this is the third bottom” may miss the actual location or a new execution zone. His Colgate-Palmolive illustration is discussed as a repeated-bottom formation. ~02:04:03–02:04:32; ~02:13:35–02:13:51

Remember

The drawing names the shape; the zone analysis asks why it exists, how much opposition remains, and whether a trade is justified.

Trendlines, triangles and narrowing consolidation

SOURCE ~02:05:21–02:22:36

The class even explains how people draw trendlines: choose a line that meaningfully connects multiple visible turning points, rather than forcing every wick to touch it. The lecturer notes that two or more points can define a line, with several additional contacts providing visual relevance. ~02:14:18–02:15:37

Why a triangle narrows

His explanation is that repeated interactions with demand and supply can make price oscillate in a progressively smaller range. A narrow base and eventual breakout do not by themselves reveal the breakout direction.

With context

Higher-timeframe demand below

The tutor considers this support for an eventual upward move, subject to actual confirmation and setup quality.

With context

Higher-timeframe supply above

The tutor considers this pressure that can favor the downside, not a reason to blindly short a triangle line.

Without a defensible higher-timeframe read, guessing the direction of a breakout is treated as speculation. If the breakout escapes before your planned entry, he recommends accepting the missed trade and looking for another valid setup instead of chasing. ~02:15:58–02:16:35

Stochastic: confirmation, not a standalone trigger

SOURCE ~02:22:36–02:34:44

The tutor introduces the Stochastic oscillator with its familiar 20 (oversold) and 80 (overbought) reference areas and two indicator lines. He instructs students to leave the platform’s default settings as shown in the class.

Conventional readingReading recommended in this class
Stochastic enters oversold → buy.First find a valid demand-zone setup. A low Stochastic reading can add confirmation, not replace the setup.
Stochastic enters overbought → sell.First find a valid supply-zone setup. A high reading alone does not justify a short.
Wait for a line crossover.Because the oscillator can lag price, the instructor says a crossover may come after the desirable entry. He does not make the crossover mandatory for his zone-based confirmation.
Use the oscillator at any chart level.He gives priority to the execution timeframe; the intermediate timeframe may provide additional context.
Lagging indicator warning · ~02:28:30–29:18

The indicator may turn convincingly upward only after one or two green candles have already appeared. For the tutor, this is why indicator confirmation should follow the demand/supply setup—not delay a well-planned entry indefinitely.

What if Stochastic has been oversold several times already?
The instructor shows cases in which earlier oversold signals did not coincide with his demand zones, while a later one did. His lesson is about conditional interpretation—not a guarantee that every zone plus oscillator reading will work. ~02:48:40–02:49:15
12

RSI: use divergence to support a zone

SOURCE ~02:34:51–03:00:16

The Relative Strength Index appears with the conventional 30 / 70 thresholds and a 50 midpoint. The instructor is especially interested in positive and negative divergence rather than treating “RSI is oversold” as sufficient reason to buy.

Positive divergence

Price weakens, RSI improves

Price makes a lower low while the oscillator makes a relatively higher low / improves. The tutor considers this potentially useful if the price is also entering a demand zone.

Negative divergence

Price strengthens, RSI weakens

The reverse relationship: price can make a higher high while oscillator strength fails to match. The relevant setting is a meaningful supply-side context.

A subtle refinement in this class

The tutor says not to insist on perfect-looking oscillator swings or exact line crossings before acknowledging improvement. If RSI has reached an oversold area and is starting upward while price has reached the intended demand area, that can count as an additional confirmation in his method. He cautions against waiting for a textbook two-bottom drawing if it delays the existing setup. ~02:49:15–02:51:35; ~02:55:50–02:56:29

He suggests RSI divergence may be useful even without a zone, but does not give verifiable backtest data for the accuracy claim. Here it is treated as the tutor’s opinion, not an empirical success-rate promise. ~02:39:37–02:40:31

Priority order

Find and assess the demand/supply trade first. Only then consult RSI or Stochastic for one more piece of evidence. The tutor prefers a clean chart with few indicators. ~02:52:42–02:53:07; ~02:59:26–03:00:16

Practical pre-trade workflow

Know the invalidation

14

Quick revision and takeaway

The lesson in one paragraph

Nearby demand/supply levels should sometimes be read as one larger LoTL structure. A visible support/resistance break may be misleading if a stronger zone or hidden origin lies beyond it. Familiar patterns—head-and-shoulders, repeated tops/bottoms, trendlines, and triangles—are price shapes whose underlying buying/selling context matters more to the tutor than the pattern name. Stochastic and RSI can offer secondary confirmation, but the lecturer’s starting point remains higher-timeframe context, actual origin, and a well-defined risk plan.

Remember these seven points

  1. LoTL means two nearby zones of the same side.
  2. When zones are linked, account for the deeper origin when defining risk.
  3. The tutor likes a middle entry in some LoTL situations, but it is case dependent.
  4. Breakout/breakdown alone does not reveal what lies beyond the line.
  5. Timeframe aggregation can hide real buying/selling origins.
  6. Do not trade a chart pattern without checking higher-timeframe demand/supply.
  7. RSI and Stochastic come after finding the actual setup.
Practice idea: finish the interactive diagrams and quiz, then compare your chart markings against the original class screenshots whenever those are available. This is especially important for examples involving exact origin and execution candles.
