01

The problem Trade Score solves

00:00–13:00

Previous lessons introduced multiple-timeframe analysis. Once you have decided the direction in which you want to trade, a new problem appears: which execution zone should you choose? You may find two weekly-income-trade setups in one stock, or two lower-timeframe demand zones beneath the same higher-timeframe location. Both may look valid until you compare their underlying quality. 00:02–01:09

Trade Score is the tutor's method of giving each execution zone a numerical quality rating using the zone's freshness, the force of its departure, and the number of candles at its base. It helps rank several candidates rather than arbitrarily picking one. 12:25–13:20

Three timeframes, three different jobs

1 · Higher timeframeLocation / curve

Find the relevant higher-timeframe demand or supply context. This points toward buyer-side or seller-side opportunities.

2 · Intermediate timeframeTrend

Check the current direction. The tutor strongly recommends keeping trend on your side.

3 · Lower timeframeExecution

Refine a zone and plan an actual entry and stop-loss. This is where the Trade Score is applied.

Important boundary: Score the execution timeframe zone, not the location or trending timeframe itself. Do not replace location and trend analysis with the score. 58:49–59:15

The teaching example uses a weekly uptrend, then goes to the daily chart to compare three demand zones. The score answers the question “which is stronger as an execution zone?”; the higher-timeframe context remains an independent decision. 11:43–12:53

About timeframe combinations: The tutor refers to MIT/WIT and other trade styles from the previous lesson. In a demonstrated MIT example, monthly is location, weekly is trend, and daily is execution. Do not generalize one example into a universal table for every style without its original lesson. 01:16:11; 02:18:11–02:19:00
02

The 7-point Trade Score

13:00–21:00

The same formula applies to demand and supply zones. First identify a genuine zone using the marking method from earlier classes. Then assign points for the following three dimensions. 13:13–19:12

Freshness / 3 + Strength / 2 + Base / 2 = Total / 7

Parameter A · Freshness (0 to 3)

Execution zone statusPointsWhy the tutor ranks it this way
Completely fresh — price has not returned to test the zone3Pending orders may still remain; the tutor considers the first revisit the strongest opportunity.
Tested once1.5Some pending orders may already have been filled; a second reaction is possible but considered weaker.
Tested twice or more0The tutor rates its freshness as zero and generally considers it not worth taking on freshness grounds.

In his example, an execution level is still “fresh” when price has not touched the proximal boundary after the original departure. A revisit and reaction may happen more than once, but the first visit is preferred. 17:25–18:03; 26:06–26:26

Parameter B · Strength of departure (1 to 2)

How price leaves the zonePointsInterpretation
Two or more exciting / explosive leg-out candles2Strong, forceful departure. Two is sufficient; three or four still receive a maximum of two points.
One exciting leg-out candle + gap2The gap is an alternative indication of strong departure in the instructor's rubric.
One exciting leg-out candle with no gap1Some departure strength, but weaker than either two candles or a one-candle-plus-gap departure.

What is a leg-out? The price move away from the base that originally formed the demand/supply zone. A bullish leg-out rises from demand; a bearish leg-out falls from supply. “Exciting” means the visibly decisive candles as taught in earlier classes. The transcript does not give a precise numeric body-to-wick threshold here, so no such threshold is invented. 14:52–16:12; 18:10–19:05

Parameter C · Time at the base (0 to 2)

Number of base candlesPointsMeaning
1–3 base candles2A relatively brief pause before price departs.
4–5 base candles1A longer pause; weaker quality by this parameter.
More than 5 base candles0Too much time at the base for full quality points.

The score rewards fewer base candles. One, two, and three all receive the same two points in this simplified formula; their qualitative differences may still matter when choosing between tied zones. 16:19–17:12; 19:05–19:22

Unusual case: no base candle. In Q&A, the tutor allows that a directly departing move without a conventional base can sometimes be marked. This is a practical exception, not a separate formula established in the verified rubric. The calculator below deliberately flags that case for manual review instead of inventing a score. 02:01:39–02:01:58

04

Compare several zones — including ties

25:40–46:00

The tutor's three-zone example

The instructor returns to a chart containing three demand zones. He assigns 7/7 to Zone 1, 6/7 to Zone 2, and 7/7 to Zone 3. That immediately surfaces a harder question: both Zone 1 and Zone 3 have the same top score, so which one is better? 25:51–30:40

Demand zone 17 / 7

Fresh; 2 leg-out candles; 2 base candles.

Demand zone 26 / 7

A weaker parameter reduces its score; not automatically useless.

Demand zone 37 / 7

Fresh; 2 leg-out candles; 1 base candle.

Initially students are asked to rank them by the points. Later, he explains that a 6/7 can sometimes be practically attractive, even more attractive than a particular 7/7, once the surrounding context is checked. 28:07–29:30

How to break a 7/7 tie

  1. Look beyond the maximum score. Compare how exciting and explosive the leg-out candles actually are; the two-point ceiling hides some qualitative differences.
  2. Compare the base and formation. If other things differ, the candlestick structures can still help separate the candidates.
  3. Examine reversal versus continuation formation. The tutor raises this distinction as one comparison in the tied-zone discussion.
  4. Assess the execution zone's position relative to higher-timeframe location. If two zones remain almost identical, the location/execution relationship becomes critical: where is “Arjun” relative to “Shri Krishna”?
  5. Consider whether more than one valid zone can be monitored. The tutor acknowledges that both may be considered rather than forcing only one. 30:09–30:28; 44:58–46:03
Do not turn 7/7 into an automatic trade. It can still be a poor contextual choice if an opposing higher-timeframe area is close, there is no supporting location, the trend is against it, or the risk/target is unsuitable. The number measures the three specified qualities, not everything on the chart.

05

Arjun, Shri Krishna, and the importance of location

34:15–58:50

The tutor uses a memorable Mahabharata analogy to teach why an impressive entry zone can fail when the larger context is missing. The analogy is about support from a stronger timeframe; it is not a literal trading indicator.

AnalogyOn the chartWhy it matters
Shri KrishnaHigher-timeframe demand locationPotential supporting zone for a lower-timeframe buy execution.
ArjunLower-timeframe demand executionThe specific buy entry area that must withstand selling pressure.
Bhishma PitamahHigher-timeframe supply locationPotential supporting zone for a lower-timeframe sell execution.
DuryodhanLower-timeframe supply executionThe specific sell entry area that must withstand buying pressure.

An isolated Arjun without Shri Krishna represents a demand execution without the higher-timeframe demand support. Duryodhan without Bhishma similarly represents a supply execution without higher-timeframe supply support. The tutor advises that these isolated executions are weaker choices even if the individual candles look excellent. 34:45–37:37

When both bullish and bearish sides have their supporting locations present, the intermediate-timeframe trend helps reveal which side is actively stronger. His teaching is to combine trend, location and execution rather than treating any of them in isolation. 38:58–39:43

Distance to the supporting location

In the equal-score comparison, execution zones sit at different distances from the higher-timeframe demand. The closer or more fully aligned an execution is to that location, the more directly the two may resist incoming selling pressure together. A distant execution may face opposing pressure first by itself; this is why the tutor calls it more aggressive. 46:21–52:17

Aggressive does not always mean forbidden

The tutor distinguishes more aggressive and more conservative demand executions. He does not claim every distant zone is automatically invalid. A powerful independent execution may still be considered, but it has less nearby higher-timeframe protection. For beginners he repeatedly emphasizes not stretching an execution so far from support that opposing higher-timeframe supply has already been hit or is about to be hit. 51:31–52:17; 56:36–57:20; 02:06:12–02:07:00

Context can defeat candles. A powerful-looking demand execution can still fail under selling pressure from opposing higher-timeframe supply. Likewise, strong-looking supply can fail against significant higher-timeframe demand. The score does not measure that conflict.
06

Good, better, best — what the score does not capture

58:49–1:06:40

The tutor's ideal execution has more than strong arithmetic. He describes a particularly strong demand zone as one with 7/7 Trade Score, powerful leg-out candles, strong closing, and coinciding higher-timeframe demand support. The same contextual logic reverses for supply. 59:15–1:02:40

A quality ladder rather than an invented threshold

GoodScore shows strength

The three scored zone characteristics have been checked.

BetterPrice confirms the move

Closes and departure behavior provide additional qualitative evidence.

BestContext aligns too

Higher-timeframe demand/supply support and trend work with the entry.

The tutor highlights that a candle can show a convincing move but fail to close above an important area because it encountered opposing weekly supply. In a real case study he calls that a “better” rather than a “best” structure, while also noting it is not necessarily worthless. 1:03:00–1:05:35

High-priority extra check: Where did the move close, not just where did its wick reach? Was opposing higher-timeframe supply or demand nearby? Does the execution zone meaningfully coincide with its same-side location? These observations improve the decision, even though they are not added as extra points to the 7-point formula.

In another case study the teacher praises a zone where location and execution coincide, both display strong closes, the execution has two forceful leg-out candles, and a single base candle. It represents his concept of an exceptional or “best” zone. 1:22:12–1:22:52

07

Entry, target, stop-loss and risk considerations

1:05:40–1:18:00

Trade Score is a quality assessment of the execution level. Actual trading also requires an entry plan, stop-loss, target, and awareness of opposing levels. The tutor gives examples with roughly 2:1 reward-to-risk and sometimes higher targets for unusually strong setups. This lesson says further risk-management and trap material is still to come, so the notebook does not invent position sizing or fixed risk percentages. 1:05:40–1:06:26

Reward-to-risk is not the same as the Trade Score

Reward-to-risk compares the distance to the target against the distance to the stop-loss. For example, if an illustrative entry is at ₹100, an illustrative stop at ₹95, and an illustrative target at ₹110, then the risk is ₹5 and the planned reward ₹10: 2:1. These rupee values are provided only to explain arithmetic; they are not quoted trade prices from the source.

R:R = Target distance ÷ Stop distanceExample: 10 ÷ 5 = 2R

Where does the tutor look for a target?

For a demand execution trade, he teaches looking toward the opposing supply zone on the intermediate/trending timeframe. In his MIT example, where monthly is location and daily is execution, that means examining weekly supply. He explicitly notes the target should be finalized when the execution entry actually appears because the relevant opposing supply levels can change during the wait. By symmetry, a sell execution must consider opposing demand; the verified passage explicitly details the demand-trade example. 1:06:43–1:07:47; 1:16:11–1:17:18

Real-example learning, without treating outcomes as guarantees

In a discussed example the tutor describes an exceptionally aligned demand structure that achieved approximately 2R quickly and more than 4R overall as price rose toward the opposing area. He illustrates that risking ₹1,000 for a 4:1 setup corresponds to a gross ₹4,000 planned reward (excluding costs). This is historical commentary from the instructor, not an expectation for future setups. 1:13:59–1:15:11

Another important correction: the label MIT, WIT, or another trade style does not dictate exactly how many days a target must take. A high-quality setup can reach its objective quickly; another can remain in a range for days. The tutor gives the contrast of a trade reaching its target in minutes versus another staying stagnant for 8–10 days. 1:15:11–1:15:58

Risk note: No point score guarantees a win, no 2:1 or 4:1 target is guaranteed, and a stop-loss can be hit. Check whether a feasible target exists before risking money. Actual loss may differ from the planned stop because of gaps, slippage, costs, or execution conditions; those are general safety considerations, not a new tutor-specific strategy.

Important class Q&A and practical exceptions

~2:00:45–2:39:00

The latter part of the uploaded lesson includes an extended question-and-answer discussion. The following consolidates questions and answers that could be confirmed from the available source excerpts; it does not purport to transcribe the entire Q&A verbatim.

1. What if we are very low on the curve but the trend points down?

The tutor prefers trading with the trend. If the desired long setup is genuinely extraordinary, he says taking a calculated chance may be considered despite a downtrend. But if any part of the setup is doubtful, his advice is to skip it. It is not a blanket exemption from trend analysis. 11:43–12:19; 2:00:59–2:01:12

2. Can a valid demand/supply zone have no conventional base candle?

Yes, the tutor acknowledges that sometimes price departs directly and that such a zone may still be marked using practical judgment. This is presented as a modification/exception to basic marking rules, not as evidence to automatically allocate “two base points” in every zero-base situation. 2:01:39–2:01:59

3. Is a 7/7 zone automatically better than every 6/7 zone?

No. As a numerical first pass, 7/7 has stronger scored attributes. But the tutor later brings in closing quality, the actual force of the move, the matching higher-timeframe location, and the opposing context. A well-supported 6/7 can be preferable to a 7/7 with problematic context. 28:07–29:30; 59:15–1:02:40

4. What if two equally scored zones are both excellent?

You can consider monitoring both. To discriminate between them, inspect their leg-out quality, formation, and especially how they are positioned against the same-side higher-timeframe location. The tutor explicitly contrasts aggressive distance from location with more conservative or coinciding positioning. 30:09–30:28; 44:58–52:17

5. Does the lower-timeframe execution have to coincide perfectly with the higher-timeframe location?

Coincidence is strongly preferred, but the tutor does not prohibit every aggressive execution. If a more aggressive execution is independently very powerful, he says it may be considered, while warning that the opposing market pressure can still overpower it. He tells beginners to take location support seriously. 51:31–52:17; 2:06:12–2:07:00

6. Can a slightly tested higher-timeframe location be considered?

In specific chart discussions he says a slightly tested location — including a roughly 25% retest in the context described — may still be considered where a newly formed execution is very strong and has good closing. This is a contextual exception about location, not a change to the fresh/once/twice numerical scores for the execution zone. 1:17:25–1:17:49; 2:09:01–2:09:37

7. If a 7/7 execution looks good, why also examine the larger chart?

Because a lower-timeframe entry alone cannot tell you whether stronger opposing orders are nearby, whether an intermediate trend supports the direction, or where the next important target/opposing area lies. The teacher uses the Arjun/Shri Krishna analogy specifically to prevent treating the lower timeframe as a self-contained system. 34:45–39:43; 1:34:28–1:35:05

8. How is the target determined for a daily demand execution in an MIT setup?

In the example where the monthly chart provides location, the weekly chart provides trend, and the daily chart provides execution, the tutor checks the weekly supply zone for an opposing target area. He says to reassess it when the order triggers, since new weekly zones can form while waiting. 1:06:43–1:07:47

9. Should we assume an MIT or WIT trade necessarily takes a long time?

No. The tutor explicitly says target timing is variable. Strong multi-timeframe alignment may produce an unexpectedly fast move, while weaker quality may result in a long period of sideways price action. Do not derive a fixed number of holding days from the style abbreviation. 1:15:11–1:15:58

10. What if price is at a higher-timeframe demand location but trend is down and no good long execution exists?

The tutor advises passing on the trade rather than entering merely because price is low on the curve. A favorable location is not enough without convincing execution. And he does not suggest shorting solely because the trend is down: that side also requires its own location and execution support. 2:28:35–2:29:19

11. Why does he discourage trying to scalp every stock?

In the Q&A he says an intraday trader or scalper should focus on a limited number of stocks they can follow consistently throughout the session. He does not present this as an extra point in the Trade Score rubric. 2:05:33–2:06:03

12. What if an apparently good setup is near a distribution area or shows other warning signs?

At the end of the Q&A, when a student raises nearby distribution/concerns, the tutor recommends leaving the questionable setup alone. This reinforces the broader theme: do not force a trade because one or two ingredients look appealing. 2:38:17–2:38:32

Instructor's learning sequence: He notes that further concepts—including traps and comprehensive risk management—are taught later. Avoid treating Episode 8 by itself as a complete live-trading strategy. 1:06:12–1:06:26; 1:39:51–1:40:30

Reusable chart-review workflow

Application & homework

This is a study workflow assembled from the tutor's stated sequence, not an additional strategy or mechanical trading signal. Use it when reviewing historical charts or preparing trade notes.

Before you score anything

  1. Mark the higher-timeframe demand and supply locations. Identify whether the relevant area favors buyer or seller opportunities and where price sits on the curve.
  2. Check the intermediate-timeframe trend. Ask whether the planned action follows the trend or is an unusual countertrend exception.
  3. Look for the lower-timeframe execution. Do not force an execution zone if the necessary base and meaningful departure are absent; consider the instructor's explicitly mentioned exceptional cases with care.
  4. Identify all competing valid execution zones. Multiple zones in one stock are the reason the Trade Score was introduced.

Evaluate every execution zone

Ignoring a nearby opposing higher-timeframe zoneStudy where opposing pressure can appear and where targets make sense.

Flashcards, quick revision and takeaways

End-of-lesson review

Memory in 60 seconds

The Trade Score is F + S + B = 7 maximum. Freshness has the largest weight (3). Strength and base duration contribute up to 2 each. The scoring system is only applied to execution zones, whether demand or supply. A perfect score is not permission to ignore higher-timeframe location, intermediate trend, the strength of the closing, or nearby opposing zones.

Card 1 / 120 mastered

The essential takeaways

  1. Score the execution zone only. Higher-timeframe location and intermediate trend have different jobs.
  2. Freshness is worth 3/7. Fresh = 3; once tested = 1.5; twice or more = 0.
  3. Departure is worth 2/7. Two exciting leg-out candles or one with a gap get two points; one without gap gets one.
  4. Base duration is worth 2/7. One to three candles get two; four to five get one; above five get zero.
  5. Equal scores do not mean equal setups. Compare closes, actual leg-out quality, formation and relationship to location.
  6. The location/execution pairing matters. The tutor's Krishna/Arjun metaphor represents higher- and lower-timeframe reinforcement.
  7. The trend is a major filter. Countertrend trades need exceptional evidence, not optimism.
  8. Targets are contextual. Inspect the opposing intermediate-timeframe zone when planning an execution trade.
  9. Know when to pass. No trade is better than forcing a doubtful setup.
