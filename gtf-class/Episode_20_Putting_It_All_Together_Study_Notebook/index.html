CHAPTER 01 · Core method · Source ~02:04–03:15

The final lesson: putting the method together

The teacher frames Episode 20 as the transition from knowing individual techniques to applying a repeatable trading process. The stated agenda is to prioritize previous trade scores, speed up stock scanning, learn a distinct method of stop-loss trailing and decide how to continue practicing after the course.

The organizing principle

A setup is not attractive merely because one indicator or candlestick looks bullish or bearish. First build a higher-timeframe interpretation of the stock, then identify a relevant zone and execution, check trend, and only then consider supplementary evidence.

What does “putting it all together” mean?

It means linking location (the higher-timeframe demand/supply area), trend (the intervening timeframe’s direction) and execution (a lower-timeframe structure from which a trade can be planned). The lesson revisits this framework while analyzing individual stocks and while showing how to store notes for future sessions.

The final episode emphasizes consistency of process rather than hunting for a new indicator. A chart can appear exciting, yet still fail the location or trend test.

Why this matters for a beginner

The beginner’s temptation is to open many stocks, notice a familiar shape and trade immediately. The teacher’s alternative is to know a smaller set of stocks deeply: what the higher timeframes show, where supply and demand lie, which direction is permitted and what would invalidate the idea.

CHAPTER 02 · Core method · Source ~09:32–40:00

Stop-loss trailing: the main advanced technique

Trailing a stop-loss is a way to stay in a favorable trade for a larger move while progressively defining where you will leave if the market reverses. This class does not present a fixed-number-of-rupees trail as its preferred long-run method.

Why the teacher questions fixed-distance trailing

At roughly 10 minutes, a conventional illustration moves through prices such as 120 and 130 and trails a stop to around 125. The criticism is that such fixed-distance adjustments are detached from the chart’s underlying supply-and-demand structure. A normal pullback may trigger the stop even when the stock’s larger uptrend remains intact.

These prices illustrate the classroom comparison; they are not a fixed rupee rule prescribed for your trades.

First decide whether trailing is appropriate

The teacher reserves this idea for a high-quality trade where the top-down picture is clear, price is moving from or has confirmed at a higher-timeframe demand zone, and a meaningful larger move is plausible. If a good-quality higher-timeframe supply zone stands overhead, that zone informs a potential target. He cautions against taking profit mechanically at an arbitrary 2:1 ratio when the chart offers a meaningful structural target.

He distinguishes the situation before a stock reaches all-time-high territory from after it moves beyond old highs, where clear historical supply may no longer exist above price.

Weekly demand-zone trail after all-time-high breakout
  1. Complete the top-down analysis and have a legitimate entry; an all-time-high price alone is not a buy signal.
  2. Once price has broken into all-time-high territory, follow new weekly demand zones as they form.
  3. Keep the protective stop below the distal (lower) boundary of the relevant newly formed weekly demand zone.
  4. As price progresses and forms newer eligible weekly demand zones, advance the stop according to the zone structure.
  5. Exit when the active stop is breached; do not keep holding merely because the peak was higher.

The teacher explains this through a Wipro chart and repeatedly stresses the weekly, not daily, timeframe for this particular long-run method. He says he moved to weekly after testing an earlier smaller-timeframe approach; this is his experience and is not independently verified performance evidence.

What the Wipro example is really teaching

In the described historic Wipro illustration, the stock rises dramatically from a low starting area. Several weekly demand zones support the advance, with a later weekly demand-zone stop ultimately hit. The key lesson is to value the large portion captured, rather than regret the distance from the ultimate price peak to the exit. The exact trade prices and historical candle placement cannot be reconstructed from the transcript excerpts alone, so the graphic below is deliberately schematic.

Boundary rule

Do not wait for a candle close if your preselected stop has already been hit. Around 30:50, the teacher specifically warns against “closing-basis stop-loss” as a substitute for the stop. Execution and gap/slippage risks remain real.

CHAPTER 03 · Core method · Source ~44:42–52:35

Trade-score hierarchy and non-negotiable zone quality

The teacher reviews the scoring factors from earlier lessons but gives them different priorities. The most important lesson is not to add every indicator mechanically: begin with a good zone, then use supporting factors to strengthen the picture.

3 pointsFreshnessGive priority to fresh, untested levels.
2 pointsStrengthLook for an energetic departure / leg out.
2 pointsTime at basePrefer an appropriate, compact base.
2 pointsSector analysisA high-priority supporting factor.
Highest priority: the first three factors

Freshness (3): the teacher says he is not assigning points here to once-tested areas; the preferred level is fresh. Strength (2): the departure should be convincing. Several powerful legs out are attractive, but one exceptionally explosive move may be strong in substance even if it visually looks like only one leg. Time at base (2): he emphasizes having only the necessary base candles rather than a drawn-out or weak base.

He calls these the highest-priority, dark-green group. Do not mistake a long list of secondary signals for an excuse to use a poor-quality demand or supply zone.

Second priority: sector alignment and the 20-period EMA

He explicitly places sector analysis above the EMA 20 among his secondary factors and gives sector analysis two points. He cites stock examples where the EMA 20 acted as a useful reference. These are supporting observations, not substitutes for top-down context or high-quality levels. A precise EMA-20 point allocation is not established by the excerpts available here.

Lowest priority: optional confluence

He lists golden/death crossovers, gaps, candlestick patterns, stochastic and RSI as optional additions, each discussed in the one-point supporting group. Their ordering within that group is not a ranking. He explicitly warns against endlessly adding more indicators and conventional patterns merely to gain more apparent confirmation.

His point is that conventional market participants also react to recognizable signals, so those signals can sometimes reinforce a demand/supply-based idea; they should not become the primary thesis.

Do not treat the overall score as magic

After the top-down thesis exists, extra confirmations can increase confidence. But a trade with multiple optional signals can still be wrong. The teacher repeatedly asks students to understand where price is in the larger structure, whether zones coincide across timeframes and whether the closing/action at the relevant area is good. This lesson does not provide a verified universal score cutoff or guarantee of success.

CHAPTER 04 · Workflow · Source ~41:20–44:30; 55:54–~02:20

Screen stocks from high timeframes to low

Screening is an opportunity-finding aid, not a trading signal. The teacher wants you to choose candidate stocks, study the full top-down picture and ignore charts whose structure cannot be explained.

52-week-high scanner ≠ all-time high

The demonstrated one-click filter includes “Stock approaching 52-week high” and a distinct “Stock crosses 52-week high”. Approaching means price is getting near that reference; crossing means it has moved through it. The 52-week high is only a one-year reference and does not necessarily equal the stock’s all-time high. The teacher shows selecting a universe (whole market or a quality stock list), running the condition and scanning the results.

Possible starting universes

The examples include a curated quality-stock list, constituents of an index such as Nifty or Sensex, and stocks you personally want to study (the teacher mentions names such as Tata Motors and Ashok Leyland). The filter narrows what to inspect; a familiar company name is never the justification for entry.

The top-down scan
  1. Inspect the long view: yearly, half-yearly, quarterly where useful to understand major context and strong opposing zones.
  2. Continue to monthly, weekly and daily views to locate demand/supply, judge trend and search for usable execution structures.
  3. Mark relevant zones; distinguish fresh and strong areas from repeatedly tested or messy (“garbage”) price action.
  4. Ask whether the intended trade conflicts with any important higher-timeframe zone, or enters a potential trap.
  5. Write the chart’s present state and conditional plan before taking any trade.
Why avoiding random lower-timeframe trades matters

The teacher warns that a seemingly attractive weekly or daily execution can lead into a higher-timeframe supply/demand area, where a trade may fail. He asks students to have the whole top-down “theory” of the stock rather than selecting a WIT (weekly income trade) or another setup from an isolated short chart.

CHAPTER 05 · Workflow · Source ~~01:02; ~01:47; 02:01–02:10

Location → trend → execution across timeframes

The recurring vocabulary of this course is location, trend and execution. A trade is considered only when these three parts tell a coherent story and the direction is permitted by the trend.

Setup nameLocation timeframeTrend timeframeExecution timeframe
HIT · Half-yearly income tradeHalf-yearlyQuarterlyMonthly
QIT · Quarterly income tradeQuarterlyMonthlyWeekly
MIT · Monthly income tradeMonthlyWeeklyDaily
WIT · Weekly income tradeWeeklyDaily125-minute

These mappings are from examples of the course’s location/trend/execution structure, including a half-yearly example at 01:47 and monthly/weekly examples around 02:03–02:10. The 125-minute timeframe is the chart view referenced for WIT in the accessible passages.

Walkthrough: a possible monthly long trade
  1. Find a monthly demand location, ideally relevant and high-quality.
  2. Check the weekly trend. It should support the planned long direction; a moving average alone is not always enough to define trend.
  3. Look on the daily chart for the execution area and assess whether it coincides with the larger location.
  4. Check the stock again when price arrives; the trend may have changed while you were waiting.
  5. Assess quality, opposing supply, target and stop before the order.

In one walkthrough, a weekly moving-average picture looks ambiguous, but the teacher reads the trend as upward because earlier supply areas were breached. The emphasis is on price action and structure, not only an EMA reading.

What if trend disagrees?

A favorable location plus a visually attractive execution is insufficient when the intervening timeframe’s trend contradicts your direction. In the scan, the teacher records trends first specifically so that the allowed and disallowed trade directions are clear.

“Check trend at arrival”

A scenario may look promising today, yet by the time price returns to the demand/supply location the relevant trend could be sideways or reversed. Therefore recheck the relevant trend before treating a planned future MIT/WIT as an actual opportunity.

CHAPTER 06 · Case studies · Source ~~00:57–01:50; 02:19–02:23

How to interpret the live chart walkthroughs

The teacher moves among real stock charts to demonstrate how a top-down view changes a decision. These are reasoning case studies, not buy/sell recommendations or exact OHLC reproductions.

Case A — apparent pattern versus true cause

A stock bounces after reaching higher-timeframe monthly demand and later forms what a conventional observer might label a “W pattern” on a weekly chart. The teacher focuses on why the shape formed: reactions to demand and intervening supply. The recognizable letter-shape is less important than understanding which zones caused the price turns.

Case B — higher-timeframe supply can cancel a neat lower-timeframe long

A daily execution may look attractive, but a serious supply zone from the monthly, quarterly or half-yearly chart may lie immediately overhead. The lesson’s scan compares these levels so that a candidate is not accepted merely on the basis of a single daily demand candle.

Case C — half-yearly short context

At roughly 01:47, the teacher discusses a half-yearly supply location, a quarterly downtrend and a monthly execution, accompanied by an observed death crossover and areas already breached on the lower chart. The confluence is useful only because the core location/trend/execution structure is present.

Case D — a clean all-uptrend stock

Near 02:20, the teacher writes quarterly, monthly, weekly and daily trends as upward. That directs attention to permitted long ideas, then he records the presence or absence of QIT, MIT and WIT opportunities and further conditions. It is a planning sheet, not a command to buy at the current price.

Important distinction

The instructor’s commentary, projected targets and examples are his interpretations at the time of the recording. The available transcript passages do not allow independently confirming the tickers’ exact historical chart levels or resulting returns.

CHAPTER 07 · Workflow · Source ~01:56:47–02:10:59

Build a practical multi-chart workspace

The goal is less clicking and faster recognition, not the maximum possible number of indicators. The instructor demonstrates a three-chart layout in a trading-chart platform.

Suggested working layout from the demonstration

Keep three synced chart views: a larger daily chart, a weekly chart and a monthly chart. Save the layout for reuse. If one chart is hard to read, he demonstrates using the Z shortcut to expand that pane temporarily and return it to the grid.

For other trade families, adjust the triplet. For example, a WIT check may use weekly location / daily trend / 125-minute execution. He emphasizes changing only the chart you need rather than resetting every timeframe.

How to use the workspace intelligently
  1. Start with the higher timeframe and write the trend.
  2. Observe the location and its quality.
  3. Inspect the intended lower-timeframe execution only after location and trend make sense.
  4. Keep a little visual room for ticker search and preserve the final marked layout for follow-up.

Large monitors can be helpful, but the underlying decision method does not depend on a specific monitor count.

CHAPTER 08 · Workflow · Source ~02:20:08–02:35:09

Build a stock-by-stock research notebook

The strongest operational advice in the class is to stop randomly jumping from ticker to ticker. Build a limited list of genuinely analyzed stocks, preserve the work, revisit it and add new names gradually.

Record these fields for each stock
  1. Trends: quarterly, monthly, weekly, daily; note which trade directions are allowed.
  2. Possible setups: QIT, MIT, WIT or another relevant income-trade idea; no need to force all types to be present.
  3. Location: the higher-timeframe demand/supply area; note important coinciding or opposing levels.
  4. Execution: lower-timeframe structure, freshness, strength, time at base and any traps.
  5. Confirmation still required: sector/index support, trend at arrival, condition that must develop.
  6. Conditional trade plan: what you will consider if price reaches the location, entry, stop, structural target and trailing possibility.
Carry the chart forward, not from scratch

Save marked price zones and notes. On subsequent checks, update what actually changed. The teacher warns about accidentally deleting all chart annotations with a bulk-delete shortcut; his practical advice is to preserve the annotated history and delete or amend individual marks as needed.

Quality of your watchlist beats quantity

He gives an illustrative pace of roughly 50 properly analyzed stocks in 30 days (fewer than two per day on average). He also says even two careful scans a day are acceptable. Later you may add one per day or tailor the list to your monitoring capacity. A deeply understood list of four stocks can be more useful than 100 randomly opened charts.

Different trading goals demand different list sizes: a trader aiming to watch intraday candles may favor a smaller set; someone seeking fewer high-timeframe setups may follow a somewhat broader, still manageable list.

Do not prematurely trade prediction-based scenarios

The teacher cautions that identifying a likely consolidation, breakout or options move at the current market price requires months of disciplined top-down practice. He mentions roughly two to six months in one warning and three to six months in another as practice horizons, not a guaranteed certification deadline.

CHAPTER 09 · Risk & discipline · Source ~18:19–19:21; 02:50:26–02:53:17

Entry, stop, target and trade management

The final class returns to one rule repeatedly: decide what you intend to do before the trade, then obey the trade plan rather than rewriting it out of emotion.

Plan the trade at the right location

Use a quality demand zone for a planned long (or a supply zone for a planned short), then place the entry and protective stop according to the level and the specific execution strategy taught earlier in the course. Look for meaningful opposing zones to determine a structural target. Episode 20’s accessible passages do not supply an all-purpose entry formula or stop buffer, so this notebook deliberately does not fabricate them.

What the teacher says after entry

At 02:50 he emphasizes: entry stays entry; stop-loss stays stop-loss; target stays target. Make adjustments before entry if the analysis demands them. Do not repeatedly move a losing trade’s stop farther away and do not average into the loss to avoid being wrong. If your stop is hit, exit, note any mistake and move forward.

This instruction addresses discretionary, emotional alterations; the preplanned, rule-based weekly trailing method discussed earlier is a separate management decision and must not be confused with loosening an initial stop.

Do not confuse a red candle with a bad long idea

When you plan to buy at a lower demand zone, price may fall sharply on the way into that zone and selling pressure may look frightening. The teacher argues that the arrival itself should not be an emotional reason to abandon a previously justified location. However, the zone and trade must be reassessed and a protective stop remains mandatory; a zone is never guaranteed to hold.

Risk warning and execution realities

Any stop may suffer a worse fill during a gap, illiquidity or sharp move. Chart examples are not probability estimates. The transcript’s accessible portions emphasize predefined stop discipline but do not establish a specific fixed account-risk percentage for this episode.

CHAPTER 10 · Mindset · Source ~02:23–03:00; 03:01–03:59

Discipline, effort and the learning journey

The teacher stresses that finishing the course does not make someone an experienced trader. The next task is repeated chart study, written planning, controlled risk, and patience.

Four recurring habits

Practice deliberately: choose fewer charts and learn each one well. Be patient: an attractive higher-timeframe entry can take time to reach. Record decisions: log where a trade was found, why, and what happened. Accept invalidation: an honestly planned losing trade is preferable to ignoring your stop and turning it into a larger loss.

Teacher’s early brokerage anecdote

During the Q&A section the teacher describes beginning with speculative commodity trades and experiencing that apparent trading profits could disappear after frequent transactions and brokerage/taxes. The teaching point is that activity is not the same as net profitability and trading without a tested process can mislead beginners.

Beware using advanced ideas too early

He warns against turning his discussions of consolidations, likely breakouts, options or other forward-looking scenarios into easy entry signals the following day. Those judgments depend on an established top-down understanding developed by studying charts over time.

Course revision homework

At the closing 03:58–03:59 segment the teacher recommends repeatedly revisiting lessons about trading psychology, risk management, sector analysis, top-down approach, and when to be aggressive versus conservative. He finishes by saying discipline matters more than any trade score.

CHAPTER 11 · Practice · Source ~02:20–02:35

Reusable daily study and scanning routine

Use this sequence as a study routine drawn from the teacher’s workflow, not as an instruction to transact daily.

Before market / study session: open your curated watchlist and saved layouts; read the last notes before changing anything.

Stock scan: record higher-timeframe trends, the strongest relevant zones, possible QIT/MIT/WIT combinations and conflicting supply/demand. Skip messy stocks.

Setup quality: rate freshness, strength and base first; assess sector and EMA20 next; look at optional signals only after a credible core setup exists.

Conditional plan: write “If price reaches ___ and the relevant trend remains ___, I will evaluate ___.” Mark the stop, potential target and trailing case before any order.

Review: update annotations, retain the history and add a manageable number of new candidates; keep notes on invalidated ideas, not just winners.

Do not chase a quota

The lesson explicitly accepts carefully reviewing only two or a few stocks per day. The purpose is to build understanding rather than collect a high number of symbols or trades.

CHAPTER 12 · Revision · Source ~End of class

Recap, takeaways and what to verify

Episode 20 is a workflow lesson: quality location first → supported trend → matching execution → written plan → rule-based management → disciplined review.

Most important takeaways
  1. Use the higher-timeframe structure before interpreting a small chart pattern.
  2. Freshness (3), strength (2) and time at base (2) have the highest priority.
  3. Sector context and EMA20 can support a setup; optional patterns and indicators are secondary.
  4. 52-week-high scans find candidates; they do not establish all-time highs or trade entries.
  5. Where a strong trade progresses beyond all-time highs, the demonstrated trail follows newly formed weekly demand zones.
  6. Have a defined entry, stop and target; never loosen risk to avoid a planned exit.
  7. A small, updated watchlist with thoughtful notes beats random chart hopping.
  8. Discipline and repeated practice matter more than a perfect indicator checklist.
