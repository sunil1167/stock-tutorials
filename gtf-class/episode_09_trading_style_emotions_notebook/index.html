01

Three ways to enter a demand or supply zone

The tutor starts with familiar proximal/distal zone marking, then compares immediate, waiting-based, and departure-based entries. His later preference remains the strongest Type 1 setup.

First, know the chart vocabulary

Demand zone

An area from which price previously rose strongly. In the lesson's long-side examples, buyers are expected to react when price revisits the marked area.

Proximal vs distal

Proximal is the zone boundary nearer the current price; distal is the farther boundary. The tutor places a long-side Type 1 entry slightly above proximal, with stop-loss below distal plus a small buffer.

Base and leg-out

Base candles represent a pause or struggle. A strong departure (leg-out) and fewer base candles are part of the tutor's strong-zone discussion.

7/7, 6/7 or 5/7

The tutor's existing seven-point level-strength framework. This episode refers to the scores; the full scoring rubric comes from earlier lessons and is not fully restated here.

Type 1 — Immediate / set-and-forget entry

When considered: the marked level is exceptionally strong in the tutor's seven-point framework (illustrated by 7/7), with a fresh zone, strong departure and compact base. Set the planned entry just beyond proximal and stop beyond distal plus breathing room. The lesson describes this as entering when price arrives at the zone, without waiting for additional candle confirmation.

Advantage: you need not monitor a second candle or risk missing an immediate bounce. Limitation: the level may still fail; a 7/7 score is the tutor's heuristic, not a guarantee.

Teaching preference Despite explaining Types 2 and 3, the tutor repeatedly advises beginners to focus on 7/7 quality and Type 1, rather than building a complex discretionary entry system.

03:27–05:20, 20:14–20:32, 27:48–28:07, 01:04:29–01:04:42

Type 2 — Confirm the first candle and the next open

Context: a zone appears less impressive—e.g. the tutor's 6/7 or 5/7 examples with too many base candles or an imperfect departure. Instead of entering on arrival, watch what happens inside the zone.

  1. The first relevant candle should close within the zone.
  2. The next candle should open within the zone.
  3. Only then consider the planned entry; the stop stays beyond the original distal boundary, as before.

At the second candle's opening, price has shown it can remain in the area for at least that transition. Entering deeper inside the zone may shorten entry-to-stop distance and improve the potential reward/risk ratio, but it does not prove the zone will hold.

Missed-trade logic: if the next candle opens outside the zone, the specified opening condition is absent. The lesson discusses both gap-ups and gap-downs. In later Q&A, the tutor says a gap-up followed by a return may still be reconsidered if the target is not already achieved, but warns the reversal can indicate weak buyers; it is not an automatic entry.

08:28–12:42, 20:43–21:19, 02:30:58–02:31:22

Type 3 — Wait for departure from the zone

Context: another method to seek stronger evidence at a 5/7 or 6/7 quality level. A candle first opens and closes in the demand zone, and the next candle opens in it. Instead of buying immediately on that second open, wait for price to leave the zone upward through the proximal boundary.

The tutor explains that you do not have to wait until the second candle itself closes; the crossing/departure can trigger consideration. The original distal stop rule remains unchanged.

Advantage: the buyers have visibly pushed price out of the area. Trade-off: you pay a higher entry price for long positions and can miss an immediate move, experience false departures, or face less favorable reward/risk than a deep-zone entry.

Important nuance: the tutor also asks how quickly buyers get price out: a prolonged fight inside the zone may be less convincing than a prompt departure.

02

Level authenticity: original move or borrowed reaction?

A zone can appear powerful because an older level caused its price movement. The tutor calls such a dependent new zone “non-authentic”—but makes an important distinction between authenticity and tradeability.

The central distinction

Imagine price entering Demand Zone 1 and bouncing. While it bounces, trading-hour boundaries or base candles make a second apparent Demand Zone 2. If Zone 2 owes its existence to Zone 1's reaction, the tutor classifies Zone 2 as non-authentic. The force moving price may be the earlier pending buying, not an independent new source of demand.

However, “non-authentic” is not equivalent to “it can never work.” The tutor specifically explains that a non-authentic level can become tradable if its departure/closing demonstrates meaningful strength. Good broader market or sector support may also affect the overall decision. Avoid reducing this to a single binary label.

How to inspect authenticity step by step
  1. Mark the level on the timeframe you are actually analyzing.
  2. Look left or behind the formation for an older zone whose reaction may have generated the new base and departure.
  3. Ask whether the second zone's price movement would plausibly exist in the same form without that first zone's reaction. The tutor uses this reasoning to distinguish independent from reaction-derived zones.
  4. Judge the quality of the exit and closing from the candidate zone. Strong movement and a meaningful close can make a previously suspect, reaction-derived level worth considering.
  5. Finally, integrate the normal location, trend and execution rules; do not let the authenticity label replace the whole setup assessment.

34:29–37:37, 40:58–48:05, 52:06–52:47, 59:22–01:04:48

What authenticity does NOT mean

It does not mean that every non-authentic level must be ignored. It does not mean that every authentic level is guaranteed to survive. It does not overrule a poor trend, location, or closing. The lesson explicitly discusses a fine-looking level that can fail within a strong opposing trend. The tutor describes authenticity as useful context, not a separate exam you must pass before thinking about anything else.

35:05–37:37, 55:17–55:57, 01:01:43–01:04:48

Remember the two questions separately: “Was the level created independently?” and “Does the complete setup have sufficient quality to trade?” The answers can differ.

AIT and DIT — the role of Nifty, sector timing and execution

The tutor warns that zone quality alone may not rescue a fast trade. For early and daily-style trades, when a stock meets supply or demand, consider whether Nifty or its relevant sector is also reacting to a supportive area. He calls out Nifty's five- and ten-minute levels as especially important to timing fast styles. If the broader market moves against the direction, even a seemingly attractive level may quickly hit stop-loss.

The lesson gives a DIT illustration: inspect the daily area, judge trend on the 75-minute chart, then examine the 15-minute chart for an execution zone. He rejects charts that look like “garbage” despite nominally matching the timeframe template; the shape, displacement and quality matter.

Fast trades can fill partially or with slippage if price moves quickly. To improve familiarity and decision quality, the tutor suggests tracking fewer stocks rather than attempting to monitor a hundred simultaneous fast setups. A stock's recurring behavior becomes easier to recognize through repeated chart study.

01:06:11–01:08:55, 01:13:51–01:19:32, 01:29:03–01:30:21

WIT — the tutor's starting preference

The tutor recommends that beginning students observe the fast styles but emphasize actual practice with WIT and MIT. His reasoning is that weekly location plus daily trend and 75/125-minute execution offers a less noisy structural context than minute-by-minute fast entries.

The clear disadvantage is an overnight gap: the next open can jump across a level, on the favorable or unfavorable side. The tutor emphasizes that gaps are not always adverse, but they cannot be dismissed. He also observes that a strong WIT setup can reach its target quickly—even the same day—so “weekly” should not be read as a fixed holding time.

01:21:52–01:26:17

MIT — monthly location, weekly trend, daily execution

For MIT, the tutor combines monthly location, a more persistent weekly trend and daily execution. He characterizes its setups as powerful when those elements coincide, with comparatively larger structural moves but rarer entries.

He gives an anecdote that an Axis Bank MIT example reached a 4:1 target in approximately two days—illustrating why the style's name is not a deadline. It is an example, not evidence of any reproducible rate or promised return.

04

How to deal with trading emotions

The emotional section starts at about 01:36:20. Its recurring theme is to simplify decisions, pursue truly selective setups, resist excitement and keep executing the same tested process regardless of recent wins or losses.

1. Quality before quantity

The tutor describes a strong setup as one where location, closing, trend and execution align—and, when appropriate, additional levels coincide. His claim that concentrating on quality resolves a large portion of psychology is motivational teaching, not a measured 50% psychological effect.

He compares trading to visiting a showroom: after seeing many items, you can decide not to buy if nothing is good enough. Similarly, if none of the charts offers a suitable setup, waiting is a valid decision. An untraded day is not automatically a failed day.

Watchlist thought experiment: if you track 200 stocks and consider four styles, there are at most 800 stock–style combinations. This is not 800 real trade signals. Even if only one in four stocks offered a candidate, that could produce 50 candidates; the tutor says even 50 genuinely high-quality trades in a month is far more than necessary. The point is to become more selective, not to chase a quota.

01:36:20–01:44:55

Wait without panic

Not finding a setup is acceptable. Let quality dictate action, not discomfort with inactivity.

Reject actively

Look for reasons a candidate fails your rules instead of searching for excuses to take it.

Study repeatably

Track a manageable watchlist and learn the chart behavior of individual stocks.

2. Strong mindset ≠ never taking a stop-loss

The tutor's memorable example is this: if seven setups consecutively hit stop-loss, a disciplined trader should still be mentally prepared to assess and execute the eighth valid setup using the same predetermined method. The lesson is about emotional consistency, not doubling risk, ignoring evidence that a strategy may be broken, or guaranteeing the next trade wins.

He makes a related distinction between chart-reading knowledge and execution: theoretical technical analysis matters, but consistent, rule-driven execution is the difficult advantage. Treat stop-loss as part of a plan rather than proof of personal failure.

01:55:51–01:56:35, 01:59:42–02:04:00

3. Emotions come from outside the chart too

The tutor argues that a person's approach to fear, uncertainty, patience and risk is shaped by everyday experiences and childhood conditioning. He describes how seeing repeated accidents may increase a person's sense of danger, and contrasts how children react when adults handle similar events differently. His examples help explain why two traders may react very differently to the same price movement.

He also warns that group activity, attention and the craving for excitement can disrupt a previously stable process. When consistent chart analysis becomes boring, a trader may begin searching for impulsive activity. He says habits cannot transform in one day; the practical answer is to repeat a rule-based process.

Interpretation caution: these are the instructor's analogies about emotional learning, not clinical claims or scientific diagnoses.

02:04:05–02:10:34

4. Intelligence, academic background and luck

The tutor challenges the idea that market success belongs only to toppers, IIT graduates, scientists, or people from a specific educational stream. He also rejects luck as a useful substitute for preparation, using the image of two opposite opinions on the same chart—one bullish, the other bearish.

His positive alternative is continuous study of chart behavior, an open learning mindset, deep attention to market structure, and managing one's reactions. These are presented as personal beliefs from the class; they do not establish that luck or statistical variability has no real role in outcomes.

00:57–01:35, 01:57:42–02:04:00

5. Focus on practical charts, not constant strategy-hunting

Rather than buying more and more “best strategies” or “100 patterns” material, the tutor urges students to read price charts repeatedly and understand real market behavior. He mentions Trading in the Zone (Mark Douglas) and psychology-related books as possible supporting reading, but emphasizes that books alone do not create trading skill.

Socially, he prefers short, purposeful discussions about charts and discourages irrelevant group chats, prolonged arguments and the pressure to prove returns to others. Seeking public praise can shift attention away from research and execution.

01:32:37–01:36:14, 02:10:08–02:16:40, 02:25:14–02:25:50

6. Do not disguise a failed trade as a long-term investment

In the class Q&A, the tutor distinguishes a position intentionally chosen as an investment from a position taken for a specific trade. A trade has the rationale and stop-loss selected at entry. When that stop-loss is hit, changing the label to “investment” simply to avoid realizing a loss violates the original plan.

The lesson also discusses the danger of treating larger capital as the automatic solution to poor results. Focus on demonstrating discipline with limited money first. Some transcript passages discuss borrowing as a personal confidence example; this notebook does not recommend borrowing or leveraging to trade.

01:51:28–01:53:28, 02:21:48–02:22:31

A process-oriented trading mindset

  • “No setup” can be a successful process outcome.
  • The market owes no one a trade, a fixed income or a win after a losing streak.
  • Score the setup quality and consistency of execution, not today's excitement.
  • Keep trading decisions separate from public praise, disappointment and unrelated daily stress.
05

Risk management, stops and execution discipline

These principles appear throughout the lesson; numerical account-risk limits are not introduced in the available Episode 9 excerpts and therefore are not invented here.

Before an entry

  • Be clear which zone and timeframe control your idea.
  • Know the intended proximal entry condition and distal invalidation area.
  • Check the market/sector context, especially for AIT and DIT.
  • Do not enter purely to avoid missing a move.
  • Choose a position small enough that you can actually follow the stop.

After an entry

  • A fast move can lead to a missed or partial fill.
  • An overnight gap can cross a planned exit level.
  • Do not convert a losing trade into an unplanned long-term holding.
  • Do not assume any 7/7 or “authentic” level must work.
  • Review decisions based on rules and evidence, not revenge or fear.
High-stakes clarification: Do not interpret the instructor's optimism about consistency, capital growth or repeated trades as a guarantee. The source gives no defensible win rate, fixed monthly return, compulsory leverage, or safe borrowing formula for this lesson. Avoid borrowed-money trades and account-breaking exposure. Market gaps and slippage can produce losses beyond the intended stop.

07

Practice with four decision scenarios

Try answering before opening each explanation. These scenarios reinterpret the tutor's lessons; they are not historical trades or live market signals.

Scenario A · A 7/7 demand zone is revisited

Question: you have a well-defined, fresh zone with compact base and strong leg-out. Which entry approach did the tutor say to prioritize?

Answer: Type 1, the normal arrival/set-and-forget entry according to the established entry and distal-stop rules. Still check the overall context and risk. 7/7 is not certainty.

Scenario B · The next candle opens above a weak demand zone

Question: you were observing Type 2, but the second candle opens outside the zone above the proximal boundary. Is the original Type 2 opening condition fulfilled?

Answer: no. The planned second candle did not open inside the area; the method can miss trades. Later revisits should be assessed afresh rather than assumed valid.

Scenario C · A fresh-looking zone is actually a reaction to an older one

Question: does “non-authentic” always mean “untradable”?

Answer: no. The tutor distinguishes the origin of the move from whether the later closing and entire setup make the zone worth considering.

Scenario D · Several losses and no attractive trade today

Question: should you lower the quality standard to recover losses?

Answer: no. The lesson favors patience and consistent process. A streak of losses calls for review of real evidence and risk, not automatically a larger bet or a forced trade.

Quick revision & key takeaways

A condensed version of the lesson to read before chart practice.

Remember these 10 rules

  1. Type 1 = arrival; Type 2 = first candle closes in zone + second opens in zone; Type 3 = enter on departure.
  2. The tutor's favored beginner emphasis is on very high-quality Type 1 zones.
  3. For demand trades, the planned stop is beyond the distal boundary with room—not casually moved to fit emotion.
  4. “Non-authentic” describes a zone formed by reaction to another level; it does not automatically mean failure.
  5. Closing and overall trade conditions can restore the tradeability of a reaction-derived level.
  6. AIT/DIT require careful short-term timing with the market and sector.
  7. WIT/MIT use higher-timeframe context and are the tutor's suggested initial focus.
  8. Overnight gaps and fast fills introduce real execution risks.
  9. Quality beats trade count; the correct action may be to do nothing.
  10. A valid process should survive emotional swings, missed trades and prior stop-losses without arbitrary rule changes.
One-sentence summary: The instructor teaches how to discriminate between three entry triggers, understand why zones may be derived from earlier reactions, select trading style using multi-timeframe context, and protect emotional discipline by waiting for only well-aligned opportunities.
