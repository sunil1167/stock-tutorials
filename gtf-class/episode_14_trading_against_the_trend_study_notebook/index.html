01The big idea: trade against a falling trend, but with proof01:39–05:37 · Source checkpoints⌄

The lesson starts with a difficult situation: a stock is falling sharply, but its price is approaching a monthly or quarterly demand zone. The tutor considers such higher-timeframe demand areas meaningful, yet does not recommend blindly buying every touch.

There are two separate problems: (1) the higher-timeframe zone may span a very large price range, leading to a wide stop-loss; and (2) powerful selling pressure may overwhelm the zone. The chapter’s answer is a confirmation entry: keep the higher-timeframe zone as context, shift to a lower timeframe, and wait for evidence that buyers can overcome a nearby selling area.

Central decision rule“Price touched monthly demand” is a location, not the entire trade trigger. The tutor looks for a close above a relevant nearby supply zone before treating the fresh demand structure as tradable.

This is a way to seek a potential reversal from a strong decline, not a claim that a new uptrend is guaranteed. The tutor repeatedly distinguishes seeing a promising bottom from having an executable trade.

02Why not simply buy the higher-timeframe zone?02:00–05:30 · 12:04–13:14 · Source checkpoints⌄

In the opening example, a monthly demand zone appears on a stock chart such as Coal India. The tutor explains why demand identified on a monthly chart may have greater structural significance than a small daily area. However, a high timeframe does not make the zone infallible.

Problem A — wide zone

Placing an entry near the top of a broad monthly demand box and a stop below the entire box can create a large distance from entry to stop. Even when a distant target could produce 2:1 reward to risk, it may take substantial time or make a position uncomfortable to hold.

Problem B — force of the decline

If price has fallen a long way (the tutor invokes large corrections such as the COVID crash), several smaller demand zones may already have failed. A brief bounce at monthly demand does not prove the market can sustain a recovery.

So the exercise is not to predict the exact bottom; it is to improve both the evidence and the execution range. Mark the higher-timeframe zone first and plan the confirmation process before the price arrives.

Clarification: “Higher accuracy” and large reward-to-risk examples are the tutor’s qualitative expectations. The excerpts do not establish a tested probability or a guaranteed outcome.
03Top-down workflow: context first, execution later05:44–08:18 · 22:59–24:31 · Source checkpoints⌄

Step 1 — locate demand: identify a monthly, quarterly or even higher-timeframe demand zone. The tutor uses at least monthly as the higher-timeframe reference in this lesson.

Step 2 — move down to the daily chart: after price reaches the marked zone, inspect the individual daily candles rather than treating the whole monthly box as the entry range. The tutor also allows a weekly reference for additional confirmation.

Step 3 — find the nearest meaningful supply: identify the nearby daily supply zone that the recovery must defeat. Its distal boundary is the important confirmation threshold; merely revisiting or piercing the edge of supply is not enough.

Step 4 — wait for a close above supply: the tutor requires price to close beyond the distal line. A wick above it followed by a close back below is not that confirmation.

Step 5 — define execution: after confirmation, work with the new lower-timeframe demand area / bottom rather than automatically using the full monthly zone. Identify a stop below that new structure and the next important overhead supply area as the target reference.

Step 6 — reject bad timing: if the confirming break occurs only after a large rally, the tutor prefers to miss it rather than chase it.

Different from a simple two-candle reactionThe tutor expressly distinguishes this method from waiting for a candle to close in the zone and the next candle to leave it. Here the confirmation is tied to breaking and closing above nearby supply.
04Reading the zone vocabulary06:55–08:43 · 16:41–17:29 · Source checkpoints⌄
Demand zoneA chart area used by the tutor to locate possible buying interest and the origin of a move higher.
Supply zoneA chart area representing selling interest or a prior downward departure, which buyers may need to break.
Proximal lineThe nearer edge of a marked zone. A touch here is not the tutor’s confirmation condition.
Distal lineThe farther edge of the supply zone, used as the close-above threshold in the examples.
Leg-in / base / leg-outThe typical arrival, pause and departure used to explain a zone’s formation. The tutor also discusses valid immediate departures with no visible base.
Newly formed demandThe smaller execution structure created during or after the rebound from the higher-timeframe area.

On zones with a narrow base, single base or explosive candle, the tutor mentions wick-to-wick marking; in other setups he mentions body-to-wick. Exact candle selection requires the original chart, so the diagrams here show the principle instead of falsely reproducing a specific stock’s candle boundaries.

05The actual confirmation: break AND close07:26–08:43 · 22:54–23:37 · Source checkpoints⌄

Suppose price approaches a monthly demand zone while the daily sequence is still bearish. Do not enter merely because the proximal line of monthly demand is touched. After a rebound begins, find the first nearby daily supply zone and draw its distal line.

What counts? A daily candle that finishes above that distal line. This demonstrates more than a transient spike; in the tutor’s framework, buyers have carried price beyond an area where sellers previously dominated.

What does not count? A wick crossing the line while the body closes back below it; a touch of monthly demand without a supply break; or an apparent recovery followed by another drop without the required close.

DecisionDaily supply distal not closed above → no confirmation entry.
Daily supply distal closed above → inspect the new demand structure for execution.
06Entry, stop-loss and target placement08:18–13:14 · 45:20–46:53 · Source checkpoints⌄

Entry: after the supply break confirms the recovery, identify the new demand zone that drove that break (or its bottom when a base is absent). The tutor discusses placing the entry at that newly formed zone, rather than at the broad original monthly box. The lower-timeframe range may be wick-to-wick or body-to-wick depending on the candle/base structure discussed in the lesson.

Stop-loss: place it beneath the new identified demand area / relevant bottom. Moving the reference from the monthly zone to a smaller newly formed area can greatly reduce the distance between entry and stop. If the smaller structure fails, the planned stop-loss applies; the lesson does not authorize ignoring it because the monthly thesis seems strong.

Target: keep an eye on the important overhead supply zone. In the early diagram the tutor discusses a strong weekly or monthly supply zone as a target-booking region. In examples, he also inspects the location of meaningful prior corrections and rejects random clutter (“garbage”) as equivalent to a clean high-quality supply zone.

Missed entry: a return to the new area can sometimes provide another entry; however, confirmation does not mean price must revisit it, and the tutor explicitly discourages entries far up the rally simply because confirmation eventually appeared.

R:R note: Examples of 2:1, about 7.64:1, and about 20:1 appear in retrieved passages. They are illustrations from the tutor’s historical chart discussion, not prospective returns or assurances.
07Departure scenario A: a new base and strong lift-off13:14–15:38 · 24:03–24:31 · Source checkpoints⌄

One straightforward shape is: price arrives inside the higher-timeframe demand area, briefly pauses on the daily timeframe, forms a small base / demand zone, and then moves upward. The breakout candle closes beyond the distal boundary of the nearest supply zone. The newly formed demand structure gives a more precise location for entry and stop-loss.

The teacher emphasizes that this is only one possible drawing: arrival may be a continuous fall, a zigzag, or another structure. Do not require every trade to recreate the idealized chart exactly. The universal condition in these passages is the meaningful supply-zone closing break.

08Departure scenario B: immediate recovery, no base candle15:45–18:59 · Source checkpoints⌄

A valid response need not produce a neat “leg-in → base → leg-out” sequence. The tutor describes price reaching the marked demand, then leaving immediately, perhaps with one strong bullish candle or a gap, without spending another day in a base.

His explanation is behavioral: if demand is truly strong, buyers may overwhelm nearby supply so quickly that there is no prolonged pause. He allows marking the bottom / immediate-departure region for the execution structure, provided the required supply-break confirmation occurs.

This is not permission to buy every green candle. The close above the nearby supply distal line remains the decision gate; the absence of a base changes how the new area is defined, not whether evidence is required.

09Arrival scenario C: no nearby supply zone exists18:59–24:31 · Source checkpoints⌄

Sometimes price falls into higher-timeframe demand in a largely continuous decline, leaving no clear nearby daily supply zone to use as a confirmation barrier. The tutor says this is a practical problem: waiting to break a very distant supply zone might mean the rally has already happened.

He proposes watching the initial recovery. If price rises and makes a correction / pullback, that correction may create a new local supply area. The subsequent move above it, with a close, becomes the confirmation reference. The daily demand structure associated with that move can then be examined for an entry.

Notice the difference: the trader does not invent a supply line arbitrarily. The trader waits for price action to reveal the reference. If no useful reference and no appropriate entry develop, it is acceptable to have no trade.

10How this improves on conventional reversal detection36:01–40:30 · 52:15–52:56 · Source checkpoints⌄

The tutor compares his approach with conventional “higher highs and higher lows” trend-change thinking. A traditional observer may wait for successive prior highs to be taken out before declaring an uptrend. This lesson argues that a trader who has already identified a powerful monthly or quarterly demand location may seek an earlier, rule-defined entry after the first meaningful supply break rather than waiting for two separate structural high breaks.

Crucial nuance: the earlier entry is not simply a prediction. It depends on the higher-timeframe location plus the actual lower-timeframe confirmation and execution zone. The tutor ties this lesson back to the previous “advanced trend analysis” class and references continuation patterns and different intermediate timeframes.

Boundaries: The excerpts mention “WIT,” “MIT,” intermediate timeframes, advanced trend and golden crossover from earlier lessons. Their complete definitions and all criteria cannot be reconstructed reliably from the accessible Episode 14 excerpts, so those are not invented here.
11Case study: Coal India and a distant monthly demand zone01:47–05:30 · Source checkpoints⌄

Coal India is introduced at the start to illustrate the tension between a prominent monthly demand zone and a strongly falling price. The teacher asks whether a monthly demand zone should be traded simply because it is normally considered strong.

The teaching point is the two-part reason to wait: a long fall may have broken multiple preceding demand areas; and the monthly zone may make the stop-loss too wide. A trader wanting to reduce both uncertainties can watch the daily reaction and look for confirmation before choosing a narrower execution zone.

Do not misread this case: the accessible transcript passages do not provide a complete dated Coal India OHLC series or the final historical outcome, so this notebook does not fabricate price levels, a return percentage or a successful trade for that example.

13Other context: multi-timeframe support and chart clutter1:02:55–1:10:52 · 1:12:43–1:41:04 · Source checkpoints⌄

Several chart walkthroughs distinguish a clean structural area from a congested set of candles the tutor calls “garbage.” He prefers judging meaningful supply by the actual selling pressure or major correction that originated there, rather than marking every minor sideways candle as a decisive obstacle.

He also discusses yearly, half-yearly, quarterly and monthly demand areas supporting a bullish thesis, and a monthly demand zone associated with a golden crossover. The take-away for this episode is that multiple supporting timeframes can increase the tutor’s confidence, but the accessible passages do not provide a formal weighted scoring equation.

The same passage also shows how multiple timeframes and strong narrative conviction can tempt an experienced trader to anticipate a breakout before the formal confirmation. The teacher distinguishes his experienced discretionary behavior from what beginners should attempt.

14Risk management, skipped trades and option warnings12:04–13:14 · 36:01–36:34 · 1:01:08–1:01:44 · Source checkpoints⌄

Risk is defined by the planned entry and stop, not by optimism about the zone. The narrower confirmed structure may offer better reward-to-risk geometry, but price can still reverse and trigger the stop. A large historical R multiple is not a reason to increase risk without a separate position-sizing plan.

A missed trade is not a failure. The tutor explicitly says there is no guarantee that the desired entry will form. If price runs away before a sound execution opportunity, let it go. A separate setup may appear in a nearby area or another stock; chasing after a fully extended rally defeats the purpose of this method.

Option premiums can go to zero. The tutor describes how buying an option in anticipation of an immediate recovery can fail when price stays stagnant or falls further. He expressly tells learners in this part of the course not to buy options yet, and to focus on equity cash until they have acquired more relevant knowledge.

Beginner guardrail from the tutorNo trade without the applicable setup and its confirmation. Do not replace disciplined execution with the hope that price “has to bounce.”
15The strongest warning: expectation-based trading1:51:48–1:54:26 · 1:59:13–2:01:45 · Source checkpoints⌄

The tutor separates rule-based trades from expectation-based trades. An expectation-based trade is taken because the trader feels the breakout or reversal is imminent, before the rule-defined trigger is present. This is much more discretionary and demands personal risk judgment.

His explicit instruction for students: do not take even a single expectation-based trade for the next one year. Learn rule-based trading first. He references a prior exercise involving 100 trades and earlier lessons on MIT/WIT, top-down analysis, and when to be conservative or aggressive.

He describes experienced discretion around a repeatedly tested level, narrowing EMA-20 squeeze and support from several timeframes. Importantly, this is not a beginner signal. The repeated question throughout the lesson is whether the trader has actually seen the required close or is merely expecting to see it.

He encourages tracking stock research over time, reviewing examples and improving observation rather than jumping directly to speculative current-market-price entries.

Lesson summary

A large higher-timeframe demand area can be interesting even during a strong decline, but it does not make a counter-trend buy automatically safe. The tutor’s principal tool is confirmation entry: identify a monthly/quarterly area, inspect the daily chart when price arrives, wait for a meaningful nearby supply zone to be broken and closed above, then evaluate a smaller newly formed demand/bottom for entry and stop-loss. The overhead meaningful supply is the target reference. If no supply exists, use a genuine corrective structure that subsequently forms; if no execution appears, skip the trade.

The strongest behavioral lesson is to learn rule-based confirmation before attempting to trade an expected reversal. The instructor specifically discourages beginner expectation-based entries and options trading.

Seven rules to remember

✓Location ≠ trigger: a monthly demand touch alone is insufficient.
✓For the counter-trend confirmation discussed here, the lower-timeframe supply distal line must be closed above.
✓Prefer executing around the new smaller structure rather than risking the whole wide monthly box.
✓Immediate departure without a base is possible; it does not remove the closing-break rule.
✓No nearby supply? Observe a real correction that creates a new reference; never manufacture a level.
✓Avoid chasing, respect stops, do not treat attractive historical R multiples as guaranteed.
✓For beginners: rule-based equity-cash setups first; the tutor strongly warns against speculative entries and options.
