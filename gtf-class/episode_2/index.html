Chapter 01 · ~04:52–06:47

The language of candlesticks

Every candle summarizes prices over one chosen period. The four reference prices are Open, High, Low, Close (OHLC). When close exceeds open, the body is usually green; when close is below open, it is red. The body shows the open–close difference, while the upper and lower wicks show price excursions beyond the body.

Body = |Close − Open|   ·   Range = High − Low
Upper wick = High − max(Open, Close)
Lower wick = min(Open, Close) − Low

Key distinction: candle color vs candle size

Two green candles need not represent equally large moves; compare their open–close distances and their wicks. The same applies to red candles. A single candle does not reveal an exact count of buyers or sellers.

Chapter 02 · ~06:53–20:48

Demand, supply, and order matching

The tutor compares shares with masks and sanitizers during COVID: demand jumped while supply was limited, prices rose; more supply and reduced demand later relieved that pressure. He applies this mental model to stock orders.

Every executed trade has both a buyer and a seller. If someone wants 1,000 shares at one price but only 500 shares are offered there, that order may partially fill. The remaining 500 might wait or execute only if more sellers arrive at the limit price. A buyer willing to accept higher offers can move execution to higher price levels.

Demand stronger at quoted pricesUpward pressure

Aggressive buying consumes available offers; buyers may pay higher levels.

Supply stronger at quoted pricesDownward pressure

Aggressive selling consumes available bids; sellers may accept lower levels.

Accurate interpretation: The tutor's “1 lakh buyers vs 50,000 sellers” and “10 lakh vs 5 lakh” are hypothetical order-size illustrations, not evidence that a traded candle literally contains more buyer trades than seller trades. Executed buy and sell quantity match by definition; unmatched interest and order aggressiveness explain the price-pressure intuition.
Chapter 03 · ~20:48–27:38

Exciting candles: directional movement

The tutor defines an exciting candle as one where the real body exceeds half of the entire candle range. Since the range consists of the body plus both wicks, this is equivalent to body being larger than the two wicks combined.

Exciting: Body / Range > 0.50
Equivalently: Body > Upper wick + Lower wick

In his interpretation, a long green exciting candle signals comparatively strong buying pressure, while a long red exciting candle signals strong selling pressure. The candle's direction comes from open versus close; its relative body dominance distinguishes exciting from base.

Does “strong exciting” mean a guaranteed continuation?

No. The episode does not promise that every strong candle continues, nor does it give a standalone buy/sell trigger for one exciting candle. Context and subsequent action matter.

Chapter 04 · ~27:38–38:48

Base candles: a struggle without a clear winner

The opposite of an exciting candle is the tutor's base candle: its body occupies less than half the full high-to-low range. The combined wicks are longer than the body. It often reflects back-and-forth buying and selling with little net movement from open to close.

Base: Body / Range < 0.50
Equivalently: Body < Upper wick + Lower wick

A near-zero-body base is doji-like. The transcript emphasizes that long wicks can coexist with an open and close almost unchanged, because both sides challenged price during the interval.

What about exactly 50%?

The lecture defines exciting as greater than 50% and base as less than 50%. It does not formally classify the equality case. Treat exactly 50% as a boundary case rather than inventing a strict rule.

Chapter 05 · ~38:48–49:43

Practice: classify, then check the actual move

The tutor quizzes students with chart examples. Some candles are obvious. Others have bodies close to 49–51% of range, or are tiny in the context of neighboring candles. He warns that a mechanical indicator may label a candle “exciting” even though the price achieved almost no meaningful displacement.

Interactive OHLC practice · examples use invented prices

Two levels of judgment: (1) the formal percentage rule; (2) the tutor's practical judgment about whether the candle made a meaningful move compared with the wider chart. The transcript gives no numerical threshold for that second judgment; do not automate it as a fixed rule.
Chapter 06 · ~49:51–58:55

How a base turns into a demand or supply zone

The tutor connects two observations: a base represents a temporary fight, then a forceful departure indicates one side gained control. The high-to-low region of the base becomes the initial zone he studies.

  1. Identify the base: one or more candles showing modest net movement.
  2. Observe the result: a strong green departure (demand interpretation) or red departure (supply interpretation).
  3. Mark the origin: the base candle high/low region is the initial area discussed in the lesson.
  4. Watch later price action: a return to this area is a retest; the tutor expects a chance of a reaction, not certainty.
Tutor's central model: base = disagreement; departure = one side temporarily wins; retest = test whether enough interest remains.
Chapter 07 · ~59:00–80:00

Why a zone may react — or break

The tutor argues that strong departures can indicate possible remaining orders around the original base. When price revisits a demand zone, renewed buying might lift it; on a supply-zone retest, renewed selling might pull it lower. He repeatedly qualifies this: nobody can prevent another large participant from placing an opposing order.

Potential demand retest

Buy interest absorbs selling

If buyers are sufficient at the revisit, price may bounce upward.
Demand-zone failure

Selling overwhelms demand

If selling is stronger than willing buying, the area can be breached.
Potential supply retest

Sell interest absorbs buying

If sellers are sufficient at the revisit, price may turn down.
Supply-zone failure

Buying overwhelms supply

If aggressive buying exceeds available offers, price can push through.
Illustrative quantities used by the tutor

He compares zones imagined to contain different amounts of pending buying and tests them against hypothetical incoming selling quantities. The intuitive lesson is that more opposing pressure can use up even a seemingly large pool of interest. These quantities are thought experiments; they are not observable from a candle and do not calculate a reliable probability.

Does a dramatic departure prove a pending institutional order?

No. The tutor uses strong moves as a footprint suggesting institutional participation and remaining orders. The transcript does not provide order-level proof of the participant's identity, exact order size, or the survival of those orders at a later date. Treat this as the tutor's trading hypothesis, not verified transaction data.

The lecturer says a higher-timeframe candle may contain smaller areas of consolidation visible only after switching to a lower timeframe (for example daily to hourly or 15-minute). The exact multi-timeframe workflow is left for later teaching.

Chapter 08 · ~81:56–124:59

Chart discussion, news, and investor psychology

The class includes chart demonstrations involving State Bank of India (SBIN) and Ashok Leyland. The transcript describes finding base areas, observing strong departures and considering later reactions, but the attached text does not include the original video chart screenshots or underlying OHLC data. Therefore the illustrations here teach the logic rather than pretending to reproduce those historical chart candles.

The tutor strongly criticizes the common behavior of buying after a dramatic rally because of positive headlines, then selling after a sharp fall because of negative headlines. He contrasts this with everyday purchases, where people usually look for a discount rather than rush to buy at a higher price.

Behavioral exercise: Before reacting to a headline, write down what the chart says, where your proposed trade would be invalidated, and what information is actually known. This is a study exercise, not a trade signal.
What does the tutor say about news and market participants?

He recommends avoiding trading decisions driven by television headlines, social-media messages, and hype. He suggests large participants may exploit emotional reactions. These are his interpretations, not verified facts about any specific news item or person. The reliable learning point is to avoid impulsive decisions and to demand independent analysis.

What about trading zones immediately when they first form?

The tutor explains that an area with a base but without a clear directional result lacks a confirmed winner. He stresses that, as a trader, a plan needs an entry, stop loss, and target; at that stage these remain unclear. He therefore discourages arbitrary trading while the “fight” is unresolved.

Chapter 09 · ~125:13–131:00

Relative strength of a zone

Late in the session, the tutor focuses on the strength of departure from a base. His general comparison is: a stronger directional move away from a zone suggests stronger pressure and, in his model, a greater chance of reaction at a future retest. Multiple strong candles can reinforce that visual interpretation.

For a supply-zone analogue, compare how forcefully prices decline after a base. The lecturer contrasts normal, strong and very strong selling departures. A zone can still fail regardless of its appearance.

Do not treat the figure as a win-rate model. The transcript provides neither a validated accuracy percentage nor a numerical test for zone quality.
Chapter 10 · ~108:00–149:09

Risk, probability, and limits of this lesson

“We cannot stop anybody from buying or selling” is the tutor's recurring principle. He says even a strong-looking setup is not guaranteed: a new participant's large order can overpower the expected pressure. His driving analogy is that skill can reduce accident risk but cannot promise a life with no accidents.

Rule 01

No guaranteed bounce

Never assume a zone must hold.
Rule 02

A plan needs three points

Entry, stop loss and target — the tutor defers exact methods.
Rule 03

Wait for a clear setup

Do not improvise a trade from a base without clarity.
Rule 04

Use evidence, not hype

Review price behavior instead of chasing emotional headlines.

What is deliberately not specified in Episode 02

  • Exact entry price inside a zone; precise stop-loss and profit-target formulas.
  • Position sizing, risk percentage, or a validated reward-to-risk requirement.
  • Definitive timeframe selection for intraday vs swing or investment positions.
  • Complete rules for multiple bases, zone freshness, invalidation or ranking.
  • Verified institutional order volumes or guaranteed success rates.

One-page recap

The class in ten sentences

  1. A candlestick expresses the open, high, low and close of a period.
  2. Green means close above open; red means close below open.
  3. Trading occurs only when a buyer and seller match.
  4. Pressure to buy through limited offers can lift prices; aggressive selling through limited bids can lower them.
  5. The tutor calls a candle exciting when body / range is greater than 50%.
  6. He calls it a base when body / range is less than 50%.
  7. Small net movement and long wicks suggest a contested period, though the actual intraperiod path is unknown.
  8. A base followed by a strong green departure suggests a possible demand zone; a strong red departure suggests a possible supply zone.
  9. A retest may react or fail because later buying and selling are not controlled by the chart pattern.
  10. Trade planning needs defined entry, stop loss, target and realistic expectations; this episode does not yet teach the precise placement rules.
Remember: Observe the candle → locate the base → see the departure → consider a potential retest → acknowledge the possibility of failure. Observe first; no automatic trade follows from this sequence.
