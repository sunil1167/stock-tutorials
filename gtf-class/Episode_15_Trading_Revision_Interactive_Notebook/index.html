02FOUNDATION · 53:44–1:02:52Candles tell the story of buyers and sellers⌄

The first class revisited the green and red candle, then used opening, closing, high and low to explain what participants accomplished during that period.

01Green versus red

A bullish/green candle closes above its open; a bearish/red candle closes below it. The body shows open-to-close change while upper and lower wicks show the price extremes reached.

02The body matters

The tutor contrasts candles with meaningful open–close separation against candles whose opening and closing prices are almost equal. He calls the latter doji-like/base behaviour and uses it to discuss temporary balance or conflict.

03Candles are the visible result

The class treats each candle as a record of transactions and changing buying/selling pressure. The instructor prefers analysing that record to trusting untested market predictions or television commentary.

04What one candle cannot prove

A base candle means that, at that point, the contest is not resolved in an obvious direction. The tutor explicitly says you must wait: the next move can be up or down.

05Working interpretation

A forceful candle moving away from a base is used as evidence that one side won that local contest. The instruction is to inspect what happened next—not assume a single candle guarantees a profitable reversal.

✳
Rule to remember

Do not forecast the direction of the next move from a base candle alone.

FROM THE CLASS

Around 1:02:26 the tutor says a base can be followed by either a rise or a fall; only after the departure can the observer describe which side appeared stronger.

PRECISION NOTE

The tutor uses a “pending orders” explanation for why price leaves a base. Treat that as his conceptual framework, not proof that hidden order size is observable from a candle alone.

03FOUNDATION · 59:46–1:06:22Demand and supply: the idea behind price movement⌄

The central teaching device is an imbalance between buying and selling interest. The zone is the area from which the imbalance appears to send price decisively away.

01Demand

A demand zone is a candidate area from which buyers previously pushed price upward. When price returns, the trader watches whether that historical interest is still evident.

02Supply

A supply zone is a candidate area from which sellers previously pushed price downward. When price returns, it may behave as resistance, but it can also be crossed.

03Why a zone, not a magic price

Candles have bodies and wicks, and prices can overshoot a line. The method therefore marks a band with two boundaries, not a perfect single reversal point.

04Absorption / balance

While a base forms, neither side has clearly established control. A decisive leg out gives more information about which group prevailed.

05Respect, testing, failure

“Respected” means price reacted at the area. “Tested” means the area has been revisited. Neither label establishes that a future visit must give the same result.

✳
Rule to remember

Use price action to judge whether a zone was respected; never treat its historical reaction as a certainty.

FROM THE CLASS

The tutor refers to everyday examples, SBI and real-estate buying/selling while explaining demand, supply and ways traders can be influenced by news (~1:01:23).

04FOUNDATION · 1:03:42–1:07:52The four foundational price-action patterns⌄

Every zone setup is assembled from a leg in, a base, and a leg out. The tutor names the candles with two simple verbs: rally (up) and drop (down).

01DBR — Drop · Base · Rally

Price arrives while falling, pauses in a base, then rises. The class groups this with the demand reversal ideas.

02RBR — Rally · Base · Rally

Price rises into a base and rises again. This is a demand continuation type.

03RBD — Rally · Base · Drop

Price arrives while rising, pauses, then falls. This is a supply reversal type.

04DBD — Drop · Base · Drop

Price falls into a base and falls again. This is a supply continuation type.

05Candle colours

In this teaching framework, a demand zone has a bullish/green departure; a supply zone has a bearish/red departure. The leg-in colour is not fixed by the zone side.

06Strength of departure

The tutor wants leg-in and leg-out candles to be exciting, with particular emphasis on a fast, explosive leg out. A clear departure provides stronger visual evidence than a weak shuffle out of a base.

05FOUNDATION · 1:09:38–1:14:05How to mark a zone: proximal, distal, freshness⌄

This is the core drawing procedure recovered from the revision: find a convincing three-part departure, bound the base, then check whether the zone has been used.

01Find the origin

The tutor repeatedly uses “look left and down” while locating the earlier structure responsible for the outgoing move. Do not arbitrarily highlight the newest candle just because its colour looks appealing.

02Demand-zone proximal line

The near boundary is defined as the highest body of the base candles. It is the side first approached by returning price from above.

03Demand-zone distal line

The far boundary is defined as the lowest wick of the base candles. The stop is positioned beyond this side, with room rather than exactly on the line.

04Supply-zone proximal line

For supply, the near boundary is the lowest body of the base; the far boundary is the highest wick.

05Fresh versus tested

After the first leg-out candle, inspect whether price returned to the proximal boundary and reacted. The tutor calls a zone fresh if price has not yet returned to use it. A previously visited-and-reacted zone is not treated as fresh.

06Quality above quantity

Minimum one leg in, one base, one leg out; there may be more candles in each part. A short basing period and pronounced leg out are preferred, but context also matters.

06FOUNDATION · 1:15:37–1:18:32Exceptional zone marking and origin pressure⌄

The tutor adds an exception to simple base-only boundaries when a prominent leg-in or leg-out wick extends beyond the base and appears important to the original imbalance.

01Why an exception is needed

A first pass at zone marking can ignore a wick that reveals an earlier forceful reaction. The tutor describes such a wick as a potentially important “strongest player” when the market returns under pressure.

02Reversal patterns

For reversal structures, the class discusses considering an exceptional extension from either the leg in or leg out where it fits the demonstrated setup.

03Continuation patterns

For continuation structures, the tutor says the exceptional consideration is associated with the leg out rather than both sides.

04Practical consequence

The far boundary and hence the stop may move further away. That increases the per-share risk and reduces the position size one can take at a fixed risk budget.

05Restraint

The exact extreme is identified by looking at the chart. Do not widen every zone by default: the exception is meant to preserve the relevant origin of pressure.

✳
Rule to remember

Exceptional marking changes the protected zone. Recompute the entry–stop distance and target; do not keep the old risk calculation.

07FOUNDATION · 1:07:52–1:14:05 · 1:36:19–1:42:59Strong-zone checklist: departure, base, and context⌄

A zone is not “best” because a label appears. The tutor layers several tests over the raw pattern to reduce low-quality entries.

01Explosive leg out

Prefer a clear, quick departure that visibly changes the earlier flow. The tutor uses the analogy of two competitors: a decisive result provides stronger evidence than a long unresolved struggle.

02Few base candles

A compact base followed by a decisive exit is repeatedly used as a favourable sign. He contrasts this with many small/base candles that make a structure messy on the chosen timeframe.

03Freshness

Previously filled or repeatedly tested zones are distinguished from untouched zones. The tutor warns that returns can consume interest.

04Actual accomplishment

A candle can technically satisfy an “exciting” formula yet do little in context. The instructor rejects such weak-looking zones despite the checkbox.

05Trade score

A numerical score out of seven is discussed, using attributes including candle excitement, base count and freshness. Scores such as 6/7 and 7/7 appear in the revision.

06What cannot be reconstructed

The searchable passages do not establish every one of the seven criteria or their precise weighting. They are intentionally not fabricated here.

✳
Rule to remember

Prefer stronger, cleaner, fresher evidence and remain willing to reject an attractive-looking but weakly structured zone.

08TRADE CONSTRUCTION · 1:14:05–1:15:37 · 1:43:23–1:45:17Entry, stop loss, target, and the 2:1 requirement⌄

Once the zone is established, the lesson moves to mechanical placement: where an order would enter, what would invalidate it, and how far the planned reward must extend.

01Entry

For a demand-zone set-and-forget trade, the tutor discusses an entry at the proximal line, at times with a small allowance to improve execution rather than chasing far above it.

02Stop loss

The stop goes below the demand distal line (above the supply distal for a short), with some gap. The bus/U-turn analogy explains why price can slightly overshoot an area before changing direction.

03Reward–risk

The tutor repeatedly uses a planned reward of at least 2× the risk. If entry-to-stop distance is R, the basic objective is 2R.

04Structural target

He later refines the rule: find an appropriate intermediate-timeframe opposing zone where the target can sensibly be placed. Do not claim 2R is available if price may meet an obstacle first.

05Measure after marking

If the zone’s distal boundary changes through exceptional marking, the stop and the reward distance must be recalculated before any execution.

06What the source does not specify

The accessible passages do not fully recover a single universal position-size percentage or an exhaustive gap formula. Neither is introduced as the teacher’s rule.

09TRADE CONSTRUCTION · 1:30:10–1:37:26 · 1:40:40–1:43:05Three-timeframe architecture: location → trend → execution⌄

The tutor’s main framework is a coordinated three-timeframe analysis. It stops an appealing candle on the smallest chart from overriding the larger market context.

01Location / higher timeframe

Locate price inside the larger demand–supply landscape (“the curve”). It answers whether the market is near meaningful buying or selling pressure and establishes the general side worth considering.

02Trend / intermediate timeframe

One timeframe lower, decide whether the prevailing direction agrees with the intended long or short. The lesson cautions against routine long entries when the intermediate trend is down.

03Execution / lower timeframe

A further step lower, find a high-quality local zone and mark the actual entry and stop. This gives a tighter area within the wider higher-timeframe location.

04Coinciding structure

The tutor favours cases where higher-timeframe location, intermediate trend and execution-zone closing all support one another. He calls the nesting or alignment properly coinciding.

05Why three?

A large location zone can be wide. Moving to a lower execution chart can identify a more specific area inside it rather than risking the entire larger range.

06Choosing direction

The larger context informs whether to look for buying or selling. The intermediate trend adds confirmation; the lower chart should not overrule the rest by itself.

11TRADE CONSTRUCTION · 1:31:08–1:36:19 · 1:40:24–1:42:59Curve location and trading with the prevailing trend⌄

The larger chart’s nearest meaningful demand and supply areas form a range or “curve.” Where price sits within that range helps judge whether buying or selling is sensible.

01Mark nearby opposing zones

The tutor marks nearest fresh demand and supply on the higher timeframe to see where current price sits between them.

02Low / high in the curve

A buying candidate near the higher-timeframe demand end has location support. A buying candidate high in the curve, near supply, faces a major obstacle.

03Trend confirmation

If the intermediate chart is trending up, a long from a supportive location is stronger than a long against the trend. Downtrend buying is treated with caution.

04Very low exceptions

The teacher says buying when trend is down is risky even low in the curve, but allows that an exceptionally strong very-low location may justify special consideration.

05Execution alignment

Within the selected location, compare possible lower-timeframe zones and look at quality and closing, not just the closest pattern.

06Question to ask

Is the planned trade supported by the higher-timeframe location or is it running straight into an area where the other side previously dominated?

✳
Rule to remember

A good-looking lower-timeframe entry near opposing higher-timeframe supply may be a trade to reject.

FROM THE CLASS

14TRADE DISCIPLINE · 15:10–16:14 · 1:42:39–1:43:05 · 1:49:20–1:59:22When to be aggressive—and when to skip⌄

The instructor says that avoiding a low-quality or badly located trade can be a more valuable skill than creating more signals.

01Demand repeatedly respected

If demand zones keep holding and price moves gradually upward, you might feel that supply must eventually break. The class warns not to trade that feeling alone.

02The opposing location matters

Do not ignore an already reached higher-timeframe supply zone simply because the lower timeframe produces a tempting demand entry.

03Aggressive entry conditions

The revision permits more aggressive participation when other important checks align—good zone, strong closing, appropriate trend, favourable location, and no crucial opposing supply already hit.

04Conservative choice

When several factors are poor or unclear, the decision can simply be to pass, rather than compromise on a trade.

05Quality mindset

The tutor proposes trying to reject each candidate until it proves good enough; looking for reasons to accept everything creates quantity rather than quality.

06Accuracy example

He uses a hypothetical 10-trade example to show how removing three weak trades that would lose improves the observed win ratio. This is pedagogical arithmetic, not evidence that a filter can identify future losers.

✳
Rule to remember

Reject a trade that fails meaningful context checks. Missing a trade is not the same as losing money.

FROM THE CLASS

A WIT on Ashok Leyland is used to illustrate how arriving at monthly supply changes the decision even if the lower entry appears attractive (~1:50:01–1:50:19).

15TRADE DISCIPLINE · 1:59:42–2:15:35Moving averages and advanced trend analysis⌄

The instructor briefly reviews why moving averages can react slowly and how his later, zone-based advanced trend analysis is intended to make earlier structural judgments.

01Lagging indicator

A moving average represents a history of prices. Its slope may still point down while price has already begun to rise, or vice versa.

02EMA use

Exponential moving averages and their golden/death crossovers are mentioned as an additional trade-score consideration, not a standalone reason to enter.

03Location-filtered crossover

The stated rule is to consider a golden crossover for buying at a demand zone and a death crossover for selling at a supply zone; the tutor rejects conventional crossover-only buying and trailing.

04Advanced structural reading

Rather than waiting for an average to turn, he examines what demand or supply zones price would have to break to reach a proposed entry. That structural path can reveal the likely trend context earlier.

05Practice and fallback

He describes basic moving-average trend checks as a useful temporary confirmation while learners are still building proficiency with advanced trend analysis.

06Scope limit

The exact EMA periods, full advanced-trend algorithm and the complete special trailing rule were not fully accessible; they are not supplied as invented rules.

✳
Rule to remember

Use a crossover as supporting context only, at the relevant zone—not an independent buy/sell switch.

17APPLICATION · Synthesis of the classOne-page rule-based workflow + final recap⌄

This checklist combines only the sequence and checks supported by the recovered lesson. Use it to revise; it does not replace the original class’s detailed entry or scoring tables.

011. Choose your style

Decide whether you are evaluating MIT, WIT or another explicitly understood timeframe set. For beginners, the tutor recommends starting with MIT and WIT.

022. Locate the curve

Mark nearby higher-timeframe demand and supply. Ask which side has structural support, whether current price is high or low in that range and where the opposing obstacle lies.

033. Verify intermediate trend

Find whether price is structurally moving in the intended trade direction; flag countertrend exceptions rather than treating them as routine.

044. Find lower-timeframe execution

Look for a leg-in → base → decisive leg-out, mark proximal/distal, identify fresh/tested status and assess quality.

055. Check confluence

Review candle closing, the score’s available components, and whether execution actually coincides with the larger location rather than floating far away from it.

066. Define the plan

Choose the supported entry style; place stop beyond the distal boundary with appropriate room; compare the proposed target with the intermediate-timeframe opposing zone and check whether 2R is realistically available.

077. Reject weak candidates

Skip if the location is poor, the overhead obstacle is too close, the trend conflicts without a recognised exception, or the zone is not convincing.

088. Follow defined exits

Do not override the stop or prematurely close at random just because the price flickers. Review the results without assuming a short sample proves an edge.

✳
Rule to remember

The point of the revision is a decision sequence—not a collection of isolated “buy here” tricks.

FROM THE CLASS

Final synthesis: location → trend → execution quality → entry/stop → realistic target → disciplined follow-through.

Class summary & key takeaways

The simplest way to remember the tutor’s progression.

Class summary. The episode reviews the path from a single candle to a complete trade. Candles show the result of the buying/selling contest. A compelling leg-in, base and leg-out defines a potential demand/supply zone. The trader then checks freshness, quality, higher-timeframe location, intermediate trend, realistic reward and downside before choosing whether to participate. The strongest idea is often to reject a trade when one important layer does not agree.

01 · Read evidence, not predictions

The instructor rejects trading because a level “feels” ready to break.

02 · Demand/supply are areas

Mark the base with proximal and distal lines; inspect the reaction, not only the price.

03 · Demand quality

Prefer decisive departure, a compact base and a fresh zone; check real accomplishment.

04 · Use three timeframes

Location informs the side; trend supports the direction; execution sets order levels.

05 · Define risk in advance

Stop beyond distal, planned 2R and sufficient structural room to that target.

06 · Permission to pass

Bad location or a strong opposing zone may be reason enough not to trade.

Quick glossary

Core terms from the revision, in plain English.

Leg in

The incoming move leading price to a base.

Base

One or more candles where the move pauses or stabilizes.

Leg out

The outgoing move that leaves the base.

Proximal

The boundary of the zone closest to returning price.

Distal

The far boundary of the marked zone.

Fresh zone

A marked area not yet revisited after departure.

Tested zone

A marked area price has already returned to.

Curve

Position of current price between larger demand and supply zones.

Location

The higher timeframe used to establish broad context.

Execution

The lower timeframe where actual entry/stop are planned.

R

Absolute distance between planned entry and stop per unit.

2R

Objective twice the planned risk distance; fees and slippage excluded.
