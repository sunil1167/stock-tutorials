Chapter 01 · 00:02–01:26

The lesson's purpose

Tutor's framing · Builds on Episodes 1–3

Before this lesson, you were taught to identify demand and supply zones. Here, the tutor moves from marking the zone to putting entry and stop-loss levels on the chart and then asks you to refine those zones by looking beyond the base candle where appropriate.

He repeatedly asks learners to finish earlier homework, avoid skipping episodes, and practise instead of jumping to live trading. At this stage of the course, correct chart identification and marking matter more than choosing the best trade or forecasting the price.

What you should learn

  • Find proximal (PL) and distal (DL) boundaries.
  • Place entry and stop-loss on the correct sides.
  • Calculate a temporary 2R target.
  • Know when the normal distal boundary must be extended.
  • Recheck zones for freshness and invalidation.

What is not finished here

  • Definitive target selection and whether a 2:1 trade is available.
  • Trade priority when many zones are present.
  • Multi-timeframe alignment and other future filters.
  • Detailed position sizing and risk management.
Tutor's recurring instruction

Do not assume a marked demand zone means a share will “rocket,” or that any nearby zone automatically justifies a trade. This lesson is about marking correctly, not entering trades immediately.

Chapter 02 · 03:35–06:31; 22:29–23:09

Recognise the three parts of a zone

The teacher wants you to recognise leg-in → base → leg-out before drawing horizontal boundaries. The leg-out should create a meaningful directional move; simply having a red or green candle is not enough to prove quality.

ComponentWhat it represents in the lessonWhat to check
Leg-inPrice movement arriving at the base.Direction of movement into the brief pause.
BaseCandle or group of candles where buying and selling interact.Actual bodies and wicks; which candles genuinely belong to the base.
Leg-outPrice's move away from the base.Strength, displacement and whether the move actually leaves the base range.

The four named patterns

Demand

DBR = Drop → Base → Rally (reversal)

RBR = Rally → Base → Rally (continuation)

Supply

RBD = Rally → Base → Drop (reversal)

DBD = Drop → Base → Drop (continuation)

How the tutor handles an ambiguous “base” candle

In the early homework review (~04:11–05:26), some students see four base candles while others include more. The tutor first follows the previously taught body-percentage rule and uses four base candles in that example. He also notes that a candle that superficially qualifies as an exciting candle, but has not truly moved beyond the base's range, may be considered part of the base when reading the overall behaviour.

Practical interpretation: use the taught rule consistently while learning; later, also check whether a supposed departure actually achieved meaningful movement. Do not reclassify arbitrary candles simply to force a desirable zone.

Important distinction

Structurally markable ≠ high quality. Around 09:48–10:29, the tutor shows a red “exciting” candle that does not leave the base range; you may be able to draw a formally correct supply zone, but the chart has not shown the strength you wanted from the leg-out.

Chapter 03 · 05:33–06:31; 22:52–22:58; 57:59–59:07

Normal marking: PL and DL

The proximal line is the nearer edge of the zone from which your proposed entry is taken. The distal line is the far edge, past which your stop-loss is normally placed. In the standard approach, both lines come from the base candles.

Normal markingProximal line (PL)Distal line (DL)
DemandHighest body price among all base candles.Lowest wick / low among all base candles.
SupplyLowest body price among all base candles.Highest wick / high among all base candles.

For a candle, “highest body” means the higher of its open and close; “lowest body” means the lower of its open and close. A wick is not used for the proximal boundary in the tutor's normal or exceptional rules.

What if a candle has no visible upper or lower wick?

Around ~25:35–25:58 the tutor explains that a candle need not have a visible wick. If its open or close is already the extreme of the candle, that extremity still serves as the high/low for distal marking.

Memory shortcut

PL = base body. DL = zone extreme. Exceptional marking can move DL; it does not move the base-body PL.

Chapter 04 · 09:24–10:29; 23:22 onward; 53:56–56:34

Quality, location and freshness

Drawing a line is only the beginning. The tutor reviews real charts and asks whether the zone reflects meaningful buying/selling pressure, and whether price has already returned to it.

Find zones relative to current price

Demand: look left and down from current market price for a strong upward departure.

Supply: look left and up for a strong downward departure.

Evidence of strength

A strong, explosive leg-out, potentially with multiple strong departure candles, is the tutor's key visual signal of meaningful pressure. A weak move trapped inside the base does not show the same evidence.

“Fresh” and “tested” are not interchangeable

The tutor treats a zone as fresh when, after the leg-out, price has not returned to and touched its proximal boundary. This matters because the lesson's logic is based on the potential for unfilled/pending orders. When the tutor evaluates a zone for a first intended entry, simply arriving to trigger that entry does not automatically make it a disqualifying “already tested” zone.

Example: price arrives for the planned first entry

At roughly 2:00:30–2:01:38, the teacher shows a DBR zone that was untouched after the departure. Price then moves down and touches the planned entry area. He asks students whether the zone is fresh or tested; his answer emphasises that this is the entry they were waiting for, not evidence that it had already been tested before they began.

At ~2:16:35–2:16:46 he adds important qualifications: inspect whether the stop has already been breached and whether a relevant move/target has already occurred. Do not mechanically reuse a spent or invalid setup.

Do not over-infer

A strong leg-out and freshness can help with candidate assessment, but the tutor explicitly postpones the complete trade-selection hierarchy. Neither one is a guarantee of profit.

Chapter 05 · 31:42–42:58

Demand zone: plan a long entry

The teacher's simple long setup: buy just above PL, with a stop-loss below DL. The entry is close to the near edge; the stop gets some additional room outside the far edge.

Diagram 1 · Demand-zone long setup

Illustrative OHLC prices, not taken from the source chart
4 teaching steps
Bullish candleBearish candleZone
Step 1 of 4 — identify the base
Prices are designed only to illustrate boundary placement and a 2R distance. PL and DL are geometric levels, not guaranteed order concentrations.
  1. Choose the demand zone: find DBR or RBR with an upward leg-out.
  2. Mark normal PL on the highest base-candle body; mark DL on the lowest base wick, then check exceptional marking later.
  3. Entry: just above PL; the tutor gives only a small, situation-dependent offset, not a universal fixed rupee/percentage number.
  4. Stop-loss: just below DL, leaving a little additional room for a temporary wick or selling pressure near the far edge.
  5. Only then consider a temporary target equal to twice the entry-to-stop distance.

Why leave more room below DL?

The tutor uses a story of buyers and sellers competing in the base. A return to the zone can reach the PL, the middle of the zone or even close to DL depending on the strength of selling pressure. A stop placed exactly on the boundary can be triggered by a temporary probe before a rebound. The class therefore draws the stop beyond the distal edge, rather than exactly on it.

This “pending orders” story is the tutor's interpretive model, not something directly measurable from a standard price chart; it does not prove such orders exist.

Why is the demand entry just above PL rather than deep inside the base?

At roughly 35:43–36:40, the instructor explains that he expects buy interest when price returns to the zone, and other participants might also place orders near the proximal line. Placing the entry a little above PL makes the setup eligible to participate if the anticipated reversal starts at the zone's nearer edge. This is a rationale, not a prediction that every touch reverses.

Chapter 06 · approx. 45:00–51:00

Supply zone: plan a short entry

Mirror the long setup. At supply, the tutor expects potential selling near the base: sell just below PL and place the stop-loss above DL.

Diagram 2 · Supply-zone short setup

Illustrative OHLC prices · reversal/directional behaviour only
4 teaching steps
Bullish candleBearish candleZone
Step 1 of 4 — identify the base
For supply, PL is the lowest base-candle body, DL the highest base wick; a small buffer sits beyond each boundary.
  1. Look left and up from current price for a valid supply pattern with downward leg-out.
  2. Mark normal PL = lowest base body, DL = highest base wick.
  3. Place the planned short entry slightly below PL.
  4. Place stop-loss slightly above DL; allow a realistic buffer, without inventing a fixed percentage.
  5. The class's temporary example puts the profit target at approximately 2R below entry.
Reality check

A supply-zone diagram is a short-sell concept. The tutor notes in his Q&A that intraday selling may be possible even without already holding shares. Whether the instrument/account allows this, plus actual costs, margin and risk, is separate from the zone-marking lesson.

Chapter 07 · 43:06–45:01; 50:08–50:40

Targets: learn 2R without treating it as final

When asked for a target rule, the tutor says he would rather defer the complete explanation for several more classes. For this marking exercise, he uses a simple temporary target of double the stop-loss distance.

Long / demand

Target = Entry + 2R

where R = Entry − Stop. The distance from entry to target is twice the distance from entry to stop.

Short / supply

Target = Entry − 2R

where R = Stop − Entry. The distance below entry is twice the distance above entry to stop.

Source example: around 43:37–44:18, the chart shows an entry-to-stop distance of approximately ₹221. The tutor doubles it to about ₹442 of target distance, producing roughly a 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio. In the supply demonstration (~50:08–50:33), a distance around ₹89–₹90 is paired with a target distance around ₹180.

Precisely what the teacher does NOT say

He does not claim that every 2R target is reachable. He specifically says later classes will examine whether a particular setup allows 2:1 given surrounding price levels. A nearby opposite zone is not automatically the target.

Chapter 08 · ~02:00–31:42; 1:39:44–1:49

Worked charts: what the tutor checks

The class repeatedly returns to named market charts to practise reading the components correctly and revising PL/DL markings. Without the source video frames, the specific historical candle prices cannot be reconstructed reliably.

Example A · DMart chart

The tutor revisits a DMart demand-zone marking on a daily timeframe around 32:41–33:39. He identifies PL and DL, places the planned buy line slightly above PL and the stop line below DL, then explains why the stop needs a little more room than the entry buffer. He later returns to the same chart (~1:39:44 onward) to test whether exceptional marking changes any distal lines.

In the recap near 2:06:25–2:06:54, he describes a supply-zone interpretation that groups several candles and recognises an alternative, more obvious structure with two base candles and two explosive leg-out candles. The instructional focus is how you explain a candle grouping and place the boundaries, not whether DMart will rise or fall next.

Example B · Kotak Bank chart

From about 20:12 onward, the tutor discusses Kotak Bank on a 60-minute timeframe, searching relative to a selected current price. He marks more than one possible demand/supply zone and explains that newer candles can change which earlier zones remain valid. In the recap (~2:07:01–2:07:25), he notes a previously seen demand zone was broken and additional supply zones had formed.

The teaching point is that the same chart can contain multiple candidate zones. At this stage, he tells students not to worry about ranking them; first confirm the pattern, body/wick boundaries and freshness.

Example C · when two students choose different bases

Early in the session (~04:11–05:26), some learners count four base candles and others count more. The tutor accepts that looking at whether a supposed departure really left the base can sometimes yield an alternative grouping with a similar zone mark. Keep a clear reasoning trail: which candle was leg-in, which candles were base, and where did meaningful leg-out begin?

Practice method drawn from the live demonstrations

Pause a chart at a chosen historical current-price point, find one candidate zone, identify its three components, draw PL/DL, and only then review subsequent candles. This is a learning exercise, not evidence of a profitable backtest.

Chapter 09 · 57:49–1:30; 2:14:33–2:15:40

Exceptional marking: the rule that changes the chart

The normal method takes DL from base-candle wicks. Exceptional marking asks: what if the truly extreme price in the relevant buyer/seller fight occurred just before or after the base? The teacher may extend the distal boundary to capture that extreme.

Never forget

Exceptional marking changes only DL. The proximal line stays on the appropriate base-candle body. It is incorrect to shift PL to a nearby wick merely because it looks cleaner.

Reversal pattern: compare leg-in, base, and leg-out

In a DBR demand reversal, the tutor extends DL to the lowest low/wick across leg-in, base and leg-out when that low is below the base's normal DL. In an RBD supply reversal, he analogously considers the highest high/wick across the same three parts.

Diagram 3 · Exceptional DBR demand reversal

How a deeper leg-in wick shifts the stop boundary
4 teaching steps
Normal DLCorrected DLPL unchanged
Step 1 of 4
The deeper wick is intentionally exaggerated to make the distinction readable. It does not imply all reversal zones require a change.

Why does the tutor do this?

At ~1:00:41–1:01:12 and later, the tutor reasons that the leg-in selling pressure and the struggle with buyers can start before the nominal base. If a reversal ultimately launches from a low that belongs to that struggle, a stop tied only to the base candle may be too close to part of the region he considers relevant. The analogous logic applies to supply, where the earlier or later high can matter.

What if both leg-in and leg-out have lower wicks than the base?

The tutor discusses this type of structure near 1:47:34–1:48:35. The concept is not to favour whichever wick is closer to the base: compare their extremes, and take the most distant relevant wick for the distal boundary. Then revise the stop to sit beyond that new DL.

Chapter 10 · 1:30:15–1:39:38; 2:14:39–2:15:40

Reversal vs continuation: compare carefully

The teacher makes an important distinction in his final recap. Reversal zones consider the leg-in, base and leg-out; continuation zones do not pull in the earlier leg-in extreme, because that leg-in is treated as a prior, separate move.

PatternTypePL (unchanged)Exceptional DL comparison
DBR · demandReversalHighest body of baseLowest wick in leg-in + base + leg-out
RBD · supplyReversalLowest body of baseHighest wick in leg-in + base + leg-out
RBR · demandContinuationHighest body of baseLowest wick in base + leg-out
DBD · supplyContinuationLowest body of baseHighest wick in base + leg-out

Diagram 4 · Exceptional RBR demand continuation

Older leg-in extreme is excluded; leg-out lower wick can shift DL
4 teaching steps
Leg-in excludedNormal DLCorrected DL
Step 1 of 4
Continuation exception compares base and leg-out only. The extreme of an earlier rally/drop is deliberately omitted, as in the tutor's recap.

Make sense of this distinction

For a reversal, the incoming leg, base and outgoing leg form the directional fight that flips the market. For a continuation, the trend was already progressing; the tutor sees the nearby base-and-departure event as the relevant new fight rather than stretching the zone back across the whole preceding rally/drop.

Three-question test

1. Is this a reversal or a continuation? 2. Is it demand (lowest wick) or supply (highest wick)? 3. Does the eligible extreme go beyond the base-only DL? If not, exceptional marking makes no change.

Chapter 11 · ~1:40–2:16:46

Recheck boundaries, freshness, and invalidation

After teaching the exception, the tutor returns to chart examples and asks students whether previously marked zones need a different distal line. Many do not change: if the base already holds the eligible extreme, the normal and exceptional DL are identical.

  1. Reinspect the whole formation. Identify leg-in, base and leg-out rather than measuring random neighbouring candles.
  2. Keep the same PL. It remains a body-based boundary on the base.
  3. Decide if an exception applies. Use the correct set of comparison candles for reversal or continuation.
  4. Move DL only if needed. If you move DL farther from PL, your stop position and absolute price risk also change.
  5. Check that the zone is still usable. A broken stop boundary or price having already departed and played out the anticipated move may make a previously marked entry idea invalid in the class's discussion.
Fresh zone when price approaches the entry: tutor's nuance

The teacher corrects students who label a never-before-touched DBR zone “tested” at the instant price first arrives to trigger the planned entry. His point is that the first entry was exactly what the trader was waiting for. This is not permission to keep buying every later retest without checking the history and the original plan.

Risk consequence

Exceptional DL often puts the stop farther away. At unchanged entry and quantity, that means larger monetary risk. The tutorial illustrates where the stop belongs on the chart, but it does not provide a complete position-sizing framework in this episode.

Chapter 12 · Mistake prevention

Common mistakes the lesson helps prevent

MistakeCorrective habit
Drawing PL from a wick.Use the relevant base-body extreme.
Ignoring the leg-out's actual achievement.Check whether the departure really moved away from the base.
Putting stop exactly on DL.Place it a little beyond DL; do not assume an exact fixed buffer.
Using the lowest/highest wick of all three candles for every pattern.First classify reversal versus continuation.
Moving PL during exceptional marking.PL is unchanged; only the distal line is tested.
Assuming 2R always succeeds.Tutor defers actual target feasibility analysis.
Assuming any marked zone is an immediate trade.Prioritisation and additional filters are reserved for later classes.
Calling an untouched zone “previously tested” only because it reaches the planned first entry.Inspect post-leg-out history before labelling first-touch versus earlier test.
Ignoring a broken or spent level.Recheck invalidation on the actual historical price sequence.

What is a rule, and what is only an illustration?

Rule taught: the side of PL/DL on which entry and stop are planned; base-body PL; eligible distal comparison; practice 2R. Not a fixed rule: an exact 1%, ₹1, ATR or candle-size stop buffer; a guarantee of reversal at PL; a claim that every zone deserves a trade.

One-minute lesson summary

Place demand entry above PL and stop below DL; reverse for supply. Exceptional marking can widen DL only—compare all three parts for reversal, base plus leg-out for continuation. The class uses a provisional 2R target while deferring trade-selection and target feasibility to later lessons.
