Build the right mental model

Episode 6 extends the first five foundation lessons. The teacher's central question is not simply “Is this a green or red candle?” It is: What buying-versus-selling struggle could explain the candle sequence, and where did the decisive imbalance begin?

The older starting point

Recognise recurring sequences of price movement, a pause or base, and a strong movement away. The established labels are leg-in → base → leg-out. The teacher repeatedly refers to the basic demand and supply formations introduced earlier.

The new layer in this lesson

Ask whether the exit candle closes decisively, whether a gap hides an impulsive leg, whether an unusual pattern has more than one possible origin, and whether a crowded area is simply garbage to avoid.

The four familiar pattern names

DBRDrop → Base → RallyDemand reversal pattern
RBRRally → Base → RallyDemand continuation pattern
RBDRally → Base → DropSupply reversal pattern
DBDDrop → Base → DropSupply continuation pattern

The tutor applies the same reasoning to reversal and continuation structures. A formation's name is not enough; he inspects the exit, competing zones, the likely origin, and whether the price response actually demonstrates strength.

Class mindset

Get fluent in the clean leg-in/base/leg-out picture first, then understand the messier real-chart exceptions. Do not force a label onto every candle cluster.

Closing concept: did the buyers or sellers finish the job?

The first major concept tests where the leg-out closes relative to the earlier leg-in, rather than rewarding a large-looking green or red candle by itself.

How the teacher reasons through a DBR demand setup

  1. Find the drop (leg-in), the base (pause), and the rally (leg-out).
  2. Identify the prior area of selling that the rally needs to overcome. The teacher starts by checking whether the leg-out closes above the leg-in candle's upper region.
  3. If the rally turns down before convincingly getting through an opposing supply area, the teacher treats the demand setup with more caution. The nearby sellers have demonstrated that they can resist the buyers.
  4. If the rally clears that supply and closes beyond it, it gives stronger evidence of buyer dominance: the price move has overcome a meaningful opposing area.
  5. Then assess the whole context. Even a demand setup whose strength has not yet been proved may react later; it is not an absolute prediction that it will fail.

Why an opposing zone changes the reading

In the teacher's example around 08:40–12:30, a fresh demand zone sends price toward a supply zone. If price then drops from the supply, the supply has effectively proved that it can reject price. The demand zone has not necessarily demonstrated equal power. The teacher asks why truly dominant buyers would stop just below the sellers, rather than break through them.

Reverse the thought experiment: if the demand-generated rally pushes through the supply area and closes beyond it, the buying side has shown additional strength by removing that obstacle. The same intuition applies inversely to supply.

Case A: close below the obstacle

Price rallies away from a demand base, encounters supply, and falls. The supply response is observable; the demand origin may still work, but the teacher calls for extra caution.

Case B: close beyond the obstacle

Price closes beyond the opposing area. This is stronger contextual confirmation of the demand leg's buying pressure—not a guaranteed future bounce.

Questions to ask while marking a zone

  • Is the exit candle really strong, or merely positive in colour?
  • Did it close decisively relative to the leg-in and the immediately relevant opposing zone?
  • Was the exit capped by an already identifiable supply/demand area?
  • Did the zone prove itself through an actual reaction, or are you only assuming that it is powerful?
Do not convert the closing concept into a guaranteed signal. The teacher explicitly recognises that less convincing zones can still react. The comparison is about the quality of evidence, not a binary certainty.

Applied reasoning08:40–13:00, 35:40–39:00 · approximate transcript location

Proven vs unproven zones

A key distinction is between a zone that has already demonstrated a response and a new zone that traders hope will respond when tested.

1

A demand setup forms and its rally reaches an existing supply zone.

2

If the market falls from that supply zone, sellers there have shown an observable reaction.

3

The demand origin remains a candidate zone, but it has not proven itself in the same way.

4

If the rally instead overcomes the supply and closes past it, the interpretation changes in favour of buyers.

A nuanced statement you should remember

“The weaker-looking zone can still work.” In the teaching examples the trader distinguishes probability and conviction from a claim that only one setup can ever function. A previous supply zone can already have made its move, while a new demand zone later finds buyers at its origin.

The teacher makes repeated comparisons between two demand areas. The nearer zone (Zone 2) may be tradable in a strong uptrend if it has a good exit and is not directly stopped by major supply. The deeper zone (Zone 1), which is the original source of buying, may be favoured as the more conservative choice when Zone 2 is questionable.

Decision framework, not a numeric rule

Compare the quality of the close, the impulse, the opposing zone, and the true origin of the move. The transcript passages accessible here do not provide a verified win rate or universal numerical score for this comparison.

Significant gap + base = an invisible exit leg

The teacher introduces one of the most memorable formulas in the lesson: “Significant gap + base candle = leg-out / exciting candle.” This allows a zone to be interpreted even when no large visible leg-out candle appears on the plotted timeframe.

Significant gap+Base candle≈Implicit leg-out

In the gap-up example, the next candle begins much higher and then forms a base. Even if the candle's own body looks small, the advance happened between the previous closing area and the new opening area. The teacher describes this as an invisible exciting candle—a way to read the impulse that was not represented by a single large real body on the chart.

The gap-down version is the mirror image: a significant downward gap before a base can imply decisive selling. This is how he reasons about supply setups lacking a conventional, obvious red exit candle.

Why the tutor treats it as evidence of imbalance

He explains that a notable gap-up open suggests buying interest acted before the regular session candle completed. A flat or small base later in that session does not erase the price change that already occurred. Read the gap together with the new base, rather than examining the base in isolation.

The important limits

  • The gap must be meaningful to the pattern. A tiny routine difference between opens and closes should not be promoted automatically to an exciting leg.
  • The teacher presents the formula as a way of reading and marking a structure, not proof that a gap reliably predicts continuation.
  • Assess opposing supply or demand, the close, and the origin; these other concepts still matter.
  • His illustrations note that gaps can arise on daily, weekly, or monthly candles. The context of the gap is still relevant.

Two demand zones: origin vs nearer continuation

An unusual price structure can contain two plausible demand areas. Instead of blindly combining all candles into one huge zone, the tutor asks which part started the move and which part offered a subsequent continuation.

How he distinguishes the zones

Demand Zone 1 is described as the origin of the buying impulse. Demand Zone 2 is higher and appears after the movement has already begun. Zone 2 can still be useful if the market is trending strongly, its leg-out and close are good, and no major supply has just halted it.

If Zone 2 has an unconvincing close or meets meaningful supply, the teacher recommends being more conservative and considering the deeper origin, Zone 1. The logic: to revisit Zone 1, price must pass through or invalidate the nearer area first. That changes the buying/selling story at each location.

Entry and stop-loss discussion

The transcript mentions possible entry around Zone 2, with a stop beneath the relevant zone; it also notes that different traders may put stops in different places, including beneath the deeper Zone 1 for a more conservative interpretation. These alternatives mean stop distance and target practicality change. Do not present one line as mandatory for every variant.

Combining both zones into one broad area may be conceptually acceptable, but the teacher points out that a broad area can lead to an unnecessarily wide stop. He therefore separates origins to make the setup easier to reason about.

The lesson is about selection

“More likely to react” is not the same as “always trade Zone 1.” The teacher explicitly says Zone 2 is not automatically weak or forbidden and should be judged by its closing, leg-out, trend, and opposing supply.

Origin of buying and selling

Real charts are not always perfect leg-in/base/leg-out textbook pictures. The tutor asks students to identify the origin of the strong imbalance even when candles appear in an odd order or a previous candle lacks the expected colour.

What is an origin in this class?

It is the area from which buying or selling meaningfully took control and launched the move under study. A chart may contain one preliminary base, another base at a different height, and then an explosive exit. The question is not merely “Which box can be drawn?” but “Where is the most relevant source of the pressure?”

At around 54:00, the teacher introduces Origin of Buying and Selling specifically to handle strange structures. He urges beginners not to dismiss a clear strong exit solely because the candles preceding it are imperfect. If there is a true leg-out and a recognisable imbalance, search for a sensible way to mark the origin.

Why the nearest base is not always the whole story

An early base can represent the first buying/selling struggle. A later base can show a new pocket of orders. When they are separated, grouping both into a single broad zone may obscure how price actually departed each area. The teacher illustrates that different plausible markings can be acceptable as long as the trading logic is coherent.

Useful flexibility

When a strong departure is obvious, consider alternative ways to isolate the origin and obtain a sensible zone rather than insisting that every candle fit the first textbook drawing.

Not permission to invent setups

The tutor also warns not to force an entry, stop, and target onto an area with no compelling leg-out or imbalance. It is valid to skip.

Comparing a clean and an awkward setup

  1. Clean: leg-in → small base → clear powerful leg-out. Easy to mark and explain.
  2. Awkward: a base, then an unexpected leg-in, then an exit—or multiple uneven bases. The apparent pattern name changes, but the underlying buying/selling impulse may remain similar.
  3. Evaluate: find where pressure first became decisive, identify each base separately if appropriate, and check whether your zone width is practical.
  4. Reject: if the region has no trustworthy imbalance, or if you must stretch the definition repeatedly to justify the trade, pass on it.
Source-specific nuance

At roughly 56–57 minutes, the tutor accepts more than one marking for some multi-base structures but challenges unnecessarily large stop-loss distances. His intent is to refine judgment, not replace the original foundation with arbitrary drawing.

Distribution of buying and selling

The teacher next examines how buying or selling may be distributed across separate candle levels, especially when successive base candles are separated by gaps or the market moves in small steps before the final decisive impulse.

Read the sequence, not one isolated candle

For example, a series of small gap-up openings followed by base-like trading suggests that buyers repeatedly pushed price higher between sessions, even though buyers and sellers fought during each individual base candle. The teacher asks where sellers finally seized control if that series ends in a sharp decline. He places the meaningful origin of selling near the area from which the explosive drop actually began.

How to make sense of the stepped price path

  • Each base reflects a local phase of negotiation between buyers and sellers.
  • The gap between bases can reflect movement not captured as a continuous large body in the immediately following candle.
  • A later impulsive drop can identify an area of stronger selling pressure than the earlier small pauses.
  • Separately marked pockets may imply separate areas where the tutor expects pending orders, rather than a single perfectly uniform block.

The same principle on different timeframes

A move can look continuous on a smaller timeframe and like repeated gaps on a higher timeframe. The teacher uses daily and weekly examples to reinforce that the candle picture is an aggregation of what happened, not the whole story of the buying/selling process.

Interpretation, not direct order-book observation: The lesson speaks of pending buyers/sellers inferred from price behaviour. The chart itself does not reveal the actual number or identity of hidden orders.

Garbage area: learn what not to trade

A chart can contain lots of action without containing a high-quality zone. The tutor calls these cluttered regions “garbage areas” and repeatedly tells students to stop forcing trades there.

What makes a structure look like garbage?

The tutor sketches an extended chain containing several base candles and mixed impulses: a drop, base, another base, a red departure, another base, a green departure, more basing, then another strong green candle. Labelling every small sub-sequence as a separate excellent zone becomes misleading. There are too many overlapping battles and ambiguous origins.

His simpler starting preference is for fewer, cleaner base candles and a stronger decisive exit. As extra basing and overlapping fluctuations accumulate, he describes the area as increasingly poor for straightforward zone marking.

Clean and garbage are qualitative judgments in this lesson, not algorithmically certified labels.

The most important practical distinction

There is a difference between (a) a non-standard structure with a genuinely powerful leg-out, which may deserve careful analysis, and (b) a crowded structure whose only appeal is that you can force a pattern name onto it. Learn the first; usually reject the second.

Worth studying

An unusual configuration but a strong, recognisable supply/demand imbalance, with logical marking and manageable boundaries.

Usually skip

Many overlapping bases, no clean origin, weak close, short exits, or a setup that requires constant excuses to justify the trade.

Observed bounce does not prove the original setup was good

In his practical chart discussion around 2:05, he notes that messy areas can still react. That is not a reason to select them: the aim is to choose the better-quality opportunities, not retrospectively claim every bounce was predictable.

Teacher's discipline rule

“Just because you opened the chart does not mean you must find a trade.” If the level feels forced or excessively confusing, leave it and move on.

Proximal, distal, and the width of a zone

The tutor compares multiple ways to mark an uneven group of base candles and stresses that marking choices affect the stop-loss width and target feasibility.

Proximal line

The nearer boundary of a supply or demand zone—the first edge price encounters on a revisit.

Distal line

The farther boundary of the zone, typically beyond the base's relevant extreme.

Wick/body choice

Different source illustrations refer to the highest body and lowest wick (for a demand example), or a wick-to-wick marking where appropriate. They are context dependent rather than a single universal geometry.

Why over-wide zones are costly

A broad zone may require a distant stop to allow the entire marked area to remain in play. Even if the trader controls maximum money at risk by reducing position size, a larger price-distance stop still changes the target distance needed for a favourable reward-to-risk outcome.

At approximately 2:37 the tutor explicitly separates the two issues: risk management can cap the monetary loss, but the system and chart structure determine the realistic target. Widening the stop indefinitely can harm the trade's practicality.

Useful pre-trade questions

Which base is the true origin? Is a narrower zone consistent with the pressure shown? If using one wide combined zone, is the stop reasonable for the planned target? If not, skipping may be better.

Practical chart reading: the tutor’s workflow

The second half of the class applies the five concepts to non-ideal chart pictures. The main skill is deciding which zones deserve attention, rather than proving that every recognizable structure produced a reaction once.

Worked reading A — imperfect continuation structure

  1. A chart contains several base candles at different levels, with an upward move between them.
  2. The original RBR label may not describe the most meaningful portion of the movement.
  3. Apply the significant-gap logic if a move occurred across an opening gap; isolate separate local bases if they imply distinct origins.
  4. Inspect the leg-out close. If the exit is weak or trapped under a higher supply area, reduce confidence.
  5. If the only way to trade it is to combine many candles into a giant zone, evaluate whether it has become a garbage area.

Worked reading B — two separated supply bases

In a later chart example around 1:49–1:51, the teacher discusses two base candles at different heights. He points out that the first and second areas can each be associated with selling pressure. A price move may touch the nearer boundary of one zone and turn down. However, he explicitly says the example is not a trade to take because the closing/context is poor; it is shown to practise the day's concepts.

Worked reading C — a poor zone can still show a response

A cluttered multi-base area sometimes produces a visible bounce. The teacher warns students not to let that hindsight convince them that a weak pattern was a good selection. The goal is a consistently readable process: identify origin, strength, close, opposition, and tradeability before observing the reaction.

Worked reading D — change candle order without changing the price story

The transcript includes reasoning that moving an awkward red candle before or after a base can change whether a simple label sounds like DBR or RBR, while the main departure remains similar. Likewise, altering a candle's opening position without changing high/low/close may not change the key zone logic. This is why the teacher argues for interpreting price action, not memorising coloured shapes.

Practice drill (source-inspired, not a quoted instruction)

Find ten historical chart examples. For each: locate the leg-in and base(s), identify the departure, test the closing concept, note any gap, mark the likely origin, record any opposing zone, and classify the region as clean, awkward-but-analysable, or garbage. Do not place a live trade just to complete the drill.

Risk management and common mistakes

The lesson is mainly about reading price action, but trading discipline appears repeatedly. The tutor distinguishes adaptable zone-marking judgments from foundational risk and stop-loss discipline that should not be casually rewritten.

Rules the teacher repeatedly emphasises

  • Do not force a trade. If no sound imbalance and entry rationale are visible, skip the chart.
  • Be selective. Prefer meaningful leg-outs and closes over long chains of overlapping bases.
  • Use context. A nearby major opposing zone can invalidate an otherwise appealing story.
  • Respect your stop-loss plan. The teacher treats disciplined stopping as more important than manipulating a rule so that a weak trade looks acceptable.
  • Account for the width of your zone. Monetary risk can be limited by position sizing; it does not solve an unrealistic price target.
  • Use the aggressive/conservative distinction correctly. Zone 2 may suit a stronger, clean context; a deeper origin may be preferable when the nearer zone is questionable.

Common errors to avoid

01

Assuming a green candle proves buyers are strong without checking its close against the earlier selling pressure.

02

Ignoring an opposing supply zone that stops an apparent demand rally.

03

Rejecting a significant gap-plus-base picture just because there is no obvious tall exit candle.

04

Combining several widely separated bases into one extremely wide, impractical zone.

05

Marking every base in a garbage area and then declaring each historical reaction a valid strategy.

06

Copying a stop-loss position from a different diagram without explaining which origin it protects.

One-page revision and key takeaways

The five concepts, compressed

01 · Closing

Did the departure close with enough conviction to overcome prior opposition?

02 · Gap + base

A meaningful gap followed by a base can represent an implicit exciting leg.

03 · Origin

Find where decisive buying/selling began, even in oddly ordered candle structures.

04 · Distribution

Pressure may be spread among separated bases; locate where control truly changed.

05 · Garbage

Mixed, overlapping, crowded candles without clean imbalance are usually not worth forcing into trades.
