CHAPTER 01 · 03:31–06:03

The central decision: aggressive or conservative

The instructor says a trader cannot remain aggressive all the time, while choosing to remain conservative is always possible. A conservative approach may skip some entries, but a missed entry is not an actual monetary loss. More importantly, being selective aims to avoid setups more likely to hit stop-losses.

Core lesson

Do not try to trade every apparent demand or supply zone. First determine whether the context permits aggressive execution. Trading fewer, better-supported setups is part of the method.

Conservative

  • Wait for a stronger location or clearer close.
  • Prefer higher-timeframe support and aligned zones.
  • Accept missed entries as the cost of selectivity.
  • Avoid forcing positions when the chart is unclear.

Aggressive

  • Consider an earlier execution only when the context supports it.
  • Check opposing higher-timeframe zones and trend.
  • Require a sufficiently strong execution-zone achievement.
  • Never interpret aggressive as trading without a stop-loss.

Important nuance: aggressive versus conservative describes entry selectivity and confirmation in this lesson. The recovered source does not provide a rule to increase capital allocation simply because a setup looks aggressive.

CHAPTER 02 · 06:08–07:10; 18:49–25:46

The framework: high time frame + top-down + closing

The tutor initially mentions several steps but clarifies that they substantially overlap. The recurring working checklist is higher-timeframe support, a top-down approach, and closing/achievement. Sector support is referenced as another factor, but this class defers its fuller explanation to a separate sector topic.

1
Higher-timeframe support

Identify the origin of the current move and the opposing supply/demand zone that price might reach.

2
Top-down inspection

Scan the large time frames first, then move down to the proposed location, trend, and execution.

3
Closing or achievement

Look for evidence that price has actually overcome a relevant opposing area, rather than assuming it will.

4
Sector context — mentioned, not fully taught here

Keep broader sector conditions in mind; do not invent a sector-scoring rule from this transcript.

What this means in practice

An attractive entry candle does not cancel a nearby monthly or quarterly supply zone. Check the higher time frame before you admire the lower-timeframe execution.

Top-down analysis: start higher than your trade

CHAPTER 04 · 15:00–18:49; 20:35–24:05

Compare the strength of opposing zones

A lower-timeframe demand zone can appear beautiful while price is descending from a much larger supply zone. The teacher repeatedly warns that this may be a poor long, even if the small execution scores very highly.

  • If price is falling from a monthly supply, the instructor prefers looking for support at a monthly demand area.
  • If price falls from quarterly supply, a quarterly demand area becomes the more natural place to look for support.
  • A smaller opposing demand zone may still matter if the higher supply is relatively ordinary and the lower demand unusually strong, with other context supporting it. This is described as an exception, not the default.
  • If the opposing zone is from a time frame higher than the proposed trade's location, the tutor says to become conservative.

Read the diagram as a schematic hierarchy of zones, not actual historical candles or guaranteed price movement.

Common error

“My daily or 15-minute entry is excellent, therefore the monthly sell pressure does not matter.” In the tutor's framework, the higher-timeframe zone can invalidate that assumption.

CHAPTER 05 · 12:00–15:10; 25:18–25:46; 39:18–43:19

Closing above a zone changes the approach

The instructor discusses waiting for a proper close above the opposing supply before treating a nearby demand area more aggressively. He distinguishes a decisive achievement from price merely touching, piercing, or approaching a level.

  1. Identify the supply area currently pressuring price.
  2. Watch whether price actually closes above it on a meaningful time frame.
  3. When the area has been overcome, an aligned lower demand setup may become more reasonable.
  4. Without the close, remain cautious or wait for a stronger higher-timeframe demand area.
  5. If price hits another supply and turns back down, do not blindly buy the upper demand; consider the lower demand location instead.
Not every close is equivalent

The tutor links the usefulness of a closing candle to how strong the surrounding area and time-frame structure are. A closing signal should be interpreted in context, not treated as a magical indicator.

CHAPTER 06 · 13:55–14:55; 01:48:40–01:49:24; 01:57:57–01:58:41

Zone achievement: what did this zone actually overcome?

The teacher repeatedly asks whether a zone has an achievement: for example, did a rally from the demand zone remove an opposing supply zone? A zone formed without overcoming anything important may be less convincing. This is particularly relevant when choosing whether to stay conservative against a large selling area.

More meaningful evidence

A demand origin produces a rally that defeats a named opposing supply area, or multiple matching time frames support the move.

Less meaningful evidence

A small demand base appears during a wider monthly selloff but fails to overcome a meaningful supply area.

The recovered discussion around 01:48–01:49 also interprets repeated demand failures combined with supply zones holding as continued selling pressure. Avoid assuming one failed zone automatically determines the whole trend; observe the repeated structure.

CHAPTER 07 · 08:55–10:00; 25:53–28:05; 43:19–43:43

Location and execution should coincide

The tutor separates a location zone (the important broader area) from an execution zone (the narrower place considered for entry). An execution area well inside or closely overlapping the higher-frame location has a stronger contextual foundation than one floating far away.

For intraday-style 15-minute executions he emphasizes proper coincidence. A good daily location close is desirable, but he explicitly says its absence does not automatically mean reject every trade. In that case, a more meaningful achievement by the execution area—for example overcoming a 75-minute or 125-minute supply together with supportive trend—is part of his reasoning for considering a more aggressive entry.

High-value distinction

“Try for a good location close” is a preference. “Ensure the execution properly coincides” is a strongly emphasized condition in the recovered explanation.

CHAPTER 08 · 30:47–43:43; 01:14:09–01:15:45

Trend, curve location, and strength determine selectivity

Even when a zone looks valid, trend and the position of the zone inside the overall range can justify extra caution. The tutor illustrates not rushing into longs while a quarterly/monthly supply area remains unbroken. He describes using a lower location, closer to the bottom of the range, in a sideways or mildly weak trend to improve safety.

  • Strong uptrend + good location + evidence of achievement: a more aggressive demand execution may be acceptable, subject to the wider-zone check.
  • Sideways or weak trend: prefer lower-in-the-curve entries, tighter alignment, and more location support.
  • Strong downtrend approaching higher-frame demand: do not keep shorting every small supply zone into the much larger demand zone.
  • Unclear, cluttered, or mixed chart: pass instead of forcing a trade.

The terms strong, extraordinary, and low in the curve come from the class's qualitative judgement; the recovered excerpts do not supply a numerical threshold.

CHAPTER 09 · 39:18–43:43; 01:21:55–01:25:35

Example workflow: a monthly-location (MIT) trade

The tutor illustrates an MIT setup with monthly location, weekly trend, and daily execution. In one discussed situation, weekly and daily demand are closely aligned, adding support to the monthly picture. He stresses that a visually appealing MIT long is still not safe if monthly supply is sending price down.

1
Begin broad

Mark yearly/quarterly and monthly supply/demand and identify the origin of price's current move.

2
Find monthly location

Check where it sits within the larger range and whether a larger opposing zone is active.

3
Read weekly direction

Watch for a clear trend or sideways range; do not overstate direction.

4
Study daily execution

Look for a narrow, meaningful zone with achievement and coincidence. Occasionally the tutor considers a narrow aligned weekly execution when daily is unclear.

5
Assess the complete setup

Only consider entry after the opposing-zone and stop-loss/target checks are satisfactory.

Two-way market, not two-way permission

The examples show that both an MIT long and an MIT short can develop in a sideways market, but each requires its own location and alignment; one direction does not automatically authorize the other.

CHAPTER 10 · 53:00–01:30:30 (selected passages)

Case study: HDFC AMC — higher-timeframe reaction

The teacher revisits HDFC AMC to show how a stock reacts around a yearly demand zone and how that higher-timeframe context changes what you should attempt on monthly, weekly, and daily charts. The indexed passages describe a yearly-demand reaction and further checks of quarterly/monthly supply and demand, not a self-contained historical chart reconstruction.

Verified lessons from the walkthrough

  • Check whether the current move originated from an important yearly/quarterly area before analyzing shorter entries.
  • Strong downtrend readings alone do not justify shorting into an already reached monthly demand zone.
  • Where the trend is sideways, the teacher prefers a conservative, well-supported location rather than a premature higher entry.
  • Aligned execution and location can support a reasonable trade even if the move's precise maximum is unknown in advance.
  • He discusses possible risk-to-reward outcomes around 2:1, 2.5:1 or 3:1 in illustrative setups; he does not promise these outcomes.
  • If the chart looks doubtful, do not borrow someone else's confidence and force a position.

CHAPTER 11 · 01:30:43–01:49:30 (selected passages)

Case study: TVS Motor — all-time highs and opposing supply

In the TVS Motor example, the teacher starts from a yearly rally-base-rally demand zone, follows the advance toward all-time highs, and then investigates large supply areas and the strength of subsequent monthly demand. He raises the possibility that a half-yearly supply zone can overpower a routine monthly demand area.

  • Do not buy simply because a stock previously rose strongly or reached an all-time high.
  • Identify the highest relevant opposing supply before accepting a monthly-location long.
  • When a half-yearly supply is active, the instructor expresses preference for at least a stronger quarterly/half-yearly response area, or an unusually strong monthly origin with supporting evidence.
  • He discusses formation of an execution near a gap-up in the daily chart, but the excerpt does not establish the complete gap-trading procedure.
  • Repeated demand-zone breaks while supply keeps holding are interpreted as a warning of continuing selling pressure.
Reject the tempting shortcut

A stock's history of rallying from demand is not a guarantee that its next small demand zone can defeat the newest higher-timeframe supply.

CHAPTER 12 · 01:57:57–02:35:51 (selected passages)

Later walkthroughs: avoid forcing an unclear chart

Later sections keep testing the same principle through further chart examples: zone achievement, quarterly/monthly opposition, missing structure, and waiting for confirmation. The teacher specifically says that when the chart looks like “garbage” and nothing is clear, abandon that setup rather than insist on finding a trade.

  • A demand zone that has not eliminated a meaningful supply zone has weaker achievement.
  • A lower-timeframe execution may appear strong, but a quarterly supply above it may still make a long too aggressive.
  • Keep quarterly and monthly supply/demand levels in view even if you intend an intraday execution.
  • At a doubtful monthly demand area, either wait for a more reliable lower location or for better price-action confirmation; the exact confirmation method is said to be taught elsewhere.
  • A substantial decline can bring multiple stocks into important demand areas around the same time. This is a reason to prepare, not proof that the next bounce must succeed.
On “100% confidence”

The tutor uses strong confidence language to teach selectivity, but no setup is objectively 100% certain. Treat it as his emphasis on rejecting doubtful trades, not a guarantee of win rate.

CHAPTER 13 · 03:37–04:05; 30:47–31:29; 01:14:42–01:15:18; 02:19:42–02:20:20

Risk management and common mistakes

This episode builds on a previous risk-management class. It does not re-teach a complete position-sizing formula or fixed stop-loss percentage in the recovered passages. Its risk focus is on which trades to avoid before entry.

Actions the teacher encourages

  • Reduce unnecessary trade count.
  • Respect higher-frame supply/demand conflicts.
  • Seek good zone alignment and meaningful closing.
  • Accept a skipped opportunity without regret.
  • Evaluate whether a trade offers a reasonable target relative to its stop.

Mistakes the teacher warns against

  • Believing a high trade score overrides higher-frame context.
  • Going long straight into larger supply.
  • Shorting straight into large demand.
  • Trading a doubtful or cluttered chart.
  • Copying another person's conviction against your analysis.

Risk-to-reward illustration (not a price recommendation)

If a setup's defined risk equals one unit R, a 2:1 target means a possible gain of 2R for a loss of 1R if the stop is hit. The instructor mentions 2:1/3:1 outcomes while talking through examples; the full stop-placement method belongs to other episodes.

The tutor suggests that eliminating easily avoidable stop-outs can improve the share of winning trades. That is a selection principle, not evidence of 100% accuracy. Skipping trades can also exclude winners.

CHAPTER 14 · 03:31–02:35:51 (cross-section)

Decision checklist and lesson synthesis

Use this as a memory aid based on the repeatedly described reasoning, not as a new automated strategy or source-quoted formula.

One-sentence summary

Be aggressive only when the location, execution, trend and meaningful price action are supported by the larger time frames; otherwise be conservative, wait for a stronger area, or skip the trade.
