Chapter 01 · Foundation

Why good predictions alone do not make a good trader

~00:52–02:50

The tutor begins by distinguishing knowing a strategy from executing it correctly. Before growing trading capital, protect it.

Even 80% accuracy is not enough if the two losing trades give back more than the eight winners earned.

A trader may identify price direction correctly most of the time but lose money through oversized positions, uncontrolled exits, averaging a losing trade, or abandoning the plan. Risk management connects technical analysis to an amount of money the trader can afford to lose.

1 · Before entry

Define the loss

Choose the entry, technical stop-loss, and fixed money risk before calculating the quantity.

2 · During trade

Protect the plan

Do not keep modifying a position simply because the price is moving against you.

3 · Over many trades

Measure the system

Evaluate a meaningful sample of rule-based trades, not just a handful of winners or losses.

Central idea: A loss is a normal planned outcome. An unplanned, oversized loss is what the risk-management system aims to prevent.
Chapter 02 · Core formula

Position sizing: decide quantity from risk, not stock price

~10:30–21:00

The tutor criticises buying arbitrary round lots — 50 shares because a stock is expensive, 200 because it is cheaper, or 500 because the price is low. That misses the most important factor: the distance between entry and stop-loss.

The tutor’s beginner risk rule

Money risk per trade = Trading capital × 1% Shares = Money risk ÷ |Entry price − Stop-loss price|

The tutor starts at 1% of trading capital, later discussing 1.5% and 2% for more experienced traders. He discourages beginners from risking more and notes that some advanced traders risk even more, without recommending that as a starting point.

Transcript-derived worked example · ~18:00

Buy around ₹983; stop at ₹963

  1. Trading capital = ₹1,00,000.
  2. Money risk = 1% = ₹1,000.
  3. Entry-to-stop distance = ₹983 − ₹963 = ₹20 per share.
  4. Quantity = ₹1,000 / ₹20 = 50 shares.
  5. At the planned stop, price loss ≈ 50 × ₹20 = ₹1,000 (excluding fees, slippage, or gaps).

₹983 and ₹963 appear in the recoverable transcript around 18:05–18:25. The trade is an instructional calculation, not a recommendation for a particular stock.

Why the same quantity in two trades can be dangerous

With 100 shares, a ₹10 stop risks ₹1,000, but a ₹20 stop risks ₹2,000. The tutor describes how a wider-stop losing trade can cancel out several smaller winners when the trader buys the same quantity without accounting for stop distance.

His solution is to adjust the quantity so the planned cash loss remains comparable across setups.

Important mathematical and practical boundaries

For a short position the direction reverses: the stop lies above entry. Use the absolute difference between entry and stop to compute monetary risk. Whole shares must be rounded down, not up. The cash needed to buy the shares is a second constraint; calculate it separately.

A stop order does not guarantee an exact exit price during a gap or illiquid market. These execution limitations are practical risk cautions added for clarity, not numerical claims from the tutor.

Chapter 04 · Plan the payoff

Risk-to-reward: accept only setups with enough space

~20:14, 1:07:39–1:08:20

After fixing loss at 1R, the tutor sets a minimum planned reward of 2R. If a loss at the stop would be ₹1,000, the target should offer at least ₹2,000 profit at the planned quantity. If the price structure does not allow that, he says to skip the trade.

Chapter 05 · Execution discipline

The stop-loss is part of the original decision

~29:50–37:25

The tutor warns against changing quantities after entry, repeatedly averaging down when a long trade falls, and cancelling a stop because a position might recover. Doing so changes the original risk calculation.

Rule-based response

Price hits the stop

Exit, accept the pre-planned loss and consider the next independent setup. The loss is not a signal that the entire method has failed.

Unplanned response

Price falls; add more

Additional shares increase exposure and risk. Moving the stop farther away breaks the trade’s original money-risk limit.

Diagram 3 · Averaging down changes the risk

Worked illustration

This simple price illustration demonstrates the tutor’s warning. The added shares increase risk, even though the average buying price improves.

Behavioural trap: Sometimes a stock recovers to the purchase price after the trader ignores the stop. The tutor says that this apparent “success” can train a dangerous habit. He describes how traders then wait only to exit at cost instead of following their original reward target.
What if several stops hit back to back?

The tutor uses five consecutive planned 1%-of-initial-capital stops as a manageable illustrative drawdown of ₹5,000 on a ₹1,00,000 starting account. He urges the student not to abandon the strategy after three or five disappointing trades, but to review a much larger sample. Real consecutive percentage losses compounded on a changing capital balance produce slightly different results.

Chapter 06 · Be selective

Ten quality trades per month — not ten mandatory trades

~1:01:00–1:08:20, 1:31:45

The tutor describes filtering a large watchlist to stronger candidates, then intentionally limiting the actual trades. For the learning phase, his rule is to allow no more than 10 trades in a month and reject weaker opportunities.

Diagram 4 · The selection funnel

Tutor's illustrative counts

The transcript discusses reducing a roughly 200-stock universe and using increasingly strict selection. The funnel explains selectivity; it is not a mechanical screen with a guaranteed conversion rate.

Limiting entries encourages the learner to judge the quality of the setup: higher-timeframe trend, location, demand/supply structure, closing/execution criteria and adequate room to the target. This is why the tutor emphasises WIT and MIT type setups for beginners rather than taking every shorter-timeframe signal.

Do not force a trade: Being in front of the screen does not create an obligation to place an order. The tutor explicitly warns against rushing to complete 100 trades for the sake of completing the count.
What do the lesson's abbreviations mean?

The transcript references AIT, DDIT, WIT and MIT to distinguish trade approaches/timeframe categories. In the capital-allocation discussion, AIT/DDIT are associated with shorter/intraday approaches, WIT with weekly/swing, and MIT with a longer monthly approach. Some automated Hindi transcription of these abbreviations is inconsistent; the notebook retains the original letter labels where exact expansions cannot be verified.

How the tutor extends his spreadsheet

In later examples he increases the risk budget to 2% after developing experience and explores higher potential ratios such as 3R. His purpose is to show that reward size and consistent risk control matter as much as win rate. Increasing risk also magnifies drawdowns; higher reward targets are not automatically achievable.

Read the return examples correctly: The tutor enthusiastically extrapolates monthly scenarios into rapid account growth. They are arithmetic hypotheticals, not demonstrated, predictable or guaranteed investment returns. Real results include losing streaks, capital constraints, costs and changing win rates. Even a strategy with a good historical win rate can lose money.
Why a strong win rate can still lose money

Consider the tutor’s opening warning: eight winners earn ₹500 each (= ₹4,000), while two uncontrolled losers lose ₹3,000 each (= ₹6,000). Win rate is 80%, but net result is −₹2,000. Equalising planned losses and keeping reward multiples meaningful helps explain the proposed rules.

The ₹500 and ₹3,000 figures are a new illustrative calculation to explain the tutor’s principle; they are not claimed as exact transcript figures.

Chapter 08 · Finding the target

Let chart structure justify 2R, 3R or more

~1:24:45–1:29:00

The tutor explains that a reward multiple is not selected merely because a trader wants more profit. The distance to a meaningful opposing supply/demand zone matters. For a long setup, monthly support, weekly supply zones and a daily execution signal are studied together.

  1. Locate the trade idea: Has price approached a meaningful higher-timeframe demand area?
  2. Locate opposing pressure: Is there a weekly/intermediate supply zone overhead? How far is it from the entry?
  3. Use execution timeframe: Mark the entry and structural stop, rather than moving the stop to force an attractive ratio.
  4. Check reachable reward: If the room is below 2R, the tutor says to pass. If structure offers 3R or 4R, that may be considered; actual price movement remains uncertain.

Target at weekly supply

The tutor refers to a trade where a price objective at a weekly supply zone offered about 4:1, reportedly within two days. The structure supplies the objective.

Illustration: 9R

HDFC setup

Another chart example is described as offering around 9:1. This is an example of a particular setup, not the standard expectation for all trades.

What about trailing a winner?

The tutor mentions that some strong trends may offer much larger reward multiples through trailing stops. He explicitly says trailing is to be studied in another lesson. No full trailing-stop procedure is invented here.

Chapter 09 · Instrument choice

Why the tutor prioritises equity cash over F&O for beginners

~43:30–1:01:00, 2:30:00

The tutor repeatedly argues that beginners should practise systematic trading in the equity cash market before attempting futures and options. His concern is that a small account may fail to control a derivative position’s loss accurately.

Futures: lot-size granularity

A cash stock can often be purchased in a chosen whole-share quantity. A future has an exchange-defined lot size, so the required position may jump in units of one whole lot. The tutor describes a case where the resulting stop could create a loss of about ₹25,000 instead of the much smaller intended risk budget — making the setup incompatible with a small trading account.

Lesson rule: If the minimum tradable lot would exceed the intended money-at-risk limit, the beginner cannot use the desired position-sizing formula in that contract. The tutor’s response is to avoid forcing the trade.

Options: being right on direction is not enough

He stresses that options present further difficulties, especially when a market stays sideways longer than a beginner expects. His sequence is to learn market direction, structure and consolidation behaviour first, not to use options to accelerate account growth.

Can you gain skill with ₹5,000–₹10,000?

Near the conclusion, the tutor says beginners can learn with relatively modest amounts, such as ₹5,000 or ₹10,000, and discusses using a 0.5% risk level during learning. His focus in that passage is practice and skill acquisition, rather than meaningful income from such a small account.

Practical caution: a low-budget setup may still be impossible to size properly when one share costs too much or charges are material; skipping the trade is legitimate.

Chapter 10 · Account management

Do not mechanically split capital into 20% / 30% / 50% buckets

~1:38:25–1:51:00

Some traders allocate separate slices of capital to intraday, swing and longer trades. The tutor specifically challenges a rigid 20% / 30% / 50% split (or 30% / 30% / 40%). In his demonstration, dividing a ₹1,00,000 account this way reduces the 1%-of-bucket risk budgets to ₹200, ₹300 and ₹500 instead of reasoning from the overall strategy’s ₹1,000 risk-per-trade baseline.

His alternative is to stay selective and let positions use available cash as setups arise, rather than reserving a fixed part of the account for a category with no current opportunities.

Trade risk versus cash committed

Risk at the stop is not the same as purchase value. A position of 50 shares at ₹300 costs ₹15,000, while 50 shares at ₹600 costs ₹30,000, regardless of having the same quantity. The tutor shows why the number of simultaneously affordable positions depends on cash required, not just the percentage stop-loss rule.

Safety distinction: The tutor’s objection to rigid sub-buckets does not mean opening unlimited simultaneous positions. A 1% per-trade limit is not a 1% account-wide limit: five open positions each risking 1% can collectively expose the account to approximately 5% before market gaps and correlations. Track both the total cash committed and total open risk.
How does the tutor expect capital to become available?

He explains that highly selective setups need not all open on the same day. As positions are closed, the funds are free for later quality trades. He presents this as a practical alternative to repeatedly taking poor setups and clogging the account with many slow-moving positions.

Chapter 11 · Case-study lens

Higher-timeframe demand, execution, and opposing supply

~2:17:00–2:24:00

In a later chart discussion, the tutor contrasts generic support/resistance thinking with his demand/supply approach. A higher-timeframe demand zone can matter even if a lower-timeframe chart has not produced a simplistic closing-break confirmation. He also discusses follow-up demand zones formed during an upward reaction.

Monthly / higher-timeframe

Find the broader location and whether price is interacting with an important demand or support area.

Daily / execution

Watch how price reacts, whether stronger follow-up demand appears, and what opposing supply levels are ahead.

Key subtlety: a retest does not mean the same thing in every model

The tutor notes that the repeated-test interpretation of support/resistance differs from the interpretation of demand/supply. He cautions against treating a visible close beyond a horizontal area as the only meaningful condition. This is a conceptual reminder from the chart discussion, not a complete rule set for classifying each possible zone.

Chapter 12 · Build habits

A beginner’s risk-first practice plan

~37:00, 1:56:00, 2:02:00, 2:27:00

The tutor recommends evaluating the method after about 100 honest, rule-based trades, not changing systems after three or five stops. Near the end, he repeats the two-part practice rule: ten selective trades per month, one hundred trades to learn the process.

Use this checklist before each trade

  • Have I identified a proper setup and its higher-timeframe context?
  • Have I marked entry, structural stop, and a credible target?
  • Is planned reward at least twice the planned loss?
  • Is the quantity computed from my fixed money-risk budget?
  • Can I afford the position using available cash?
  • Have I checked my total open risk across all positions?
  • Am I respecting the tutor’s selectivity rule instead of forcing an entry?

The first, second, third, fourth, fifth, and seventh checks restate the tutor’s framework; combined open-risk check is an additional practical clarification.

Chapter 14 · One-page review

Summary and key takeaways

Keep for reference
The thesis: Prioritise controlled losses and repeatable execution. One correct prediction means little without position sizing; one stop-out should not cause the trader to abandon disciplined trading.
  1. Fix cash risk first: Start at 1% of trading capital per trade under the tutor's beginner framework.
  2. Quantity varies: Risk money divided by entry–stop distance determines share quantity.
  3. Reward must justify risk: Tutor's minimum is planned 2R, grounded in chart structure rather than wishful profit goals.
  4. Respect exits: Do not average a loser repeatedly, widen the stop or wait indefinitely to get back to cost.
  5. Trade less, study more: Use a selective maximum of 10 per month; assess many trades before modifying a method.
  6. Learn in equity cash: The tutor discourages beginners from seeking faster growth through futures or options.
  7. Do not confuse allocation with risk: Cash committed, per-trade stop risk and portfolio-wide exposure are different quantities.
  8. Financial projections remain scenarios: The lesson’s 5%, 8% and 11% hypothetical monthly results are calculations, not expected guaranteed returns.
The repeated routine: Identify setup → Mark entry/SL/target → Fix risk ₹ → Size position → Check cash → Execute and respect SL → Journal → Review
