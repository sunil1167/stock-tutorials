01

The big idea: three charts, three jobs

Why the tutor moves beyond a single timeframe · 00:02–01:06, 20:30–26:31
⌄

By Episode 7, the course has already discussed demand/supply zones, zone strength, base candles, and basic entry, stop-loss and target planning. Previously, a student could mark a zone and plan a trade using only one chart timeframe. This lesson changes the workflow, not the underlying ideas of a supply or demand zone. It distributes the analysis across three timeframes, each answering a different question.

HTF · HigherWhere am I?

Location / curve: decide whether the larger price picture favors considering buying or selling.

ITF · IntermediateIs the trend supportive?

Trending timeframe: confirm whether the current trend agrees with the intended direction.

LTF · LowerWhere is the actual setup?

Execution timeframe: locate the more precise zone and plan entry, stop-loss and provisional target.

Core formula from the classHigher timeframe = location → Intermediate timeframe = trend → Lower timeframe = execution. Do not mistake this for simply checking whether every chart is green: the jobs of the three charts are different.

The tutor presents the approach as a way to align the broad market picture with the prevailing trend and then refine the potential order area. A zone visible on a large chart can be wide; finding its underlying origin on a smaller chart can reduce the entry-to-stop distance while leaving more potential reward available. This refinement is conditional on the smaller zone meeting the execution rules and does not guarantee a winning trade.

Key vocabulary

02

Trend identification: 50 SMA and the clock test

The important opening lesson before multi-timeframe analysis · 07:05–20:23
⌄

The instructor first demonstrates a visual trend-checking method. The chart study is Simple Moving Average (SMA), period 50. On the platform shown in the class, he opens the Add Study / Average menu, keeps the type at Simple, changes the period to 50, and makes the curve thicker for visibility. The precise chart-platform shortcut is not essential; any platform with an SMA(50) can reproduce the teaching method.

Choose the timeframe on which the trend must be checked.

For an actual MTF trade this is the intermediate timeframe (for example, daily for a weekly-purpose trade).

Add the 50-period Simple Moving Average.

Do not substitute an exponential or weighted average while replicating this class demonstration.

Count seven candles back, including the latest/current candle as candle 1.

Mark a vertical reference line through candle 7. This is how the tutor demonstrates his visual comparison.

Mark the intersection of that vertical line with SMA(50).

Draw a horizontal line through the intersection, then compare the moving average's current direction against that reference.

Imagine a clock centered on the marked intersection.

An upward-running average toward the 12-to-3 o'clock quadrant corresponds to an uptrend; a downward-running average toward the 3-to-6 o'clock quadrant corresponds to a downtrend. An approximately horizontal curve is sideways.

The instructor explicitly says that the seventh-candle window and the 50 SMA are his preferred teaching method and calls them mathematically proven. No derivation or backtest appears in the recovered excerpts, so treat that as the tutor's assertion rather than independently established performance evidence. Similarly, the display color is a cue for the curve's direction, not a separate signal.

Do not generalize a timeframe's trendThe tutor gives a concrete example: a stock may look uptrending on weekly yet downtrending on daily. This is exactly why the timeframe on which you check the trend matters.
Why do we count a specific candle rather than look only at the last one?

The instructor wants a fixed historical anchor so the SMA's direction can be seen relative to a horizontal reference. He specifies the seventh candle; he does not teach a universal statistical proof that seven is objectively optimal.

Can a live current candle be counted for the trend check?

Yes, the demonstrated trend check includes the current candle in the seven-candle count. This differs from validating a completed leg-out candle for a zone, which must close first (covered later).

03

Choose the three-timeframe set

HIT / DIT / WIT / MIT and the descending ladder · 26:38–35:30
⌄

The trading purpose determines the highest timeframe. The tutor calls the main purposes hourly, daily, weekly and monthly income trades. These are his labels for the trade's intended time horizon; they are not promises of hourly, daily, weekly or monthly income. He also mentions extending the ladder to quarterly, half-yearly and yearly purposes.

Interactive study tool / Three-timeframe selector

Change your trade purpose

The roles never change. Only the selected timeframes change.

Why the ladder matters

Moving down is not a matter of subtracting one minute from a candle duration. The class teaches a specific practical progression: monthly → weekly → daily → 60-minute → 15-minute → 5-minute. The tutor says 15 minutes is his preferred next step after 60 minutes, rather than arbitrary choices such as 30 or 45 minutes. He also allows 75-minute charts in place of some 60-minute uses and explores 125 and 240 minutes during execution examples. These preferences are as taught, not guaranteed statistically optimal intervals.

Hourly and daily purposes give more frequent opportunities because the underlying bars and zones form more frequently; weekly/monthly purposes are generally more patient. In the closing comments, the tutor encourages practice across the different purposes and particularly suggests that beginners start by focusing on WIT and MIT rather than hunting the fastest setups.

Memory shortcutYour purpose = HTF. Go one step lower for trend, then one more step lower for execution.
04

Higher timeframe: draw the location curve

Fresh demand and supply, proximal lines, thirds and five zones · 38:41–52:16
⌄

Location analysis answers: In what part of the larger demand–supply range is the price situated? The tutor demonstrates the process on a monthly chart for a monthly-purpose trade, but the same process applies to the chosen HTF for any other trade purpose.

Start with CMP.

Draw a horizontal line at current market price. The class allows a live current candle here as a location reference.

Look left and down for the nearest fresh demand zone.

Mark the relevant zone and its proximal boundary, using the course's established zone-marking conventions.

Look left and up for the nearest fresh supply zone.

Mark the corresponding supply zone and its proximal boundary. The tutor emphasizes proximity to CMP and initial freshness when selecting the curve anchors.

Connect the two proximal lines with a retracement tool.

The area between the proximal lines is divided into equal thirds using the 33.33% and 66.67% divisions.

Name all five regions.

Demand-zone interior = very low on the curve; bottom third = low; middle third = equilibrium; top third = high; supply-zone interior = very high.

Important later clarificationThe tutor initially assigns buy/sell actions by the curve region, then explains why trend should always be checked, even when CMP is low or very low. The position of the eventual execution zone on the curve can matter more than the instantaneous position of CMP, which may move across a dividing line during the session.

The retracement is a classification tool, not a prediction that price must reverse exactly at 33.33% or 66.67%. The intent is to organize location, trend and potential execution decisions.

05

Intermediate timeframe: trend confirmation

Turn location into a directional view · 52:16–55:48, 1:05–1:08, 1:18
⌄

Once the HTF curve has been marked, go to the middle timeframe. Do not re-mark the whole curve as if this were another independent location analysis. On the ITF, use the SMA(50)/seven-candle test to classify trend as up, down or sideways. This gives a second input into the intended buying or selling view.

Example A · alignedLow curve + uptrend

HTF suggests demand-side interest; ITF is rising. The instructor treats alignment as a stronger reason to examine lower-timeframe demand zones for longs.

Example B · alignedHigh curve + downtrend

HTF favors supply-side interest; ITF is falling. Examine a qualifying LTF supply zone for shorts.

Example C · equilibriumMiddle curve + trend

The initial curve rule is to follow the ITF trend: up → long interest; down → short interest. No automatic direction in the middle.

Example D · tensionLow curve + downtrend

The larger location suggests demand but the middle trend disagrees. The tutor calls this riskier, not an ordinary high-conviction long.

The instructor also mentions a different, more advanced trend-analysis method scheduled for later lessons. He says it may detect direction sooner than his SMA approach, but the actual method is not taught here. Do not substitute unprovided rules for it.

What if the trend is sideways or against the desired trade?

In the WIT walkthrough, the tutor declines to delve into a sideways-trend case, saying it will be handled later. Similarly, he briefly allows that very-low-curve situations may eventually permit a carefully selected countertrend trade if the execution quality warrants it, but this lesson does not establish a complete countertrend checklist. The teachable baseline is: seek alignment; classify a disagreement as higher risk; do not assume a trade is valid just because one chart suggests buying or selling.

Avoid a frequent mistake“Very low on the curve = buy instantly” is not the complete procedure. Zone location, trend confirmation, execution quality and price arrival still matter.
06

Lower timeframe: refine the execution

Use a smaller, stronger zone inside a supported big picture · 55:48–1:08, 2:13–2:16
⌄

The LTF is where the tutor goes from the directional view to a specific possible entry. For a long, look for the relevant demand zone. For a short, look for the relevant supply zone. The underlying zone types and marking conventions are those taught in earlier classes; the new idea here is that the execution zone must be studied in the context of a higher location and trend.

Two different quality standardsHTF location: a fairly ordinary recognizable demand or supply zone can be used as a broad reference, even without multiple powerful leg-out candles; the tutor even discusses limited prior testing in location marking. LTF execution: apply the stricter quality rules from the earlier classes — freshness, suitable bases, convincing departure/closing behavior, and valid marking. Do not treat an unconvincing location anchor as permission to enter there.

Execution walkthrough

Carry the HTF view and ITF trend forward.

For an aligned long, the HTF location favors demand and the ITF trend is rising. For an aligned short, the location favors supply and the trend is falling.

Go down to the designated execution timeframe.

For MIT this is daily; for WIT the instructor demonstrates the 60/75/125/240-minute family.

Find one or more execution-side zones.

Potential patterns include drop–base–rally for demand, and rally–base–drop for supply. More than one candidate can exist; mark candidates first.

Judge whether each candidate is actually usable.

Check the originating area, completed departure candle, freshness, base and closing strength. The teacher defers a final numeric trade-scoring method to Episode 8.

Plan the trade from the LTF zone, not the full HTF zone.

Entry and stop-loss are derived from the actual chosen execution zone according to the course's earlier rules. The tutor temporarily uses a 2:1 target ratio pending later target-selection rules.

Refining broad location into precise execution

Why a smaller area can help

A high-timeframe candle covers a larger span of price and time. By investigating the origin of a move within it, the instructor looks for a smaller region where pending buying or selling orders may remain. A sufficiently strong and fresh LTF zone can make the entry-to-stop distance narrower than using the entire HTF zone. A smaller stop is meaningful only if it is placed beyond an appropriate invalidation boundary and the trade still satisfies the teacher's rules; a tight stop alone does not improve win probability.

Why not take the trade directly at the higher-timeframe zone?

The teacher's explanation is that an entry at the broad zone can involve a larger stop distance and more imprecise location. The lower timeframe is intended to locate the more specific origin, while retaining support from the higher location and middle trend.

07

Exceptions and edge cases

Missing supply, live candles, damaged zones and a moving CMP · 1:05–1:24, 1:37–1:39, 2:24–2:26
⌄

Case 1 · No fresh supply zone exists above CMP

The tutor uses stocks near all-time highs as an example of a chart with no overhead supply zone available. Do not invent a supply zone just to complete the five-part picture. If one of the two proximal endpoints is missing, the retracement-based curve cannot be drawn as before: skip the three-thirds step. Mark an available demand zone, go to the ITF, and seek a rising trend before considering a long setup on the execution timeframe. The absence of overhead supply is not, by itself, permission to buy.

Case 2 · Location-zone marking is imperfect

The transcript contains an extended discussion of ambiguous base/leg-out markings and whether an apparently fresh higher-timeframe zone may have been slightly touched. The key distinction is that a rough location zone is allowed to establish a broad picture, while an execution-zone candidate has to meet stricter standards. Re-marking or slightly refining the HTF curve may change which third a potential entry occupies without necessarily changing the ITF trend or the original LTF zones; the analysis must still be completed in order.

Case 3 · The currently forming candle

For the trend check, the current candle is counted when finding the seventh candle. For validating a new leg-out candle, the tutor explicitly says the candle must close first. A live candle may end as a base candle or change character before closure. On a monthly chart, a candidate leg-out candle still forming for the month must not be treated as a completed departure. This distinction is easy to miss.

Case 4 · CMP moves between curve regions

The example mentions CMP oscillating around a boundary: one moment “low,” the next “equilibrium.” The tutor argues against changing the whole trading decision every time the quote crosses a dividing line. Keep checking the trend and ask where the planned execution zone itself lies on the curve. Its position is more meaningful than a fleeting tick of CMP.

Case 5 · The HTF location is breached

Near the end, the tutor makes a clear boundary rule for a demand-side example: the lower-timeframe execution must remain within the valid higher-timeframe location. If price has moved beyond the higher demand zone's distal line, that zone has been breached; a lower demand zone sitting beyond the invalidated higher area should not be traded as though it still had the original HTF support. Mirror the principle logically for an invalidated higher supply area, while recognizing the transcript's explicit example was demand-side.

Three especially important “do nots”
  • Do not invent supply when the chart provides none.
  • Do not treat an unclosed candle as a confirmed leg-out.
  • Do not trade an LTF zone based on an HTF area that price has already invalidated.

Practical cases reconstructed from class

MIT, WIT, all-time-high charts, multiple demand zones · ~53:00–2:16
⌄

Worked case A · MIT long idea when price is in equilibrium ~53:00–1:08

  1. The tutor starts on monthly, since this is a Monthly Income Trade. He marks nearest demand below and supply above CMP and divides the price gap into thirds.
  2. CMP is near equilibrium. The curve alone does not settle direction; he moves to weekly for trend.
  3. The weekly SMA trend is judged up. The lesson now has a bullish view supported by the middle chart.
  4. He moves to daily execution and locates candidate demand zones: more than one may be present, but they are not equally suitable.
  5. He separates broad-zone tolerance from strict execution quality. A level can be a recognizable zone yet fail the execution filters.

Lesson: an equilibrium position does not imply “do nothing forever”; it says the trend must choose which side to examine. Even after trend alignment, the execution zone must pass validation.

Worked case B · Monthly chart with no overhead supply ~1:21–1:26

The tutor considers a stock making new highs with no usable fresh supply above. Rather than drawing fictional resistance, he omits the retracement, checks the weekly trend, and looks for a suitable daily demand-side setup only if the middle trend supports buying. A bearish trend would spoil the long view even with a demand zone below.

Worked case C · WIT long, daily trend and several execution charts ~1:33–1:49

  1. Weekly HTF: locate demand and supply or handle absent overhead supply.
  2. Daily ITF: tutor checks SMA trend and finds it pointing up in the demonstrated example.
  3. Execution: he checks 60, 75, 125 and 240-minute charts. In the presented case, possible demand setups are visible across several of them.
  4. He expresses a preference to inspect the 125-minute view for that particular case. He explicitly says a setup does not have to appear on every execution timeframe; seeing one viable setup may be sufficient.
  5. He compares a newly formed, rather weak drop–base–rally to a stronger lower origin zone. Rather than declare any recognizable pattern automatically tradable, he defers final numeric ranking to the next lesson.

Worked case D · Federal Bank: comparing two demand areas ~1:29–1:33

In one discussion, the tutor says the second demand zone looks stronger than the first, partly because the first has an unimpressive close. He also notes that broader market/sector support might sometimes offset one weaker element of a setup; that exception is not fully taught here and refers ahead to later sector analysis. The beginner-safe takeaway from this lesson is to distinguish recognition of a zone from approval of a trade and to avoid applying undefined exceptions.

Worked case E · Short side and trend disagreement ~1:53–2:06

A high-curve location plus a falling middle trend supports looking for short-side execution in a supply zone. But the instructor also walks through situations where the trend is down yet the larger location suggests a demand-side long. He describes such longs as riskier in the merely low region and says an exceptionally strong setup in very low may warrant special consideration later. It is not a rule to ignore trend. Another example illustrates that even with a downtrend, if no valid supply zone exists, the instructor will not simply short for the sake of being in a trade.

09

Trading rules, mistakes and risk management

What to check before calling a setup usable · ~58:00, 1:26, 2:22–2:26
⌄

Rules repeated or made explicit by the tutor

  • Choose trade purpose first; that timeframe becomes HTF.
  • HTF: mark CMP and nearest relevant fresh demand/supply as available.
  • Only divide into thirds when both proximal anchors exist.
  • ITF: always check trend, even when CMP appears near an extreme.
  • Use LTF for actual execution-side zones and entry / stop-loss planning.
  • For execution, prefer stronger, fresh, convincingly departing zones.
  • A live leg-out candle is not confirmed until it closes.
  • Do not rely on a lower zone after the supporting higher zone has been breached.
  • Multiple candidate LTF zones are allowed; final selection requires quality judgment.
  • The closing teaching target is temporarily reward:risk = 2:1; full exit/target rules are intentionally deferred.

Common beginner mistakes

MistakeCorrection from the lesson
“Weekly is rising, so daily must rise.”Check each actual timeframe separately; trends can disagree.
“Price is low on the curve, so I buy immediately.”Check ITF trend and the position/quality of the LTF execution zone.
“Every demand zone is an entry.”HTF location permits ordinary reference zones; LTF entry needs stronger evidence.
“The last candle is green — the departure is confirmed.”Wait for the leg-out candle's close before validating it.
“I must find a signal on 60, 75, 125 and 240 minutes.”The tutor says a viable setup need not be visible on every candidate execution chart.
“A missing supply zone means I can draw one.”Do not fabricate it; skip the two-anchor retracement and lean on trend plus legitimate setup.
“A tighter stop automatically means a better trade.”Refinement matters only if the chosen LTF zone is valid within the HTF picture.

Risk and reward: what is actually taught here

The lesson does not present a complete money-management plan with position sizing, fixed account-risk percentages, or brokerage calculations. It does emphasize avoiding unsuitable zones, keeping stop placement tied to the chosen execution area, and using 2:1 as a provisional target framework. If the entry-to-stop distance is one unit of risk, the illustrated reward distance for early exercises is two units, with the real target method to be taught later. This is a learning ratio, not a guarantee of profitability.

⌄

One-page class summary

Multiple timeframe analysis does not replace demand/supply zones; it assigns distinct responsibilities to three charts. The trading purpose becomes HTF; HTF shows whether price is relatively near demand or supply. Using nearest zones and three equal divisions creates five price-location bands. ITF is where the teacher checks the 50 SMA trend, counting back seven candles, using a vertical/horizontal crosshair and a clock analogy. LTF supplies the detailed zone for entry and stop-loss, with tougher quality rules than the HTF reference zone. The teacher prefers curve and trend alignment, acknowledges difficult exception cases without fully teaching them, uses a tentative 2:1 early target, and defers trade scoring and advanced trend analysis to later classes.

Remember these seven lines1. Purpose picks the HTF. 2. HTF decides location. 3. ITF decides trend. 4. LTF decides execution. 5. Never invent a missing zone. 6. Validate completed departure and HTF support. 7. No setup is guaranteed.
