01What sectoral analysis adds to a trade00:49–06:54 · Foundation⌄

The instructor calls sectoral analysis one of the most important tools in his “Trading in the Zone” method. It is the process of studying the relevant market index or sector index alongside a stock before deciding how much confidence to place in that stock’s demand or supply setup.

He presents this lesson as a two-point component of trade score. This is not a stand-alone buy signal: he keeps the earlier course concepts—freshness, time at base, strength, location, trend, execution, and closing quality—alongside it. His central claim is that a stock setup becomes more compelling when its broader group is positioned to move in the same direction.

Essential principle

A stock’s chart tells you what the stock might do. Its sector chart helps determine whether the stock has support from a larger group of participants.

Two uses of sector analysis

Investment selection

Find sectors and stocks whose positions in their larger market cycles provide attractive accumulation areas; spread holdings across sectors.

Trading confirmation

Use the related index at a demand or supply zone as one more piece of evidence for the directional trade you already identified.

The tutor warns beginners not to buy something because he casually says it looks bullish. At the start he has Sun Pharma open, explicitly says this is not a recommendation to buy a call option, and says to wait for a properly identified trade.

Tutor's emphasis: an aligned sector may help even an ordinary trade; an opposing index may frustrate an otherwise excellent setup. His “six out of seven” / “eight out of eight” expressions are rhetorical examples, not measured hit rates.
02Index composition, weightage and fact sheets07:03–15:39; 32:58–43:40 · Market mechanics⌄

The class starts with Nifty 50, an index built from 50 constituent stocks across banking, financial services, IT, energy, FMCG, metals, healthcare, automobiles and other industries. Those stocks do not contribute equally. A movement in a large-weight constituent can matter far more to the index than a similar movement in a small-weight constituent.

How to check the weights (the tutor’s workflow)

  1. Open the index provider’s Nifty 50 or sector-specific index page.
  2. Find its constituent breakdown and sector allocation, then download the latest fact sheet. Check the sheet's effective month/date rather than assuming the newest web page means newest weights.
  3. Identify the highest-weight constituents; compare their demand/supply areas with the index’s areas.
  4. Keep a monthly updated reference of important weights beside your charting workspace. The tutor suggests a printed sheet or sticky note until the composition becomes familiar.
  5. Use a sector heat map to see which groups and constituents led the current rise or fall. Red/green colors show performance; they are not an entry signal by themselves.

03Index → sector → stock: the support hierarchy15:39–26:09 · Core method⌄

Think in nested circles. For SBI, the tutor discusses Nifty → Nifty Bank → Nifty PSU Bank → SBI. A stock can be related to several indices, but the most specific related index may reflect the stock's activity most closely. He describes Nifty as a broad reference for Nifty constituents, while acknowledging that some stocks behave more independently.

The SBI–Nifty example (2016)

Using historical monthly charts around February 2016, he points to demand zones appearing at approximately the same time in Nifty and SBI. He asks students to imagine three cases:

  1. Nifty at demand; SBI not at demand. A broad index rebound may still support SBI through market participation.
  2. SBI at demand; Nifty neutral. SBI might rise because of its own buyers and nudge the index according to its weight.
  3. Both in demand together. He regards this as stronger mutual confirmation: index buying supports the stock, while the stock contributes to index buying.

Why the timing need not match exactly

He then shows Axis Bank reaching a monthly demand area in January 2016, approximately a month earlier than the broader alignment in February. The zone initially held without an immediate strong rally; other banks and the index were still declining. When broader banking constituents found their own demand areas, Axis Bank’s recovery strengthened. He also mentions ICICI Bank in the same period.

Practical deduction

A stock at demand while its sector continues down may stall, temporarily hold, or even stop out. A later sector reversal may support it, but that later recovery does not cancel an earlier stop-loss.

He names Britannia and Asian Paints as examples of stocks that sometimes appear relatively independent of the broader market. His general preference, however, is to seek market alignment in most situations rather than assume an exception.

04Directional rule: demand for longs, supply for shorts21:35–33:00 · Trade selection⌄

The tutor’s directional filter is very simple: when Nifty, Bank Nifty or a relevant sector is approaching demand, look for good long setups; when it is near supply, look for good short setups. This is a search filter, not a command to enter every stock.

Favourable long context

Stock demand + sector demand + appropriate trend/location/execution and available upside.

Favourable short context

Stock supply + sector supply + appropriate trend/location/execution and available downside.

What if the trade is counter to the index?

He says that a short initiated while Nifty is rebounding strongly from demand must be unusually strong to overcome that headwind; similarly, a long while the relevant index is falling from supply may struggle even to reach a relatively modest target. His example is a stock whose own supply zone temporarily holds for several days while the market continues to rise, until that supply eventually fails.

Earlier course scores still matter

He revisits an earlier trade marked with a good higher-time-frame location, a daily execution zone, and a respectable score. There was still a distinction between finding a promising zone and executing a real trade. He layers location, trend, zone freshness/strength, coincidence, closing, and now sector support. In a sideways or sideways-to-up context he describes taking a relatively quick 2:1 reward-to-risk outcome rather than always expecting a much larger trend target. Clearer trends may justify looking farther, subject to the course’s other rules.

The tutor mentions older course abbreviations (MIT, DDIT, WIT, AIT, QIT). They are retained as course terminology; this transcript does not consistently define each acronym, so this notebook does not guess their full forms.
05Sector rotation: the big market-cycle idea44:15–53:00 · Investing⌄

Sector rotation means that leadership changes. The lecturer draws Metal, FMCG, Auto, PSU Bank, Realty and Pharma at different heights. He is explicit that their vertical order is arbitrary for explanation, not a permanent sector ranking.

A sector at an all-time high today may later decline and another that was deeply depressed may recover. He attributes these cycles to the changing balance of demand and supply in the underlying industries and shares. Examples include autos when vehicle sales change, real estate when property demand changes, and metals when their demand cycle changes.

Why he prefers charts over headlines

In his telling, sector charts often reveal changes in perceived demand before they are widely discussed in public commentary. This is presented as the tutor’s technical-analysis interpretation, not a guaranteed predictive property of charts. He warns that pessimistic headlines may coincide with low prices, while widespread positive sentiment may coincide with late-stage highs.

Sector is not identical to one stock

He contrasts an entire sector with a company: an individual stock can remain weak indefinitely, even while its industry recovers. He uses Yes Bank as an illustration of a troubled company not permanently holding back the larger banking group. He singles out FMCG as comparatively persistent in the historical charts he is showing; that does not make any sector immune to falls.

What to observe, rather than assume

Track whether the sector is high, depressed, consolidating or revisiting a major demand zone. Then inspect the individual stock’s own position: not every constituent moves simultaneously.

06Nifty Metal: a full cycle and aligned stocks49:39–1:13:00 · Case study⌄

On a monthly Nifty Metal chart, the teacher walks through successive rises and deep corrections, citing approximate levels from the historical display: around 2,400 → 3,400 → 1,600 → 3,500 → 1,400 → 4,200. The pedagogical point is the back-and-forth journey between supply and demand—not a permanent up-only or down-only sector.

He examines monthly demand zones and moving averages together. At one point a weaker-looking monthly demand zone appears to be defended in conjunction with an EMA. Weekly and daily candles did not necessarily look impressive. If the remaining major support failed, he believed a deeper correction could unfold in stages.

Compare the sector with constituents

  • Tata Steel: the tutor compares its monthly demand/supply structure with the Metal index, including the Covid-era recovery and later supply-zone reactions.
  • Hindalco: he recalls large multi-fold rallies from former demand areas, a return to those areas, and the possibility of sharp corrections after extended advances. Price levels mentioned belong to the historical chart shown.
  • JSW Steel and other metal stocks: the more of these that reach supportive demand areas together, the more mutually reinforcing the setup appears in his analysis.

One especially useful distinction: an index rising threefold does not mean each stock rises threefold. He contrasts a Metal index move of about 3× with a Hindalco example of around 5×, underlining dispersion between the sector average and a particular constituent.

Approach to tested supply on the way down

When many overhead supply zones have already been revisited, the lecturer calls them “tested”. He thinks that a future bounce from a more consequential demand area may face fewer fresh overhead barriers. He also notes that prices can bounce from nearby zones, resume falling, and later test deeper areas: a zig-zag sequence is possible.

Statements in the class forecasting specific future rallies or corrections were scenarios offered at the recording date, not live forecasts.
07Nifty PSU Bank: from negative sentiment to recovery53:00–1:40:06 · Case study⌄

The lecturer uses Nifty PSU Bank to connect rotation with price-action structure. His historical narration follows a decline toward a depressed low, reactions at demand zones, rallies stopped by successive supply zones, consolidation, and later bullish moves. He repeatedly stresses that the strongly negative commentary near lows made buying psychologically difficult.

Quarterly supply zones were important: a rally could stop at one zone, consolidate, then encounter another; a zone could show separate reactions from two base candles. He marks both a proximal boundary and a distal area rather than treating every resistance level as one exact price.

Canara Bank and Bank of Baroda

He puts Canara Bank and Bank of Baroda (BOB) next to the PSU Bank index. In his reading, quarterly and monthly demand zones continued to attract buyers while some earlier supply areas became tested. He emphasizes the similarity of their structural story, without saying their candles or targets must be identical.

For Bank of Baroda he recalls a Covid gap-down into quarterly demand, a pronounced same-day rebound, followed by more selling and consolidation around an EMA. This illustrates a crucial nuance: a very large bounce is not automatically the end of the correction; supply and demand may contest the same area for months.

Investment and trading are both possible—but different

The tutor alternates between accumulating shares at attractive higher-time-frame zones and using shorter-time-frame trading setups there. He distinguishes his own years of experience from students’ execution requirements. Repeatedly, a zone being plausible for a long-term investor does not waive the need for a defined entry and stop in an active trade.

Historical context

He discusses the period’s Adani/Hindenburg headlines and market panic as examples of news conflicting with chart-based confidence. His speculation about what institutions knew or why news appeared is opinion, not established evidence.

08Building a sector-diversified portfolio1:12:58–1:18:52; 1:40–1:43; 2:47–2:50 · Portfolio⌄

For an investment portfolio the teacher suggests roughly 12–15 stocks (a small deviation is fine), rather than collecting 30–50 without a plan. His suggested starting heuristic is often two to four holdings per sector when the opportunities justify them—not putting the entire portfolio in one depressed sector.

His allocation logic

  1. Inspect each sector’s position in its broader cycle and major demand/supply areas.
  2. Prefer individual stocks available near useful technical levels, not necessarily the stocks that have already enjoyed the biggest rally.
  3. A deeply depressed sector may justify more selections; a sector at an extended high may justify fewer, but not necessarily zero.
  4. Include different sectors so one sector’s correction does not dominate the entire basket.
  5. Build the position gradually rather than deploying all available funds on one day, particularly when a deeper demand zone remains possible.

Numerical illustration used in class: approximately ₹1 lakh across 12 stocks, treated as about ₹8,000 per stock for ease of explanation. Instead of buying ₹8,000 instantly, he might invest roughly ₹4,000 and keep the rest for a lower identified zone. This is illustrative sizing, not a mandatory formula.

He references a “40–40–20” investment method from a separate lesson/video but does not fully explain its stages in this transcript. Therefore it is recorded here as a cross-reference, not reconstructed as a made-up rule.

His SAIL accumulation example

He contrasts buying SAIL after an expensive rally (around ₹154 in his illustration) with considering it near a much lower monthly/quarterly demand area (around ₹65 in his illustration) and keeping further capacity for deeper prices. The point is buying location and staged entries, not a promise that a cheap stock must recover.

09Nifty Pharma: the Sun Pharma divergence42:21–44:10; 1:14:55–1:16:45; 3:06:49 · Case study⌄

The most important lesson in the Pharma example is that an index and a high-weight constituent do not have to peak together. The teacher says Nifty Pharma had already staged a substantial rally toward highs while Sun Pharma had not participated to the same extent.

He suggests that other high-weight constituents—Divi’s Labs, Dr. Reddy’s and Cipla in the example—could have driven much of the earlier index rise. Later, when some of those names weakened, Sun Pharma could recover while the index stayed fairly flat or even softened. Relative movements can offset each other in a weighted basket.

Investment conclusion: an index trading at all-time highs is not by itself a reason to avoid every constituent. Check the individual stock’s technical location, how far it already moved, and the number of similar exposures already in the portfolio. The tutor explicitly corrects a questioner at the end: Sun Pharma was not being offered as an immediate trade just because its chart had been open.

10Auto and FMCG: different historical personalities1:40–1:44; 1:58:58–2:02:23 · Case studies⌄

Auto

Nifty Auto is used to illustrate a sector that had previously suffered large declines, found a major higher-time-frame demand area, consolidated, cleared a moving-average/trend-line region and later reached new highs in the historical chart. He compares the opportunities in Tata Motors and Ashok Leyland with the index. His practical point: an index near a high does not necessarily mean both stocks have risen equally or are equally overextended.

He describes strong follow-up areas, a retest of a larger demand zone, and the danger of buying the most extended leader when the market is already high. Select the stock at a reasonable technical location, not simply the sector’s biggest recent gainer.

FMCG

The teacher presents Nifty FMCG as an example of a historically persistent uptrend in his chart sample, including strong resilience after a Covid-era drop. He attributes this to recurring demand for everyday goods and suggests its quarterly demand zones had held remarkably well on the chart he displayed. This is a historical observation, not a guarantee of future uninterrupted appreciation.

Link with HDFC Bank

He also observes that HDFC Bank’s historical chart showed relatively sustained buying, which he associates with its importance in many large-cap mutual-fund portfolios and regular investment flows. He then switches from that observation to the more specific Bank Nifty study.

11Nifty Realty: DLF and Godrej Properties1:46–1:58:58 · Case study⌄

The real-estate case revisits the high-weight-stock + high-weight-sector idea with DLF, Godrej Properties and Nifty Realty. He looks for overlapping higher-time-frame demand zones on all three, not one isolated line.

Multiple-time-frame reasoning

  1. Yearly and half-yearly: locate major prior supply and deep demand regions.
  2. Quarterly and monthly: decide whether old supply was pierced but not closed beyond, and whether demand regions overlap near the next pullback area.
  3. Weekly: refine the lower edge of a monthly region with a smaller drop-base-rally demand pattern.
  4. Read the descent: if price declines while repeatedly consuming intermediate supply areas, he believes a subsequent rebound may have fewer fresh obstacles.

A crucial phrase is “crossed the supply zone but did not close above it.” The lecturer does not treat an intraperiod break through supply as equal to a confirmed higher-time-frame close. He describes prolonged consolidation (“garbage”) and the possibility of waiting many weeks or months for a deeper entry.

He mentions a personal DLF purchase around ₹123 and speaks about possible targets around ₹655, ₹778 and ₹1,075 derived from historical monthly/quarterly supply interpretation. They are source-era examples, not current levels or actionable forecasts.

Experience caveat from the tutor: he sometimes says his own confidence makes him comfortable acting without an extra confirmation. But he explicitly tells beginners to wait for their regular rules and confirmation instead of copying his entry or trading at current market price without setup.

12The Bank Nifty roadmap: confirming and missing support2:02:23–2:14:52 · Detailed method⌄

This is the lecture’s longest applied analysis. The teacher starts with a constructive reading of HDFC Bank, whose historical weekly supply areas appear tested and whose daily demand has produced a forceful candle. He then opens Nifty Bank and its related groups to test whether the wider system agrees.

What supports the bullish case?

  • A historical MIT setup and associated demand zone had previously helped Nifty Bank and large constituents rebound.
  • Nifty 50 had reached a monthly demand zone during the earlier setup.
  • Nifty Financial Services had also reached relevant demand; HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank were important components.
  • Several weekly/daily demand areas on Bank Nifty and major banks appeared to have reacted as expected in his chart study.

What is missing?

He refuses to assume the index must rally immediately: Nifty Private Bank had not reached the monthly area he wanted, and Nifty PSU Bank and Nifty Bank had not fully tested certain weekly demand boundaries. He explicitly keeps two paths open—an immediate rebound or a further low that completes broader alignment.

The lesson’s strongest analytical habit

Write down both the supporting evidence and the missing confirmations. A compelling chart in one leader does not remove a missing sector test. Reduce aggressiveness until the larger picture agrees.

He then works top down through quarterly → monthly → weekly → daily, searching for coincident demand areas. He highlights a weekly demand origin near a historical golden-crossover region that he regards as having initiated a major rally; several upper demand areas had already reacted. The region’s importance is in the chain of historical reactions, not a magical guaranteed floor.

13Follow-up demand: will a bounce become a larger rally?2:14:52–2:19:05 · Pattern reading⌄

After a sector and multiple constituents reach demand, the lecturer wants to see what the following rally creates. He contrasts weak, messy candles with strong candles that leave behind fresh follow-up demand zones.

If Nifty Bank rises a few hundred points from a demand area but creates only fragile, overlapping candles (“garbage”), the next selling wave might break those minor supports and carry it toward the deeper demand zone. If the rise instead produces a convincing leg-out with good new bases across several banks, the instructor considers the sustained-rally case stronger.

He discusses a possible move toward Bank Nifty 50,000, and a much deeper hypothetical 24,000 zone in a future crash. These are historical, highly confident scenarios from the recording—not forecasts for today or guaranteed support.

Another important price-action distinction

A strong demand zone may sit far below current price precisely because it generated a very large earlier rally. Revisiting it may require a substantial decline. The instructor uses a schematic stock moving from around ₹50 to ₹100 and requiring a return toward ₹50 to retest the original zone, rather than confusing a newly formed shallow zone at ₹98 with the original strong origin.

14Comparing HDFC, ICICI, Axis, AU and IDFC First2:19:05–2:43:17 · Case studies⌄

HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank

For both leaders he relates quarterly, monthly and weekly demand to Bank Nifty’s own zones. Some intermediate areas had already reacted. The question is which remaining zone will coincide with the index and other banks at the next decline. He distinguishes the two stock charts, especially a monthly zone on ICICI Bank that had failed after spending time holding price without broad-sector help.

Axis Bank

The same hierarchy appears: identify quarterly/monthly origins, move to weekly for refinement, and compare the locations with Bank Nifty. Axis is described as another significant constituent that could contribute to a mutually supported rebound when its zones align with the index.

AU Small Finance Bank

AU is a useful counterexample. Its earlier higher-time-frame charts do not show an obvious setup to the tutor at first; he moves lower to monthly/weekly levels and a channel-like structure. Unlike a very heavy index constituent, AU’s direction is more likely to be influenced by Bank Nifty than to determine Bank Nifty’s direction in his interpretation. A local area that already reacted could still produce another bounce if the broader bank index reaches a powerful demand zone at the same time.

IDFC First Bank

He examines available demand regions from half-yearly, quarterly, monthly and weekly views, differentiating a near trading opportunity from a deeper investment accumulation area. He briefly notes that fundamentals, management and track record may add context but does not develop a fundamental valuation system here.

Risk reading: a related sector can rescue a minor or partly tested stock area on some occasions; on other occasions, an unsupported stock zone fails. The lesson is about the quality of confluence, not bypassing stop losses.

15Risk, psychology, investing vs trading1:17; 2:29–2:54 · Mindset & limits⌄

Why the best demand zone can be hardest to buy

He repeatedly points out the paradox: a deep, high-quality demand origin may only be reached after a violent decline, frightening news, and a sharp loss on existing holdings. A strong leg-out means the original zone is far away; a later retest of it can look alarming in percentage terms. He urges students to recognize how price location and emotional pressure interact.

His advice is to do chart work in advance, track major zones, compare constituents, and decide how to manage the trade before headlines overwhelm the decision. He is deeply skeptical of using television and social-media news to override the analysis; that skepticism is his opinion and is not proof that serious news can be ignored safely.

Investing vs trading, as he presents it

Investing

Buy a diversified selection at technically appealing prices; accumulate in stages; less frequent chart-by-chart tracking.

Trading

Use precise entries, defined invalidation/stop, quantity calculated from risk, and frequent monitoring from high to lower time frames. The instructor believes this builds a reusable skill with practice.

He makes exceptionally optimistic claims about capital doubling within a year and even much faster trading growth over time. Those are unverified claims made by the tutor, not expected outcomes. Real investments can lose money, and active trading can experience prolonged losses. Do not plan financial commitments around the numbers from this class.

He also says he prefers direct stock investing to mutual funds for his experienced audience, while suggesting periodic mutual-fund additions near Nifty demand and withdrawals near supply for someone else. These are the speaker’s preferences, not evidence that funds are unsuitable or that timing improves results.

Execution discipline

For live trades, preserve the earlier course’s entry, stop-loss, position sizing and exit plan. This transcript does not supply a universal rupee stop-loss, probability, or guaranteed reward multiple that fits every sector trade.

16Intraday use and the Q&A clarification2:54:30–3:08:50 · Trading application⌄

The tutor closes with the shortest possible rule for active trading: when your stock offers a valid long setup and its relevant index is at a demand zone, add sector support to the trade score. The corresponding logic applies in reverse for short setups at supply.

The two charts do not have to use the same trade pattern or time frame. A DDIT or AIT in an individual bank stock does not require the Bank Nifty chart to show an identical DDIT/AIT. In Q&A, he explicitly says even a sector demand zone on a different time frame—including a small intraday frame—can provide support, provided the sector is actually at that area, not merely moving toward it.

What if the sector is “intermediate” or has not reached demand?

He distinguishes a demand zone on an intermediate time frame (which can count as support) from a sector chart with no demand touch yet (which should not receive the supporting trade-score point). A stock must then stand on its own, and a strong decline across related stocks may overwhelm its nearby zone.

When to be aggressive or conservative

  1. Identify the stock trade through the existing top-down method.
  2. Locate the most relevant broader index and sector demand/supply zones.
  3. Check whether these areas have actually been reached and whether major constituents corroborate the direction.
  4. Look for contradictions and missing tests before deciding your level of aggressiveness.
  5. After any bounce, inspect follow-up demand; do not assume the first green candle guarantees a new uptrend.
  6. Only then use the earlier lessons’ entry, position sizing and stop rules.
Final lesson recap from the tutor

Sectoral analysis helps identify potentially outperforming sectors, leading stocks, more coherent portfolios, additional trade-score confirmation, and an overall market view. He asks viewers to keep practicing and make their own annotated charts.

Rapid revision

The core rules, separated from historical price predictions.

01 / Direction

Sector demand favours looking for valid longs; sector supply favours valid shorts.

02 / Weightage

A large constituent matters more to an index than a smaller constituent.

03 / Confluence

Market, sector and stock demand aligning can strengthen the case; none guarantees a bounce.

04 / Timing

Stock demand can appear earlier than sector demand; the initial bounce may be weak or delayed.

05 / Rotation

Leaders and laggards change. Inspect each sector and constituent rather than buying a label.

06 / Portfolios

The teacher suggests approximately 12–15 names, sector diversification and staged purchases.

07 / Follow-through

After the first bounce, examine the strength of new candles and follow-up demand bases.

08 / Discipline

Keep the original setup, risk sizing and stop plan. Record missing support, not just the bullish clues.
