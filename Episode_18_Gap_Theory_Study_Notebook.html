02 / Core concepts

What is a price gap?

The tutor starts with a basic day-to-day comparison. If a stock closes at ₹100 and opens the next morning at ₹102, ₹103 or ₹104, that is a gap-up opening. If it opens at ₹98, ₹97 or ₹96, it is a gap-down opening. A gap is the difference between the earlier close and the next open. 03:48–04:53

₹100 → ₹103

Gap up

Next opening price is higher than the prior close; the opening difference is +₹3.

₹100 → ₹97

Gap down

Next opening price is lower than the prior close; the opening difference is −₹3.

Real versus fake gaps, as described by the tutor. A real gap reflects the difference between a genuine close and the following session’s genuine reopening. The class calls mathematically caused/adjustment-related gaps “fake” and says these are mainly a concern elsewhere, not in the ordinary Indian cash-market examples being discussed. It briefly mentions commodity rollover-related gaps. This is the tutor’s classroom classification, not an independently verified rule about all markets or adjusted data. 05:20–07:27; 1:48:57–1:50:10

Read accurately

An opening gap does not tell you whether the whole new candle will be green or red. Classification initially depends on where the session opens. Subsequent movement may be very different. The tutor demonstrates this explicitly for outside gaps.

03 / Candle geometry

Inside gaps vs outside gaps

Compare the next candle’s opening with the entire previous candle, including its upper and lower wicks—not merely its body. ~07:30–18:50

Inside gap
The next candle opens at a price different from the previous close, but inside the previous candle’s high-to-low range.
Outside gap
The next candle opens above the previous high or below the previous low, outside that entire range.
After the open
Even if the new candle later moves back inside the old range, its opening remains classified as outside.
Not a mechanical filter
A meaningful inside gap can still matter when the difference between close and open is clear. A tiny outside gap may add little.
  1. Draw the previous candle’s highest wick and lowest wick as two horizontal guides.
  2. Locate the next candle’s opening price; do not use its eventual closing price for this question.
  3. If open is inside those guides, label it inside; if beyond either guide, label it outside.
  4. Then ask whether the gap is significant relative to the chart. The tutor does not give a fixed rupee or percentage threshold.
Why a strict “outside only” rule is misleading

The tutor compares candle examples where one gap technically remains inside the prior wick range but still leaves a visibly large close-to-open difference. He argues that such cases may be practical even without satisfying the textbook outside-gap definition. Conversely, a negligible opening difference should not be promoted to an important signal merely because it clears an earlier wick by a tiny amount. 17:55–19:22; 1:51:20–1:52:26

What if I cannot tell by eye?

Use a horizontal reference line through the earlier candle’s high or low. The tutor shows that even adjacent red candles, or adjacent green candles, can be confusing to judge without a guide. 12:52–13:51

04 / Trend context

Novice gap vs pro gap

Here the relevant comparison changes: not one candle against another, but the gap direction against the prevailing trend. These are classroom names. 15:54–25:00

Novice gap · with trend

Price rises in an uptrend and gaps up; or price falls in a downtrend and gaps down.

Pro gap · against trend

Price rises in an uptrend but gaps down; or price falls in a downtrend but gaps up.

Uptrend + gap up = novice gap; the opening moves in the same direction as the prevailing trend.
Important correction from the tutor: Do not call a gap “novice” only because two neighboring candles happen to be green. A few green base candles do not erase the wider downtrend. Read trend first. 23:50–24:49
Can a novice/pro gap also be inside or outside?

Yes. Inside/outside describes opening position relative to the previous candle range; novice/pro describes gap direction relative to the trend. They answer different questions. The class warns against demanding an outside gap in every usable case. 16:20–18:45; 1:51:20–1:51:50

05 / The trading framework

The tutor’s four gap applications

These are the heart of the class. In the tutor’s framework, a gap is an additional point of confirmation around an already high-quality demand/supply zone; it is not a standalone reason to trade. The examples are mostly discussed using demand zones for long positions and supply zones for short positions, but the principle is mirrored. ~28:00–46:00; 1:52:27–1:55:08

Application 1: after an earlier demand zone forms, a novice gap down delivers price back into it. This is the initial, pre-entry gap clue.

How to apply the framework without over-trading

  1. Choose the setup first. Identify a genuine demand or supply zone and assess the trend, the higher time frame, its base/exiting candle quality, and relevant sector/index context.
  2. Locate the gap at the right time. “Into” means price is entering a zone; “from” means price has already reached the zone and now gaps away.
  3. Prefer a visibly meaningful gap. A five-paise difference on a chart should not be treated as exceptional just because it qualifies by definition.
  4. Separate early evidence from later validation. An into-zone novice gap can be known as the entry arrives; a from-zone pro gap is observed afterward.
  5. Do not chase a four-part fantasy. The tutor explicitly assigns finding a real combined example as homework, not as a mandatory entry rule.
Nuance: does the gap need to open exactly on the zone boundary?

Not necessarily. In an example the tutor allows the gap-opening price to be slightly outside the boundary, provided the candle enters the relevant zone and the gap is meaningful. He is asking for practical understanding, not point-by-point geometric perfection. 30:48–31:12

Nuance: does the retest have to happen on the first gap candle?

In the Q&A, the tutor says an entry on the second candle can still count as a novice-gap-into-zone context when the previous gap clearly set up that arrival. The underlying sequence matters more than an arbitrary midnight/candle boundary. 2:05:33–2:06:21

Nuance: what if “pro gap zone” is debatable?

The tutor argues that adding the word “pro” does not change the core trade. If the distinction is ambiguous, focus on whether the supply/demand zone itself is genuinely high quality and whether a proper gap helped price enter it. 55:59–56:47; 1:23:40–1:24:16

06 / Chart-reading practice

What the real-chart walkthroughs teach

Throughout the class, the tutor moves between examples from Nifty, Bank Nifty, and individual stocks. The point is not a fixed price level or a universal ticker rule, but repeated recognition of the same structures in multiple charts and time frames. ~46:00–1:26; ~1:39–1:48

Bank Nifty demand zone

The tutor discusses a strong zone and an MIT trade in which price arrived with a novice gap. He cautions against reading small gaps in isolation when the underlying demand area is not valid. A nearby horizontal level can help locate the structure. 1:13:55–1:18:30

Many components opening at demand

He describes an index gap down while its significant constituents and related market pieces simultaneously open near their own demand zones. He sees stronger possible recovery when several independent conditions align than from one attractive candle. 49:35–50:10

Higher-time-frame context

A daily pattern takes on more meaning when it aligns with relevant weekly/monthly zones, the broader trend and sector direction. He repeatedly invokes top-down analysis and when to be aggressive versus conservative. 1:36:37–1:37:51; 2:04:47–2:05:25

Historical examples as homework

The instructor invites students to search charts at different time frames for two or three components of the combined setup and, if found, a clean example of all four. This is pattern-recognition practice, not a demand to trade them live. 1:54:54–1:56:22

Common misreading

“This candle gap-opened into something, therefore it must be an excellent zone.” The tutor explicitly rejects that approach. A strong gap cannot compensate for weak trend alignment, a poor or fictitious zone, or an unsuitable overall setup.

07 / Special attention

Window gaps and missed entries

This is the long practical segment late in the lesson. A strong departure candle can gap away from the base, leaving a window between the base and the later candle’s opening/wick region. When price returns, it may react at that window without reaching the original base-zone entry line. 1:26:21–1:48:00

Chart 5 · Why price may turn before the original entry

Visual comparison: a traditional proximal/base entry versus a possible window-gap-adjusted entry.

A return can touch the window gap and rebound above the proximal/base entry. This explains some missed entries; it is not automatic permission to move orders higher.

The tutor’s reasoning

  1. First a base forms: price pauses and buyers and sellers compete. A strong rally follows.
  2. The departure candle opens higher than the base area and may create a lower wick. Its lower body/wick region can act as another observed buying origin in the tutor’s interpretation.
  3. On the return, price may reverse from the window, possibly between the base high and the departure-candle low, so the old entry at the base is not triggered.
  4. If the overall trade is excellent and the window is very close to the ordinary entry, the tutor sometimes permits adjusting the entry toward it.
  5. The original zone-based stop-loss reference does not automatically move up with the entry. Recalculate the distance to stop and the reward-to-risk before acting.

When an adjustment is plausible

Well-formed zone; strong departure/exciting candle; compelling top-down, trend and sector context; window near the usual entry; risk-to-reward still acceptable. 1:35:29–1:37:51

When to leave the original order

Window is far from the base; stop becomes very wide; target-to-risk worsens sharply; or the trade itself inspires low conviction. If the trade is missed, let it go. 1:33:33–1:36:05; 1:40:34–1:41:21

The class’s concrete reward-to-risk comparison: a setup giving about 2:1 from the original entry might fall to about 1.7:1 after an aggressive window-gap adjustment. The tutor says to keep the original entry rather than degrade the trade just to avoid missing it. 1:35:48–1:36:05
Does a window gap always mean “pro gap”?

No. The tutor explicitly contrasts a reversal pattern (which may yield a pro gap) with a continuation pattern (which can yield a novice gap). “Window” describes the space and its use in entry planning; “novice/pro” describes direction relative to the trend. 1:37:52–1:38:41

What if the departure candle has no lower wick?

In a student’s question, the tutor confirms that for the illustrated gap-up situation, if the candle has no lower wick, the reference may be its lower body/opening area instead. Apply this in the specific strong-window/zone context taught, not to every gap-up candle. 1:32:54–1:33:05; 2:00:09–2:00:27

What if price already bounced from the window once?

The instructor warns that subsequent visits should not automatically be treated with the same confidence. Even if reactions can occur, he discourages taking unnecessary risk after the area has already produced a move. 1:33:11–1:33:28

How does the idea work in supply zones?

Mirror the picture for a supply zone: price may rally toward the gap below a strong downward departure and then turn down without reaching the original supply-zone entry. The tutor says similar missed-entry cases are common in supply-chart examples too. 1:59:04–1:59:53

08 / Protect the process

Trading rules, errors and risk control

Episode 18 does not establish a new universal fixed-stop or fixed-target formula for gap trading. It relies on the course’s existing zone-marking, top-down, trade-score and reward-to-risk framework. The explicit caution is especially sharp when moving an entry nearer a window. 30:48–31:35; 1:33–1:36; 2:00–2:05

Before acting on a gap
  • Mark a legitimate demand/supply zone first.
  • Check the higher-time-frame view.
  • Identify the prevailing trend rather than candle color.
  • Consider sector and index agreement.
  • Ask if the gap is visually meaningful.
  • Check timing: into zone or from zone?
  • Know entry, true zone stop, target and reward-to-risk.
Mistakes the class warns against
  • Inventing demand zones to justify a gap.
  • Counting microscopic gaps as high-quality signals.
  • Confusing novice/pro with green/red candle pairs.
  • Calling any gap-off-base a tradable window.
  • Moving entry a long distance while leaving stop fixed.
  • Shortening stop without a structural reason.
  • Chasing a missed fill out of fear.

One trade, two entry locations

Consider a purely illustrative demand-zone example: the zone’s original entry would be near the base; a newly identified window sits slightly above it; the stop remains below the original zone. A higher entry increases the rupee distance to that stop, so the position-size/risk implications and reward-to-risk must be rechecked. The lesson supplies the directional rule and one 2:1 versus approximately 1.7:1 comparison, not a position-sizing equation or a mandated risk percentage.

The most valuable risk rule here

Missing a trade is acceptable. The tutor repeatedly argues that fear of missing a move is a poor reason to change your entry. Confidence in the setup, proximity to the original entry, and acceptable reward-to-risk should govern any adjustment. 1:35:48–1:36:05; 1:40:34–1:41:21

09 / End-of-class clarifications

Selected tutor Q&A

These practical questions appear during the class’s closing discussion. They help interpret ambiguous situations without reducing the method to overly strict labels.

Q. Is it okay if the second candle, not the gap-opening candle, reaches the zone?

A. Yes, the tutor allows it if the overall gap-into-zone sequence remains clear. The candle boundary alone does not cancel the preceding novice-gap context. 2:05:33–2:06:21

Q. Can I use a tighter stop at the window candle if my sector is supportive?

A. The tutor calls this risky in the example of a window far above the original base. Sector support does not automatically justify placing the stop at a new, shallower wick; he refers back to the original zone stop. 2:00:33–2:01:27

Q. Which factors most strengthen a bullish or bearish bias?

A. He emphasizes three: higher-time-frame (top-down) support, trend and sector. These also influence whether to be aggressive or conservative. 2:04:47–2:05:25

Q. Does a strong gap from a zone validate an entry that already happened?

A. It can add confidence after the fact, but was not available as proof at the original entry. The tutor even discusses that later confirmation may change an existing exit/holding assessment, but it should not be backdated. 42:15–42:49; 2:06:26–2:06:44

Q. Should I always use a pro-gap classification if a few candles look opposite?

A. No. View the relevant trend and the zone’s quality. A new “pro” label on a minor base fluctuation does not improve a poor trade. 24:24–24:49; 55:59–56:47; 1:23:47–1:24:16
