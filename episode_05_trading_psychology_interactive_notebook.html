The real agenda of Episode 5

⌄

This lesson is titled Trading Psychology | Your Mind vs News, but the instructor actually devotes the first major part to practical demand/supply-zone analysis. Only later—at approximately 1:51:25—does the dedicated discussion about news, earnings announcements and emotional traps begin. Reading only the news section would leave out much of the tutor's argument.

Part I · technical structure

Why a zone may hold

Base-candle count, explosive departures, marking boundaries, hidden bases on smaller timeframes, retests and monthly zones.

Part II · trader's mindset

Why headlines can mislead

Good news does not force a rally; bad news does not guarantee a fall. Use concrete historical examples to understand emotional mistakes.

Vocabulary before we begin

Demand zoneA price area the tutor associates with strong buying or buy orders; often drawn near the origin of an upward move.
Supply zoneAn area associated with strong selling or sell orders; often drawn near the origin of a downward move.
Leg-in / base / leg-outThe approach to a zone, the pause or fight between buyers and sellers, and the directional departure.
Proximal line (PL)The nearer boundary of a zone—the first edge approached by returning price.
Distal line (DL)The farther boundary; the tutor locates a protective stop beyond it, with room.
Fresh / testedFresh means price has not returned to use the marked zone since departure. Tested means it has returned.
How this notebook treats the tutor's ideas: statements about pending institutional orders, news manipulation, or zones being “powerful” are the instructor's interpretation, not certainties about markets. Historical examples are narrated as the instructor described them; diagrams below use illustrative synthetic candles, not live or historically accurate charts.

ZONE QUALITY05:01–12:51

Which zone is stronger? One base candle versus four

⌄

The tutor begins by comparing two pictures. Their leg-in and leg-out are identical. In picture A, price pauses for just one base candle; in picture B, it pauses for four. He considers A the more powerful zone when all other factors are equal.

Diagram 1 · the same departure, different pause

Toggle the number of base candles. Only the duration of the base changes.

With one base candle, the buyer–seller contest ends quickly, suggesting a more decisive outcome in the tutor's comparison.

Bullish candle Bearish candle Base region

His wrestling / cricket analogy

If two competitors continue fighting for a long time, both may be strong. A drawn-out contest does not decisively demonstrate one side's superiority. If one side wins very quickly, the instructor views it as evidence of a larger imbalance. He transfers that analogy to candles: few base candles = shorter struggle; more base candles = prolonged two-sided struggle.

Not a rigid maximum

  • One or two base candles are especially attractive in his explanation; he says two are more than enough and three can still be strong.
  • Four or five are not automatically rejected. An unusually explosive or multi-candle leg-out may compensate for the longer base.
  • A zone's quality should not be decided from candle count alone.
Tutor's two-part strength test
  1. Minimum base candles, preferably narrow in range.
  2. Explosive and/or multiple leg-out candles, signaling a pronounced departure.

He repeatedly asks students to assess the combination, rather than search for one magical number.

Worked comparison — illustrative

Imagine two demand setups that both rise sharply after forming. A rests for a single candle while B forms four small sideways candles. Under the instructor's stated controlled comparison, A ranks above B. If B instead has a materially stronger, longer departure than A, that new information must be evaluated; the original comparison no longer has equal conditions.

CHART READING13:02–24:48

Mark the zone: body-to-wick versus wick-to-wick

⌄

The tutor revisits body-to-wick marking and introduces wick-to-wick marking. Both use proximal and distal boundaries. Wick-to-wick includes the extreme wick on the near side as well as the extreme wick on the far side, creating a wider zone than a comparable body-to-wick marking.

Diagram 2 · marking a demand zone

Switch the near boundary. The distal low stays the same.

In the illustrated demand setup, proximal = highest body in the base; distal = lowest wick.

Boundary rules as explained in the demand examples

MethodProximal (near)Distal (far)
Body-to-wickHighest relevant body of the base candlesLowest wick of the base candles
Wick-to-wickHighest wick of the base candlesLowest wick of the base candles

For a supply zone the orientation is reversed: the near edge is at the bottom of the base and the far boundary at the top. The instructor also illustrates similar marking ideas for rally-base-drop supply patterns. The exact near-edge choice requires inspecting that particular base, not mechanically copying a demand diagram.

When does he favor wick-to-wick?

He discusses using it when the setup looks especially clean: few, narrow base candles and strong explosive or repeated leg-out candles. An overly long wick can be a reason to stay with body-to-wick. He warns that practice across chart examples is important and does not give a universal “always choose wick-to-wick” rule.

Entry and protective stop — the tutor's example

For demand he describes an entry slightly above the proximal line and a stop beyond the distal line with some room. Changing body-to-wick into wick-to-wick may move the entry, while the distal line—and hence the stop reference—can remain unchanged. He recommends going to a smaller timeframe for a more precise placement when appropriate. He does not provide a universal percentage buffer or fixed target here.

Important distinction

Choosing a wider area can lead to an earlier touch, but it also changes the trade geometry. A wick touch and a deeper body touch are not two independent fresh opportunities by default. The tutor explicitly addresses that risk in the next concept.

04
DISCIPLINE19:23–24:03

When is the zone already tested?

⌄

Suppose a trader marks a demand zone wick-to-wick. Price comes down, touches that zone, and rallies. Later, price returns deeper and finally touches the area that would have been included in body-to-wick marking. Should the trader treat the inner touch as a second fresh setup?

The tutor's answer is no, if the original wick-to-wick version was genuinely judged suitable and already triggered. The first touch tested that zone. He notes that prices can sometimes react again after a deeper return, but says not to base a repeat entry on that possibility alone.

Decision example
  1. Before the return, decide that the clean, narrow, explosive base qualifies for wick-to-wick marking.
  2. Price touches your wick-to-wick proximal line and responds upward.
  3. Label the zone tested, even if the price did not reach the inner body-to-wick boundary.
  4. Do not retrospectively relabel it “fresh” simply because a different marking method offers another chance.
Core principle

Choose the marking method before the reaction; be consistent when judging whether the zone has been consumed/tested. Avoid changing rules after the move to justify an entry.

PRACTICAL EXCEPTION25:20–51:02

What if no base candle is visible?

⌄

The earlier definition of a zone was leg-in → base → leg-out. The tutor then asks what happens when a chart looks like an abrupt reversal: an incoming bearish candle is followed directly by an outgoing bullish candle, without an obvious sideways base on the current chart.

His explanation is that an imbalance and an originating area can still exist even if the current candle aggregation does not display a neat base candle. He suggests examining a smaller timeframe to reveal a possible base and refine the boundary.

Diagram 3 · uncover a hidden base

Select a teaching step to see why the smaller timeframe matters.

On the larger timeframe, the reversal seems to jump from a bearish candle into a bullish one without a base.

The tutor's workflow

  1. Identify a significant upward or downward move and locate the turning area on the original chart.
  2. If a clear base is missing, inspect the next smaller timeframe. He mentions monthly → weekly or daily, weekly → daily, and daily → intraday frames (examples include 125-, 75-, 240-, and 120-minute views).
  3. Look for a base candle or group of candles near the origin of the move. Mark the appropriate near and far boundaries.
  4. If a smaller timeframe does not yield a tidy base, he also explores capturing the lower overlap of the adjoining reversal candles on the same timeframe; this is contextual and should not be mistaken for a universally specified automatic formula.
  5. Keep the original far price extreme in view; refinement often changes proximal placement, not the origin distal.

What not to do

He cautions against switching through ever more arbitrary minute values just to keep moving the line in your favor. At the time of this class, he says a more standardized timeframe selection will be taught later; do not invent a promised fixed timeframe table from this lesson.

Illustrative use case

A daily chart appears to show a sharp red candle followed by a powerful green candle. On a 75-minute chart, one small pause candle becomes visible near the reversal. Rather than assuming the daily chart contains no tradeable area, the tutor uses the lower timeframe to understand the origin. A clean lower-timeframe area is still only a candidate setup; actual outcomes can differ.

MARKET STRUCTURE52:31–1:12:00

Why monthly zones can matter more than daily zones

⌄

The tutor compares three otherwise identical pictures drawn on daily, weekly, and monthly charts. Because the structure looks the same, his question is: which formation represents the stronger buyer–seller imbalance?

His answer is that a higher-timeframe structure carries more weight in this comparison, with monthly ahead of weekly and daily. The argument is aggregation: a monthly candle reflects the buying and selling across roughly 20–22 trading days (depending on the month), while a weekly candle aggregates several sessions and a daily candle just one.

Diagram 4 · what one candle compresses

One tall candle may represent many sessions of opposing trades.

A monthly candle summarizes a month of trading; its apparent single green body does not imply that every session was green.

Decode a monthly green candle

A green monthly candle does not mean every day was bullish. Some days might have seen sellers in control; the final open-to-close result indicates that buyers won overall. Likewise, a monthly base candle describes a lengthy contest that did not resolve into a comparable directional close for that month.

Why the tutor cares about the travelled distance

He compares illustrative movement represented by a large timeframe candle with comparatively small movement on a daily or intraday candle. A wide, decisive move spread over a longer timeframe can encode a more meaningful extended battle; the same apparent shape is not the same amount of time or price action.

In the class's framework

For otherwise comparable leg-in/base/leg-out structures: monthly > weekly > daily > intraday in presumed significance. This is a teaching heuristic, not a guarantee that every monthly zone survives and every intraday zone breaks.

How he interprets pending orders

For demand zones, he theorizes that orders may remain unfilled through a strong departure and can influence a return; for supply zones, he uses the parallel idea of pending sell orders. These are not quantities verified from the visible candles. A chart cannot on its own prove how many individual institutional orders are resting at a price.

Worked examples from the practical chart section

⌄

During the long chart walkthrough, the tutor uses real-market stories to reinforce the same concepts: he shows how an apparent news-driven move can run into a previously identified higher-timeframe zone and how multiple NIFTY demand zones behaved during later pullbacks.

Example · PNB / PSU bank recapitalisation

A strong positive day still has a supply area overhead

Near 1:18, he discusses an episode of government recapitalisation of public-sector banks. He describes a very large single-day move in PNB—roughly 45% up in his account—along with upper-circuit-like strength across PSU bank names. His point is that even a spectacular upward move may run toward a powerful supply zone; the news does not erase that chart area.

What to observe: previous supply, the arriving candle's unusually strong buying pressure, the proximal/distal zone, and whether price reaches the nearer boundary or pushes deeper before reacting. The reference to PNB is historical, not a present-day live price signal.

Example · NIFTY monthly demand

Repeated higher-timeframe demand zones

Starting near 1:25, he returns to monthly NIFTY charts. One zone is discussed around 6,800 (in the old chart context), followed in his explanation by price moving toward 9,100–9,200. He highlights clean, narrow bases and consecutive strong monthly leg-outs, arguing that these zones carry greater importance than nearby smaller-timeframe structures.

He also recalls people predicting much deeper crashes (for example, extreme NIFTY forecasts) and contrasts those headlines with his reading of monthly demand areas. Do not treat any old index level as a usable price target today.

Example · COVID-19 sell-off

What changes during exceptional selling pressure?

Near 1:35, he discusses the 2020 COVID-19 crash and unusually strong selling that penetrated areas where a standard first-touch trade may otherwise have been expected. He describes waiting for a lower point rather than mechanically entering at the first area when selling pressure looked extraordinary.

Lesson: an apparently strong monthly zone can face an exceptional market-wide shock. The instructor's examples include successful holds and zones where stops were reached. This is why chart zones should not be interpreted as certainty or used without controlled downside.

Example · NBFC stress and corrections

A failed level does not imply every level fails

Around 1:39–1:45, he recalls financial-sector turbulence, including NBFC-related selling, and discusses a NIFTY monthly demand area where a stop was hit while a lower demand zone subsequently held. He treats corrections as a normal part of market movement, not proof that an entire long-term thesis is finished.

What to observe: sector concentration, pace of selling, nearby higher-timeframe zones, and the outcome of each level separately. His own example allows for losing trades.

PSYCHOLOGY1:51:25–2:03:31

Your mind versus the news: the central argument

⌄

The dedicated psychology segment asks students to separate what a headline says from what price actually does. A company may announce excellent earnings and still decline; a company may report trouble and then rebound. The tutor wants the trader to identify pre-existing demand and supply zones rather than react impulsively to headlines.

Diagram 5 · headline versus chart location

Two hypothetical market reactions; neither is a historical price series.

Positive news can accompany a rally toward an overhead supply zone, where the price may stall or reverse.

Three questions the tutor wants you to stop asking

  • “The result is good, so should I immediately buy?”
  • “The result is bad, so should I immediately sell?”
  • “If good results can produce falls, should I always short good news?”

He explicitly rejects flipping the simplistic rule upside down. The correct takeaway within his approach is not “buy every bad headline and short every good one.” It is to avoid deriving the trade direction from the headline alone.

The flat-selling analogy

Imagine wanting to sell your flat in a housing society. You may first tell people inside the society because potential buyers are nearby; outsiders learn later. The tutor uses this to argue that market insiders may be positioned before public company news. As an educational analogy, it explains why a public announcement is not necessarily the beginning of price discovery; it does not prove insider trading in any particular example.

Headline-response discipline

Headline → emotion → impulsive trade is the loop he wants students to challenge. His preferred sequence is identify the major zone → examine price approach → decide whether the setup qualifies → use a defined stop. A news release alone is insufficient evidence for a buy or sell.

Case studies: HDFC Bank, Infosys and corporate news

⌄
HDFC / HDFC Bank merger announcement

Good news, a strong initial move, then supply

In the recorded example (around 2:03–2:05), the tutor describes excitement after the HDFC and HDFC Bank merger news. A green candle initially appeared very strong, but as it reached a weekly supply zone it lost momentum and subsequently moved down. His point is that a favorable story is not a guarantee of uninterrupted upside.

Reading exercise: before looking at the news, mark the higher-timeframe supply zone; then observe where the burst of buying enters it. This example is a narrated classroom chart, not the diagram's literal price path.

Infosys · executive resignation on 18 August 2017

Bad news, selling into an existing demand area

Near 2:11–2:13 the tutor shows Infosys and refers to Vishal Sikka's resignation. He describes a large red candle associated with the news that reached a demand region, after which the chart in his example recovered. The purpose is to question an automatic “CEO leaves → company is finished → sell” reaction.

He emphasizes that large companies have organizational succession and internal context, while retail traders may first encounter a story at publication time. That observation does not mean every executive departure is harmless or that every falling stock must rebound.

Other corporate-story examples

Earnings, product trouble and investigations

He names quarterly earnings, an Axis Bank example, the situation where a company's product is discontinued (Nestlé is mentioned), a bank facing a CBI investigation, and the possibility of a management resignation. The lesson is to ask where price sits relative to chart structure, not jump from a headline to a total-company conclusion.

He separately mentions exceptional serious fraud situations such as Yes Bank as reminders that major underlying problems can exist. The lesson must not be simplified into “ignore all corporate risk.”

A caution about the instructor's narrative: he frequently describes news as being used to move retail traders into traps. This is his market-psychology interpretation. Some moves can reflect genuine new information, changing valuations or systemic risk; the transcript passages do not independently verify deliberate manipulation in these named events.

APPLY THE IDEA2:14:00–2:24:30

Psychology: refuse the emotional short-cut

⌄

Retail traders often encounter public news after a move has already begun. The tutor argues that chasing the emotional story can leave traders buying near local highs or selling near local lows. His practical remedy is to keep chart location and predefined rules central.

The reaction he criticizes

  1. A large green candle appears alongside good news.
  2. The trader experiences urgency, feels the move will continue, and buys immediately.
  3. Price reaches a supply area; late buying is vulnerable if profit-taking or selling occurs.

The process he advocates

  1. Find higher-timeframe supply and demand zones before making a forecast.
  2. Assess the base, leg-out, freshness and approach into the zone.
  3. Set a boundary and protective exit before taking any trade.

Do not over-learn the lesson

The claim is not that every good result triggers a fall, every bad result a rally, or that reading financial disclosures has no value for long-term investment. Rather, the tutor is targeting emotion-led, short-term entries made from headlines without understanding the price location.

Mindset checkpoint

If you removed today's headline, would the chart setup still meet your original criteria? If the answer is no, the position may be driven more by excitement or panic than by the lesson's zone method.

What the tutor says about winners and losers

He discusses the idea of trading as a competitive setting, saying that many participants lose while some make money, and encourages students to change unproductive habits. The particular percentages he quotes in the live lecture should be read as his motivational framing rather than independently established statistics or a promise of profitability.

HOMEWORK2:25:00–2:31:08

A source-grounded practice routine

⌄

Near the end, the instructor asks students to open multiple stocks on a monthly timeframe and study which demand and supply areas worked and which failed. This is homework about observation—not permission to invest blindly in every marked zone.

Weekend chart exercise

  1. Open a watchlist of stocks. Switch each chart to monthly.
  2. Mark obvious rally-base-rally, drop-base-drop and related clean origin structures.
  3. Count base candles, note their width, and inspect the leg-out strength.
  4. Compare body-to-wick versus wick-to-wick marking where appropriate; decide which you would have selected before a return.
  5. Look for any case where no clean base appears and inspect a smaller timeframe.
  6. Record fresh, tested, holding and failed examples; do not discard losing examples from your study set.
  7. Finally, note large news events on the chart and ask whether they forced price to ignore the prior zone—or whether a zone still mattered.

MASTER THE LESSONSynthesis of the lesson

Revision checklist & teaching recap

⌄

The story in one paragraph

A zone represents a contest between buyers and sellers. In the instructor's framework, a short, narrow base with an explosive leg-out suggests a decisive imbalance. Correct marking depends on the base structure, and a wick-to-wick zone should not be called fresh again after a valid first touch. Apparent no-base reversals can be examined on smaller timeframes. Higher-timeframe areas, especially monthly ones, summarize more trading activity. This is why the instructor prefers to analyze price location and predefined zones over reacting emotionally to company results, mergers, resignations and negative headlines.
