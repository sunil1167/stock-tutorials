02 / Mechanics

What does a moving average actually do?

1:02:47–1:04:06

A simple moving average is the average closing price of a chosen number of recent candles, recalculated whenever a new candle arrives. The tutor uses the 50-period SMA to understand trend.

The formula explained in class
SMA(50) = sum of latest 50 closes ÷ 50

Daily chart → 50 daily candle closes. Weekly chart → 50 weekly closes. A new candle enters; the oldest candle leaves.

Interactive mini-example — a 5-candle SMA

The instructor teaches with a five-day calculation before returning to the 50-day SMA. Move the slider to see which closing price is added, which is removed, and why the average can rise even after the latest price drops.

Repeat every candle. The moving average is a series of rolling averages, not a fixed horizontal level.
03 / Why smoothing is delayed

Price turns first. The average may turn later.

1:07:52–1:26:55

The tutor emphasizes a counterintuitive fact: a falling close does not necessarily mean a falling moving average. What matters to the rolling average is the difference between the new price entering and the old price leaving.

When the average rises

The new close can be lower than yesterday's price, yet higher than the oldest close being replaced. Then the sum of the 50-bar window rises.

Classroom logic“New number added is higher than old number removed” → the average moves up.

When the average falls

As the rolling window advances, older high closes may finally be removed and replaced by lower closes. The average can start falling even after price has already rallied or stabilized.

Key insightThe moving average reacts with a delay because it includes many historical candles.
SituationWhat entersWhat leavesEffect on SMA
Price dipped, SMA risingA lower close than yesterday, but still above an old closeOld low closeRises
Price bounced, SMA fallingA new close still below an old closeOld high closeFalls
New close equals old closeEqual numberEqual numberUnchanged

This is arithmetic, not a prediction. The tutor's long five-candle example demonstrates precisely this mechanism.

Why smaller windows change direction more quickly

Compare a two-period average with a 50-period average. In the two-period case, each new observation replaces half of the data. In the 50-period case, it replaces only one fiftieth. The tutor uses this contrast to show why a smaller moving average is more sensitive and the 50-bar trend measure is slower.

For example, the class gives the two-day average of 100 and 110 = 105, then shows how a later rolling pair moves to 115. These values illustrate responsiveness, not a recommended 2-period trading strategy.

Source pointer: ~1:18:34–1:19:32.

04 / Cross-timeframe reading

“50 periods” means 50 candles on that timeframe

1:26:55–1:31:23

The tutor addresses a common confusion: the same security can show an uptrend on a daily chart while still showing a downtrend on a weekly chart.

Chart timeframeSMA(50) includesWhat that means
15-minute50 fifteen-minute closesRelatively frequent new readings
75-minute50 seventy-five-minute closesSlower evolution than 15-minute
Daily50 daily closesCan differ from the weekly reading
Weekly50 weekly closesChanges gradually because only one new weekly candle arrives per week
Monthly50 monthly closesCan take a long time to shift
Practical implication for planned tradesWhen price travels far away from a proposed location and eventually returns, the relevant timeframe's trend may have changed. A setup should be rechecked at execution rather than assuming its old trend assessment stays valid forever.

The tutor's Hindustan Petroleum discussion contrasts a daily uptrend with a weekly downtrend. His reasoning rests on the different 50-candle windows, not contradictory price data.

06 / Key definitions

Golden crossover vs death crossover

1:44:40–1:48:45

The tutor puts EMA 20 and EMA 50 on the chart. The smaller-period EMA reacts more quickly; it is the direction in which the faster line crosses the slower line that defines the crossover.

Golden crossover

EMA 20 crosses above EMA 50

Faster EMA moves from below to above the slower EMA. Conventional followers may call this bullish, but the tutor does not say to buy automatically.

Death crossover

EMA 20 crosses below EMA 50

Faster EMA moves from above to below the slower EMA. Conventional followers may call this bearish, but the tutor does not say to sell automatically.

Why the faster average crosses

When recent buying pressure lifts price strongly, the 20-period EMA tends to turn before the 50-period EMA. With enough movement, the 20-period line crosses above it. A sharp decline can reverse the relationship. The price move came first.

07 / Rejected conventional shortcut

Why “buy golden, sell death” is insufficient

1:48:45–1:58:19

One of the lesson's most emphatic warnings: a crossover signals what price has already done. By the time the lines cross, the price may be close to a powerful opposing zone or already extended.

The conventional rule the tutor critiques

Conventional approachBuy when EMA 20 crosses above EMA 50. Hold until a death crossover appears.
What can go wrongThe buy happens after a price rally. Price reverses near supply. The delayed death crossover may produce an exit much lower.

Likewise, a trader selling on a death crossover may be shorting just before price reacts from a strong demand zone. Waiting for an opposite crossover to exit can erase unrealized profit or produce a loss.

The tutor's positionDo not use crossovers alone for entry and do not use their opposite crossover as the trailing-stop mechanism. His class uses a separate trailing-stop framework, which is not specified here.

What this does—and does not—imply

The tutor acknowledges that a one-directional strong rally or decline may make a conventional crossover sequence profitable. He rejects relying on that possibility without reading demand/supply location. This is a criticism of a simplified method, not a claim that every moving-average strategy is always unprofitable.

08 / The actual correlation the class teaches

Match crossover direction to a pre-existing zone

1:58:19–2:01:45, 2:09–2:11

“Correlation” here is about agreement between the zone and the crossover—not the statistical correlation coefficient. The setup is identified first. A matching crossover near that zone can supply one additional trade-quality point.

Demand-side confirmation

Demand zone + golden crossover

If a valid demand execution setup already exists and the EMA 20 crosses above EMA 50 near the relevant zone, the tutor considers it a bonus trade-score point.

Supply-side confirmation

Supply zone + death crossover

If a valid supply execution setup already exists and EMA 20 crosses below EMA 50 near that zone, this can likewise add one supporting point.

The location condition is important

The tutor does not award this point if a crossover occurs far away. He asks for proximity to the zone's proximal/distal boundaries and roughly the leg-in / base / leg-out region. A little variation is acceptable; a far-off crossover is not a strong match.

He uses a classroom score example: a previously strong 7/7 setup may be described as 8/8 after adding a matching crossover. This is his scoring illustration, not a measured success probability.

The most important instruction · ~1:59:28–2:00:37Do not search for a crossover first and then invent a trade around it. Find the usual quality setup (location, execution, closing) and check the crossover afterward. If no matching crossover appears, the original valid setup can still be tradable under the existing rules.
09 / Entry, stop and timeframe

The tutor treats moving averages as a supporting overlay, never permission to compromise on base-level trade quality.

10 / Market regime filter

Trending markets only—not sideways chop

2:11:48–2:12:43

The tutor explicitly warns that EMA 20 and EMA 50 can intersect repeatedly in a sideways market. A “golden” or “death” label becomes a poor reason to act when the market keeps reversing.

Diagram 5 · A whipsaw-prone sideways sequence Illustrative
EMA 20EMA 50Price path

A hypothetical oscillating series shows the principle of repeated crossings; it is not historical evidence or backtesting.

In a trending market

Moving averages may stay directionally separated for longer. The tutor permits checking the matching crossover as supportive evidence—still only after a valid zone-based setup.

In a sideways market

EMA lines can cross above and below one another many times without a sustained directional move. The tutor says not to use crossovers in this environment.

12 / Cases & clarifications

Classroom examples worth remembering

Selected recoverable passages
Case A · Hindustan Petroleum: daily uptrend, weekly downtrend

The instructor observes that the same price can be classified as trending upward on the daily chart while downward on the weekly chart. He explains why: the daily SMA uses 50 daily bars whereas the weekly SMA uses 50 weekly bars. A sharp daily reversal will not immediately force a weekly SMA-50 trend change.

Remember: lower-timeframe movement and higher-timeframe trend are not interchangeable.

Source: ~1:26:55–1:30:31.

Case B · Ashok Leyland: comparing average types

The instructor opens a stock chart to compare a 50 SMA and a 50 EMA. The EMA line sits closer to and turns with price more quickly than the SMA. The subsequent weighted-average example is for learning how responsiveness differs, not a new entry trigger.

Remember: fast response is a property of the indicator, not independent evidence that the next candle will move a certain way.

Source: ~1:37:56–1:43:54.

Case C · A demand zone and a near-zone golden crossover

The tutor points to an existing rally–base–rally demand structure and adds a matching golden crossover around the execution. The setup was already selected; this agreement grants one extra trade-score point.

Remember: the crossover should be near the setup's proximal/distal and leg region. It is not valid to “find crossover, then invent zone.”

Source: ~1:58:25–2:01:45.

Case D · A tempting crossover near opposing higher-timeframe supply/demand

The conventional approach of buying the golden crossover can fail if price has already reached strong supply, while selling the death crossover can fail into strong demand. The instructor walks through the consequences of taking such entries and then waiting for an opposite crossover to exit.

Remember: evaluate nearby opposing locations before interpreting an indicator-based signal.

Source: ~1:49–1:58.

Case E · Alert and reason log for an untriggered trade

The class demonstrates setting an alert a little ahead of a planned zone and writing a detailed rationale such as MIT setup, weekly support and the quality of the origin or closing. The reason log helps you re-evaluate the chart when the price eventually returns rather than remembering only a number.

Remember: an alert is a reminder to review, not proof the trade remains valid.

13 / Avoidable mistakes

Seven ways to misread this lesson

Derived from explicit tutor warnings
MistakeCorrection
Buying every golden crossoverFirst confirm a quality demand setup, not merely a crossed pair of EMAs.
Selling every death crossoverCheck for strong demand below and whether the signal has come after the decline.
Using the opposite crossover to trail exitsFollow the class's separate stop/trailing method rather than a delayed crossover.
Hunting for a zone after finding a crossoverFind the setup through top-down location and execution before checking averages.
Counting a distant crossover as confirmationOnly count near-zone agreement in the leg-in/base/leg-out area.
Demanding matching crossovers on every timeframePrioritize execution timeframe; intermediate may also count.
Using crossovers in sideways conditionsRecognize repeated intersections/whipsaw risk and avoid this filter there.
Final caution on certaintyThe tutor's trade score is a qualitative classroom score. “8 out of 8” is not an 8/8 win guarantee, not a statistical win rate, and not permission to ignore loss limits.
14 / Quick revision

What to retain after one reading

The lesson in 60 seconds

  1. A 50 SMA averages the last 50 candle closes on its own timeframe and changes as the window rolls.
  2. It can rise after a down day or fall after an up day because what is removed matters.
  3. A shorter/faster MA reacts sooner; EMA is faster than the comparable SMA in the tutor's discussion.
  4. Golden: EMA 20 crosses upward through EMA 50. Death: EMA 20 crosses downward.
  5. Both are lagging; do not trade or trail solely because a crossover appears.
  6. At a demand execution, a near-zone golden crossover can add a point. At a supply execution, a near-zone death crossover can add a point.
  7. Check the execution timeframe, optionally intermediate. Avoid sideways crossover noise.
  8. Never weaken location, execution, closing or risk discipline for a moving-average bonus.
